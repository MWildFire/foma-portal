## Big idea

Transformation includes the way texts rework other texts and the way reading can change an interpretation or a reader. Changes within a story are another useful subject of analysis, rather than the whole concept.

## Explanation

The IB guide connects transformation with intertextuality: adaptation, appropriation and reinterpretation can give familiar material a different purpose. Ask what is retained, what changes and how the change matters. A new medium or a different reader can also alter how a work is understood. This lesson then practises one narrower application: analysing a change within a narrative.

Transformation is a key concept because it connects what happens in a text with how the text is written. A change may happen in a character, a relationship, a society or a self. It is not the same as the plot: the plot says what happened, while transformation asks what is different at the end and how the text makes the reader feel that difference.

<Callout kind="define">Transformation: reworking between texts and changes arising through reading; a represented change within a story can also be explored when the question calls for it.</Callout>

A text usually shows transformation at a turning point. An <Term id="epiphany"/> is a sudden moment of understanding, often about the self. A <Term id="reversal"/> is a sudden change of fortune or direction, so that what the reader expects does not happen. In a sonnet, a <Term id="volta"/> is the turn of thought, where the argument or the tone changes; in the Italian form it usually comes at line 9. A <Term id="climax"/> is the point of greatest tension, and the <Term id="denouement"/> shows what the change has made of the characters afterwards.

Compare the start and the end. Look at the voice (does the narrator speak with more or less certainty?), the tone (is the mood warmer, colder or angrier?) and the imagery (do the images move from closed spaces to open ones, or from light to dark?). Structure can show change too: a text may move from a before to an after, with a turn between them.

Look also at <Term id="pace"/>, which is often the clearest sign of change. Short sentences can produce rapid sequence or marked pauses; a long coordinated sentence can flow quickly or accumulate slowly. Explain the actual syntax and context instead of assigning speed from length alone.

Finally, transformation includes the reader. A text can change our view: we begin by sympathising with a narrator and end by seeing the narrator's limits. When you write about transformation, name the change, locate it in the text, show the technique that marks it, and explain its effect on the reader. Plot knowledge can contribute to understanding, but retelling alone does not develop analysis. Naming a technique is not a separate mark prerequisite.

## Worked example

Both paragraphs below are original. The first comes before a change; the second comes after it. For this invented example, stipulate that the later “I” is Ana herself; the pronoun alone would not establish that identity.

Before: "For eleven years the kitchen door stayed shut. Ana set the table for three, washed three cups and said nothing when her brother's chair stayed empty. She had learned that silence was the cheapest way to keep the peace."

After: "Then, on a wet Thursday, the door swung open on its own. I stood in the draught for a long time, and the house, which had been holding its breath, let it out all at once: the clock, the radiator, the dog scratching at the hall floor. I could not stop smiling, and I did not want to."

<Steps>
<Step>**Name the change.** The narration moves from a distant third person, "Ana", to a direct first person, "I". The change is in a self and in a home: from silence and control to release.</Step>
<Step>**Find the first technique: imagery.** The shut door, the silence and the empty chair make a closed world. In the second paragraph the same door "swung open on its own", and the house "let it out". The image of enclosure becomes an image of release.</Step>
<Step>**Find the second technique: voice.** The first paragraph gives access to Ana's habitual thought in third person, with a list of routines and a general rule ("She had learned that..."). The second switches to "I" and runs on through a long sentence with a list after a colon. The accumulated sounds can make the release feel expansive; the final admission can suggest delight or defiance. This effect depends on the pattern and context, not simply on a longer sentence.</Step>
<Step>**Explain the effect.** The reader sees the change in the form before the narrator names it. The closed sentences give way to open ones, so the release is felt as well as described.</Step>
</Steps>

### A second application: reworking a text

Original invented line: “The gatekeeper gave the traveller a key, and the village welcomed her.” Reworking: “They gave her a key that opened no door, then called her one of their own.” The key and welcome are retained, but the useless key makes belonging questionable. This is transformation through rewriting, even if neither version contains a character who changes by its ending. A strong answer compares the retained image and the altered implication; it does not call any change of wording automatically better.

## Visual

| What changes | How the text shows it | The technique |
|---|---|---|
| A character's self-knowledge | Understanding arrives in one sudden moment | Epiphany at the turning point |
| A relationship | The roles reverse: the one who kept silent speaks first | Reversal |
| A society or a place | The enclosed setting opens: doors, windows, rooms | Imagery moves from enclosure to openness |
| The narrator's voice | "She" gives way to "I", and the sentences grow longer | Change of person and voice; pace |
| The reader's view | A late detail changes the meaning of what came before | Denouement, or a turn that reframes the story |

## How it is examined

Transformation is one of the key concepts, so it can shape an answer in both papers. In Paper 1 the guiding question may ask how a text presents change: name the change, locate it in the extract, and explain the technique and its effect. In Paper 2 the concept helps you compare: two works can change a character in opposite ways, and the so what is the difference in the reader's experience. Explain asks you to make the relationship clear; analyse examines how relevant choices work; evaluate adds a supported appraisal. Use evidence suited to the question; these are not interchangeable commands.

<Callout kind="exam">Support a claim about change with precise evidence and reasoning about how meaning is constructed; there is no automatic score for naming a device.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">The most common error is to describe what happens and call it transformation. A turning point is useful only when you show how the text marks it.</Callout>

- Retelling events ("then she left, then he came back") instead of naming the change in a character, a relationship or the reader.
- Saying that the text "changes" without locating the change: give the line, the paragraph or the stanza where the turn happens.
- Naming a technique such as imagery without saying what has changed between the start and the end.
- Calling every event a turning point. A turning point changes the direction of the text, and most scenes do not.

## Summary

- Transformation is a change in a character, a relationship, a society or a self, and in the reader's view.
- Find the turning point and name how the text marks it: an epiphany, a reversal, a volta or a climax.
- Compare the start and the end through voice, tone, imagery and structure.
- Pace and sentence form often show a change before the characters name it.
- Name the change, locate it, show the technique and explain the effect on the reader.
- Plot knowledge can contribute to understanding, but retelling alone does not develop analysis. Naming a technique is not a separate mark prerequisite.
