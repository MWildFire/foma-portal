## Big idea

Into the Wild (1996) by Jon Krakauer investigates Chris McCandless, who graduated in 1990, left his family, took a new name and died in the Alaskan wilderness in 1992. For Paper 2 the book shows how a non-fiction author builds an argument: through his own position as narrator, through the documents in which the subject speaks, and through a parallel story from his own life. Its open question about the cause of death makes it a strong text for the tension between a chosen identity and the expectations that surround it.

## Explanation

Jon Krakauer published Into the Wild in 1996. The book grew out of a magazine article he wrote about Chris McCandless in 1993. It is non-fiction, but it reads as an investigation: Krakauer narrates throughout, interviews people who met McCandless on the road, and appears in the story himself, as the <Term id="narrator"/> who decides what the reader learns and when.

<Callout kind="define">A narrator is the voice that tells the story and controls what the reader learns, and when.</Callout>

The book runs two plots side by side: McCandless's journey and the author's investigation of it. McCandless graduated in 1990, cut ties with his family, took a new name and died in the Alaskan wilderness in 1992. His body was found in an abandoned bus in September of that year. The reader learns about him through the people who saw him and through his own letters, postcards and diary. Two useful witnesses to study are Wayne Westerberg, a grain-elevator operator in South Dakota who employed him, and Ron Franz, an older man in California who befriended him. The epilogue returns to McCandless’s parents, Walt and Billie.

**Identity.** Identity is the book's central <Term id="theme"/>. McCandless introduces himself to strangers as Alex and takes the name Alexander Supertramp, so the book sets the identity he chose against the one his family knew. Chapter 1 follows a stranger who gives him a lift. In chapters 6 and 7 his letter to Franz urges an older man to leave his routine for the road. Identity matters to the author too: in chapter 15 Krakauer recalls that his father wanted him to pursue a medical career, and that he did not follow that path.

**Power.** Family expectations and natural danger are important lenses, not the book’s only concerns. In chapter 12, Carine recalls the 1986 telescope gift with a tearful speech of gratitude. Later in that chapter, the concealed overlap between Walt’s two family relationships complicates this earlier warmth. In chapters 14–15, Krakauer’s own dangerous attempts on the Devils Thumb expose limits to willpower; he eventually reaches the summit by a different route. A successful climb does not erase his retrospective criticism of his youthful judgement.

**Freedom and Control.** McCandless seeks independence from family expectations, but departure does not neatly resolve belonging. Chapter 18 discusses his lack of a detailed map and nearby cabins, then explains that their supplies had been vandalised: finding a cabin is not a demonstrated guaranteed rescue. In the epilogue, the parents’ visit and the note urging contact with parents return attention to relationships and loss.

**Truth and Deception.** Separate evidence, interpretation and publication history. Chapter 18’s early account proposes a wild-potato-seed alkaloid hypothesis; later investigations revised the toxic-agent explanation. Do not put all later findings into the original chapter or present a proposed mechanism as settled proof. Analyse the actual wording in your edition. The concealed family history in chapter 12 provides another, distinct question about trust.

**Author's decisions.** The author's choices are visible in four places. He places his own climb in chapters 14 and 15, a <Term id="flashback"/> to his youth that runs beside McCandless's journey, so the two stories form a <Term id="juxtaposition"/> of two lives. He lets McCandless speak through letters and postcards in chapters 6 and 7. The bus works as a <Term id="symbol"/>: it is where the body was found, and in the epilogue the parents turn it into a memorial and a warning. It is also a recurring <Term id="motif"/>, present at the discovery and again at the end. The epilogue puts Krakauer beside Walt and Billie on the return visit, so the last scene belongs to the family rather than to McCandless.

<Callout kind="exam">An answer scores better when it treats the author's choices as evidence, not only as events of the plot.</Callout>

## Worked example

**Claim:** Krakauer presents the father–son relationship as more complex than a simple rejection of family. **Evidence:** chapter 12 juxtaposes Carine’s memory of Chris’s emotional gratitude at the 1986 telescope presentation with his later discovery of concealed family relationships. Chapter 15 lets Krakauer reflect on his own conflict with paternal expectations. **Analysis:** the gift is not evidence of lovelessness; the changing relationship and attributed memories make trust and interpretation central. **Comparison:** in *Much Ado*, the public rejection of Hero in 4.1 similarly asks whose testimony a community accepts, though a staged accusation differs from a reconstructed family history.

## How it is examined

Each of the five criteria is worth 5 marks, 25 in total.

- Criterion A (understanding of the prompt and both works): the top band needs identity and expectation defined precisely and traced through both works, including the book's two plots and the narrator's position.
- Criterion B1 (authorial choices and their effects): analyse relevant choices and explain their effects precisely, for example the author's climb in chapters 14 and 15 and the letters and postcards in chapters 6 and 7. A list of terms alone does not demonstrate analysis; no fixed number of techniques determines the band.
- Criterion B2 (comparison, sustained and linked to the prompt): each point sets this book beside the second work on identity and expectation, with the link stated.
- Criterion C (focus and organisation): a precise thesis on how the book shows identity against expectation, in a logical order, ending with a conclusion that weighs the cost of the choice.
- Criterion D (language): precise vocabulary, such as narrator, flashback, hypothesis and parallel, with references built into the sentences.

Into the Wild is non-fiction. In Paper 2 it may be paired with a text in another form, such as a play or a poem. Then the how of the non-fiction lies in the author's choices (the documents he includes, his position as narrator, the order of the chapters), and the other text must be analysed in its own form rather than as if it were a novel.

## Common mistakes

- Retelling McCandless's journey in order, as if the book were a plain travel story, instead of analysing the two plots and the author's investigation.
- Presenting the cause of death as settled. The text proposes a mechanism; distinguish its version from later investigations.
- Treating the narrator as invisible: forgetting that Krakauer narrates, describes his own climb and appears in the epilogue.
- Quoting letters and postcards without a chapter location, or without saying what the reader learns from McCandless's own voice.
- Using "Chris" and "Alex" as if they were two people, without a note that they refer to the same man.
