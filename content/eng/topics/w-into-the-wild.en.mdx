## Big idea

Into the Wild (1996) by Jon Krakauer investigates Chris McCandless, who graduated in 1990, left his family, took a new name and died in the Alaskan wilderness in 1992. For Paper 2 the book shows how a non-fiction author builds an argument: through his own position as narrator, through the documents in which the subject speaks, and through a parallel story from his own life. Its open question about the cause of death makes it a strong text for the tension between a chosen identity and the expectations that surround it.

## Explanation

Jon Krakauer published Into the Wild in 1996. The book grew out of a magazine article he wrote about Chris McCandless in 1993. It is non-fiction, but it reads as an investigation: Krakauer narrates throughout, interviews people who met McCandless on the road, and appears in the story himself, as the <Term id="narrator"/> who decides what the reader learns and when.

<Callout kind="define">A narrator is the voice that tells the story and controls what the reader learns, and when.</Callout>

The book runs two plots side by side: McCandless's journey and the author's investigation of it. McCandless graduated in 1990, cut ties with his family, took a new name and died in the Alaskan wilderness in 1992. His body was found in an abandoned bus in September of that year. The reader learns about him through the people who saw him and through his own letters, postcards and diary. Two witnesses matter most: Wayne Westerberg, a grain-elevator operator in South Dakota who employed him, and Ron Franz, an older man in California who befriended him. McCandless's parents, Walt and Billie, appear at the end.

**Identity.** Identity is the book's central <Term id="theme"/>. McCandless introduces himself to strangers as Alex and takes the name Alexander Supertramp, so the book sets the identity he chose against the one his family knew. Chapter 1 follows a stranger who gives him a lift. In chapters 6 and 7 his letter to Franz urges an older man to leave his routine for the road. Identity matters to the author too: in chapter 15 Krakauer recalls that his father wanted him to pursue a medical career, and that he did not follow that path.

**Power.** Family expectations shape the book, and nature has its own power. Storms and ice give the book its strongest <Term id="imagery"/>. In chapter 12 McCandless gives his father an expensive telescope before he leaves, a gift that shows gratitude without the warmth the reader might expect. Later in the same chapter, during his travels, he learns that his father had kept an earlier family, and Krakauer links this discovery to McCandless's anger. In chapters 14 and 15 storms force Krakauer's own climb of the Devils Thumb to turn back, so the reader sees how little human will can do against the weather.

**Freedom and Control.** McCandless wants to escape the conventional life his family represents, and the book shows what that freedom costs. In chapter 18 Krakauer argues that, without a map, McCandless could not know that cabins stood nearby, so refusing guidance becomes a danger. The epilogue closes the story with a different kind of control: Walt and Billie leave a note at the bus urging runaways to contact their families.

**Truth and Deception.** The official cause of death was starvation, but Krakauer offers another explanation. He first linked the death to wild sweet pea seeds, and later to mold on wild potato seeds. Lab tests found no toxins in the first samples, so in chapter 18 the author revises his theory. The book's account of the death is therefore a hypothesis, not a settled fact. The father's secret in chapter 12 is another kind of deception, and the book presents it as a source of McCandless's anger.

**Author's decisions.** The author's choices are visible in four places. He places his own climb in chapters 14 and 15, a <Term id="flashback"/> to his youth that runs beside McCandless's journey, so the two stories form a <Term id="juxtaposition"/> of two lives. He lets McCandless speak through letters and postcards in chapters 6 and 7. The bus works as a <Term id="symbol"/>: it is where the body was found, and in the epilogue the parents turn it into a memorial and a warning. It is also a recurring <Term id="motif"/>, present at the discovery and again at the end. The epilogue puts Krakauer beside Walt and Billie on the return visit, so the last scene belongs to the family rather than to McCandless.

<Callout kind="exam">An answer scores better when it treats the author's choices as evidence, not only as events of the plot.</Callout>

## Worked example

**Claim:** in Into the Wild, Krakauer shows that McCandless's chosen identity pulls against the expectations of his family, and he shows the same pull in his own life. **Evidence:** in chapter 12, McCandless gives his father an expensive telescope before he leaves, and during his travels he learns that his father had kept an earlier family; he cannot forgive him. In chapter 15, Krakauer recalls that his father wanted him to pursue a medical career, and that he did not follow that path. **Analysis:** the two chapters place the pressure of parental expectation in two generations, so the reader sees McCandless's flight as one answer to a common problem rather than a private eccentricity. The gift in chapter 12 shows expectations met with objects instead of affection, while the author's choice not to follow his father's path in chapter 15 shows the same struggle from the inside. **Link:** in Much Ado About Nothing, Hero is publicly denounced as unchaste at her own wedding (Act 4, scene 1), and her standing in the community depends on what the men around her decide to believe, so again a community's judgement decides who a character is allowed to be.

This paragraph earns B2 because the comparison is explicit: the claim names the shared tension, and the final sentence links it to the second work instead of leaving the reader to make the connection. A full B2 mark needs the same explicit link in every point.

## How it is examined

Each of the five criteria is worth 5 marks, 25 in total.

- Criterion A (understanding of the prompt and both works): the top band needs identity and expectation defined precisely and traced through both works, including the book's two plots and the narrator's position.
- Criterion B1 (authorial choices and their effects): name two or more choices and explain each one precisely, for example the author's climb in chapters 14 and 15 and the letters and postcards in chapters 6 and 7. Naming choices with general effects reaches only the middle band.
- Criterion B2 (comparison, sustained and linked to the prompt): each point sets this book beside the second work on identity and expectation, with the link stated.
- Criterion C (focus and organisation): a precise thesis on how the book shows identity against expectation, in a logical order, ending with a conclusion that weighs the cost of the choice.
- Criterion D (language): precise vocabulary, such as narrator, flashback, hypothesis and parallel, with references built into the sentences.

Into the Wild is non-fiction. In Paper 2 it may be paired with a text in another form, such as a play or a poem. Then the how of the non-fiction lies in the author's choices (the documents he includes, his position as narrator, the order of the chapters), and the other text must be analysed in its own form rather than as if it were a novel.

## Common mistakes

- Retelling McCandless's journey in order, as if the book were a plain travel story, instead of analysing the two plots and the author's investigation.
- Presenting the cause of death as settled. The book gives a hypothesis, and the author revises it after lab tests found no toxins.
- Treating the narrator as invisible: forgetting that Krakauer narrates, describes his own climb and appears in the epilogue.
- Quoting letters and postcards without a chapter location, or without saying what the reader learns from McCandless's own voice.
- Using "Chris" and "Alex" as if they were two people, without a note that they refer to the same man.
