## Big idea

Communication in a text is a chain: someone says something to someone, through a medium, and some things are left unsaid. The reader's work is to hear both the words and what is withheld.

## Explanation

The course concept includes how a work communicates with readers through style, structure and assumed knowledge, and how readers interpret it. Exchanges within the story are one way to investigate that relationship: ask who speaks to whom and through what medium. A text can carry speech between characters, a letter from one person to another, a dialogue that an audience watches, a monologue spoken alone, or an address to a listener who cannot answer. Each medium sets its own rules. A letter separates composition from reception; that can make clarification harder, but it does not require more disclosure or more concealment. On stage, the audience hears what characters choose to say to each other, and sometimes what they say only to the audience.

Start with what is withheld. The most important exchanges in literature often leave out the reason for a decision, the answer to a question or the feeling a character refuses to name. Evasion is the usual form of this: a character answers a different question from the one that was asked. Misunderstanding adds to it. When two people hear different things in the same words, the gap between them is where the writer creates tension, comedy or sorrow. The meaning under the words is <Term id="subtext"/>, and the reader infers it from tone, repetition, silence and context.

<Callout kind="define">Subtext is an implied layer beneath explicit words or actions; it need not be consciously intended by a character.</Callout>

Drama shows most clearly who hears what. A <Term id="soliloquy"/> presents a character’s thoughts as self-directed speech, often while alone; check who is present and what they can conventionally hear. An <Term id="aside"/> is treated as unheard by some other characters and may address the audience or a particular character. Either can create <Term id="dramatic-irony"/> when it gives the audience relevant knowledge another character lacks; neither automatically creates it in every use.

<Term id="direct-address"/> works in prose and drama alike. A narrator who says "you" invites the reader in; a character who speaks to the audience makes the audience a witness. Always ask who the text assumes its <Term id="audience"/> is, and what that audience already knows. The intended audience may differ from the people who actually read or watch the text.

A poem can speak to someone who cannot reply. An <Term id="apostrophe"/> is a speaker's address to an absent or dead person, or to an idea or an object. The address does not require an ordinary reply. Silence may matter, but a poem or play can also imagine an answer.

The writer also communicates with the reader directly. The text decides how much the reader is told, how quickly, and whether the reader shares a character's ignorance. A text that holds information back makes the reader do the work of communication: to infer, to guess and to judge.

## Worked example

Read this original exchange, written for this lesson. Two people share a kitchen after a long absence.

**Ana:** You've set two plates.

**Leo:** Force of habit.

**Ana:** Habit usually sets one.

**Leo:** Then it's a bad habit. I'll clear it away.

<Steps>
<Step>**Read the surface.** Ana notices two plates and Leo calls it a habit. On the surface this is a small argument about crockery.</Step>
<Step>**Find what is withheld.** Leo avoids explaining the second plate and offers to remove it. This can suggest discomfort, but the dialogue does not establish a death or permanent departure.</Step>
<Step>**Explain the subtext.** Ana’s "Habit usually sets one" challenges the explanation. Leo might have hoped Ana would stay to eat but be reluctant to admit it; another possible reading is an association with an absent person. More context is needed to choose between them. Ana’s question does not prove she already knows the reason.</Step>
<Step>**Name the effect.** The gap can create curiosity or unease because the literal explanation seems inadequate. A careful answer distinguishes that supported observation from an invented backstory.</Step>
</Steps>

## Visual

|---|---|---|---|
| A character | Another character | Spoken dialogue | The true motive, hidden under a polite question |
| A character | The audience alone | Soliloquy or aside | A decision the character has told no one |
| A narrator | The reader | Written narration | The narrator's bias, or the outcome the narrator already knows |
| A speaker | An absent person | Apostrophe in a poem | The reply that never comes |

## How it is examined

Communication appears in questions about dialogue, narrative voice and audience. Examiners reward an account of who speaks to whom, and through what medium, and an explanation of what is withheld and why. "Identify" and "State" ask you to name a device or a piece of subtext, such as an aside or an apostrophe. Explain and analyse call for reasoning supported by relevant details. A Paper 2 comparison might examine different media, but it can also compare two dialogues or two narrators; different media and quotations are not compulsory.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">The most common error is to quote a line of dialogue and never explain the subtext behind it.</Callout>

- Retelling what the characters say without saying what they withhold.
- Treating every soliloquy as proof of physical solitude, or every aside as audience-directed only.
- Treating the audience as one fixed group, rather than asking who the text assumes it is.
- Ignoring the medium: a letter, a spoken line and a stage direction each communicate differently.
- Writing "the characters are hiding something" without naming what is hidden and which evidence shows it.

## Summary

- Communication is a chain: a sender, a receiver, a medium and what is withheld.
- Subtext is implied meaning beneath explicit words or actions; find it in context, repetition, silence and evasion. A character need not create it deliberately.
- A soliloquy and an aside reveal what a character hides from others, and they change what the audience knows.
- Dramatic irony appears when the audience knows what a character does not.
- Direct address and apostrophe show who the text speaks to, including a listener who cannot answer.
- The writer communicates with the reader through what is shown and what is held back.
