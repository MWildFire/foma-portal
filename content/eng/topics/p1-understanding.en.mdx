## Big idea

Criterion A asks whether you understand the extract: what is happening, who is speaking to whom, and what attitude the words carry. Interpretation goes further than paraphrase, because it says what the words mean in this situation.

## Explanation

Before you analyse anything, you must know the situation. Ask four questions: who is speaking, to whom, where, and when? In a poem the speaker is often a <Term id="lyric-persona"/>, a voice the poet invents, so do not assume that the speaker is the poet. In a story the narrator may be unreliable, and in a play the audience sees only what the characters say and do. Note what has happened just before the extract, because that explains much of its mood.

Next, separate the literal meaning from the implied one. The <Term id="denotation"/> of a phrase is its dictionary sense: what it says. Its <Term id="connotation"/> is what it suggests, the associations the reader brings to it. Much of the meaning in an extract is <Term id="subtext"/>, a feeling or intention that is never stated outright.

<Callout kind="define">Tone is the attitude of the speaker to the subject or the listener, shown through word choice and rhythm. Mood is the feeling the reader is left with.</Callout>

Name the <Term id="tone"/> with a quotation. "Angry" is weak evidence until you show the words that make it angry. Test your label by asking whether another careful reader could reasonably choose a different word. If so, choose a more precise word or say why the tone is mixed. Watch also for <Term id="verbal-irony"/>, where the words say one thing and the context means another. Irony is safe to claim only when the surrounding words support it.

Finally, find the turn. Most extracts change direction at some point: a new speaker enters, the tone shifts, or an expectation is broken. Locate the turn, say what changes, and connect it to the <Term id="theme"/> that the extract develops.

Paraphrase says what the lines say in simpler words. Interpretation says what the lines mean in the situation and why it matters. "The speaker is sad" is a claim; "the speaker's calm hides a fear that the room is not yet ready" is an interpretation that you can prove from the text.

## Worked example

<Question id="p1-dickinson-fly"/>

Start with the situation, then build the reading in five steps.

<Steps>
<Step>**Who is present.** The words "The eyes beside had wrung them dry," show the watchers at the bedside. Their eyes have wept until there are no tears left, so the room has been grieving for some time. The speaker is the person at the centre of this room, the one who is dying.</Step>
<Step>**What the room is waiting for.** The next lines say that "breaths were gathering sure / For that last onset, when the king / Be witnessed in his power." The breaths are gathering, so the watchers draw themselves together. The "last onset" is the final attack of death, and one common reading takes "the king" as death itself. The room waits for death to arrive in its full power.</Step>
<Step>**Literal and implied meaning.** Literally, the eyes are dry because they have wept. The <Term id="connotation"/> of "wrung" is harder: wringing is squeezing cloth, so the grief has been twisted out of the people. The <Term id="subtext"/> is that the watchers have run out of tears and are now only waiting.</Step>
<Step>**Tone.** The <Term id="tone"/> is solemn and expectant. "Sure" suggests firmness, and "onset" and "power" are formal words, so the register is grave. The reader feels that a formal, public moment is about to begin.</Step>
<Step>**The turn.** The formal wait is broken by a small interruption, "There interposed a fly". The turn changes the mood from solemn expectation to something smaller and stranger. Name the turn and say what it changes, because the rest of the poem depends on it.</Step>
</Steps>

Put the five steps together and the extract has a clear situation: a dying speaker, a room of grieving watchers, and a formal moment that is about to be interrupted. The <Term id="mood"/> moves from grief to suspense and then to a sudden, almost absurd smallness.

## How it is examined

<Callout kind="exam">Criterion A is worth up to 5 marks. Examiners reward an accurate account of the situation, a correct tone supported by a quotation, and at least one interpretation that goes beyond what the words literally say.</Callout>

Each command term asks for a different depth. Identify asks for a name with no development. Describe asks for the features of the situation or the tone. Explain asks for the reason: why the words create that tone. Suggest asks for a possible interpretation that the text supports, so use cautious language such as "the words suggest" and end with a quotation.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">The most common loss of criterion A marks is a line-by-line paraphrase that never names the speaker, the situation or the tone.</Callout>

- Paraphrasing each line in simpler words without saying what the situation is.
- Treating the poet as the speaker, so that every "I" is taken as biography.
- Naming a tone such as "sad" without a quotation that proves it.
- Stopping at the literal meaning and missing the connotation or subtext.
- Saying that there is no turn, or describing a turn without saying what changes.

## Summary

- Start with the situation: who speaks, to whom, where and when.
- Separate the denotation of a phrase from its connotation and subtext.
- Name the tone precisely and prove it with one short quotation.
- Interpretation says what the words mean in this situation; paraphrase only says what they say.
- Find the turn in the extract and say what changes at that point.
- Use irony only when the context supports it.
