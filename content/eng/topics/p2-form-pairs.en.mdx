## Big idea

Two forms give you comparison points before you have chosen a single quotation. A poem and a novel differ in length, time and voice; a play and a story differ in who speaks and who shows. Plan around the differences the forms make, then compare what each writer does with them.

## Explanation

Each form offers levers: choices that the form makes easy and that another form makes hard. A poem can hold a moment in a few lines. A novel can carry a life across years. A play must show its meaning through voices on a stage. A speech must persuade its audience directly. When you put the levers of two works side by side, the comparison axes are already there.

Before you choose a pair, list the levers of each form. The lists below are a starting point, not a checklist: use only the levers the prompt needs.

- **Poetry.** Line and stanza breaks, rhythm and rhyme, a lyric voice that addresses someone or no one, compressed time, and <Term id="imagery"/>.
- **Prose fiction.** The <Term id="narrator"/> and <Term id="focalisation"/>, time across years (<Term id="flashback"/>, ellipsis), <Term id="direct-characterisation"/> and <Term id="indirect-characterisation"/>, and the shape of chapters or scenes.
- **Drama.** Dialogue, <Term id="subtext"/>, <Term id="stage-directions"/>, <Term id="mise-en-scene"/> and silence on stage, and an audience that can know more than any character (<Term id="dramatic-irony"/>).
- **Non-fiction and speeches.** Persuasion through <Term id="ethos"/>, <Term id="pathos"/> and <Term id="logos"/>, <Term id="direct-address"/>, a clear <Term id="purpose"/> for an <Term id="audience"/>, and the structure of the argument.

A pair works when the levers differ. Two first-person narrators give you little to compare. A first-person narrator set against a play, in which the audience knows more than any character, gives you two different ways of controlling what the reader or viewer knows.

The thesis for any pair can follow one skeleton: *both works use [a shared device] to [a shared concept]; the first does it through [its lever], the second through [its lever], so [the effect], and [a degree]*. Fill the brackets with a technique, not a plot event.

With two works in the same form, such as two novels or two poems, the levers are the same and the choices are not. Choose one device that both works use, and give two different effects for it.

## Worked example

Invented pair, for the method only. The prompt: *Compare how a poem and a play show a person hiding behind a public name. To what extent does the audience's knowledge make the hiding more painful?*

<Steps>
<Step>**Levers.** The poem has a voice that speaks to one listener in a few lines, so the hiding is intimate. The play has a character who must speak to other characters in front of an audience, which can see the gap between the public name and the person.</Step>
<Step>**Axis 1: voice.** A lyric voice confides; a character performs. Set the direct address of the poem against the <Term id="aside"/> or the <Term id="soliloquy"/> of the play, and decide which one exposes the person more.</Step>
<Step>**Axis 2: knowledge.** In the play the audience can know more than the character: that is <Term id="dramatic-irony"/>. In the poem, the reader is let into the secret from the start. Decide which of the two makes the hiding more painful, and to what extent.</Step>
<Step>**Axis 3: ending.** The poem closes on a private agreement between two people who hide. The play closes with the public name still in use. The first consoles, the second condemns, so the audience's knowledge makes the cost of hiding larger.</Step>
</Steps>

A thesis for this plan: *In the poem and the play, a public name becomes a shield. The poem turns the shield into a shared secret, whereas the play makes the audience witness its cost, so the hiding hurts more to the extent that the audience knows more than the character.*

## Visual

| Pair | What the forms give you | Usual trap |
|---|---|---|
| Poetry and prose | a moment against a life; a lyric voice against a narrator | comparing length instead of what the length allows |
| Prose and drama | a narrator who explains against characters who must show; the reader's inner view against the audience's outer view | treating stage directions as if they were narration |
| Poetry and drama | one voice addressing someone against several voices in a scene | calling a dramatic monologue a lyric and ignoring the audience |
| Prose and non-fiction | a story that implies an argument against a text that states it | summarising the argument instead of naming the technique |
| Drama and non-fiction | a speech inside a play against a speech made to persuade | describing what the speech says, not how it is performed |
| Poetry and non-fiction | imagery and sound against direct persuasion | ignoring the rhetoric in the poem |
| Two works in one form | the same tools, used with different choices | listing two different plots |

## How it is examined

Criterion B (0–10) rewards a comparison of choices. A strong plan uses the levers as its points: each point then compares one technique in both works, and the form becomes evidence for the comparison. Criterion C rewards a thesis about effect. A thesis about the forms, such as "poems are short and plays are performed", is not a thesis about effect, so it earns little under criterion C.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a form in general ("a poem is short") instead of how this poem uses its shortness.
- Treating a convention of the form as the writer's choice. A play has stage directions; what the writer does with them is the choice.
- Choosing a pair whose narrators work in the same way, so there is no contrast.
- Writing a thesis about the forms rather than the works.
- Forgetting that two works in one form share their tools, so the difference must lie in their choices.

## Summary

- List the levers of each form before you choose the pair.
- Use the levers as comparison axes: voice, time, audience, staging and persuasion.
- A pair needs levers that differ; two first-person narrators give you little to compare.
- Write the thesis as a skeleton: one shared device, two levers, one effect and one degree.
- Two works in one form share their tools, so the difference must lie in their choices.
