## Big idea

Literary forms suggest possible questions about time, voice, structure and audience. Check which features each actual work uses: genre alone does not establish its length, perspective or effect. Build the comparison from significant choices in the works.

## Explanation

Each form offers resources and conventions that writers can use, adapt or reject. A poem can hold a moment in a few lines. A novel can carry a life across years. Drama may use speech, action, silence, stage images and narrators; a speech may inform, commemorate or persuade. Possible comparison axes emerge when you examine how the actual works use these resources.

When considering a pair, note relevant formal possibilities. These lists suggest questions, not fixed genre properties; select only features the works actually use and the prompt makes significant.

- **Poetry.** Line and stanza breaks, rhythm and rhyme, a lyric voice that addresses someone or no one, compressed time, and <Term id="imagery"/>.
- **Prose fiction.** The <Term id="narrator"/> and <Term id="focalisation"/>, time across years (<Term id="flashback"/>, ellipsis), <Term id="direct-characterisation"/> and <Term id="indirect-characterisation"/>, and the shape of chapters or scenes.
- **Drama.** Dialogue, <Term id="subtext"/>, <Term id="stage-directions"/>, <Term id="mise-en-scene"/> and silence on stage, and an audience that can know more than any character (<Term id="dramatic-irony"/>).
- **Non-fiction and speeches.** Persuasion through <Term id="ethos"/>, <Term id="pathos"/> and <Term id="logos"/>, <Term id="direct-address"/>, a clear <Term id="purpose"/> for an <Term id="audience"/>, and the structure of the argument.

A pair works when the question and the actual works support a developed comparison. Two first-person narrators can offer rich similarities or differences in reliability, hindsight, diction or information. A first-person narrator set against a play, in which the audience knows more than any character, gives you two different ways of controlling what the reader or viewer knows.

One optional planning aid is to name a significant formal choice in each work, compare how it shapes meaning and connect that comparison to the question. The choices need not be identical. State a degree when the prompt asks for one; no fixed thesis formula is required.

Works in the same broad form need not use the same devices or conventions. Compare relevant choices and meanings; a shared device and two opposite effects are useful possibilities, not requirements.

## Worked example

Invented pair, for the method only. Every voice, ending and effect below is a stipulated feature of these imaginary works, not a deduction from genre. The prompt: *Compare how a poem and a play show a person hiding behind a public name. To what extent does the audience's knowledge make the hiding more painful?*

<Steps>
<Step>**Formal possibilities in this invented pair.** The poem has a voice that speaks to one listener in a few lines, so the hiding is intimate. The play has a character who must speak to other characters in front of an audience, which can see the gap between the public name and the person.</Step>
<Step>**Axis 1: voice.** In this invented poem the voice confides; in this invented play the character performs a public role. Either form could handle disclosure differently. Set the direct address of the poem against the <Term id="aside"/> or the <Term id="soliloquy"/> of the play, and decide which one exposes the person more.</Step>
<Step>**Axis 2: knowledge.** In the play the audience can know more than the character: that is <Term id="dramatic-irony"/>. In the poem, the reader is let into the secret from the start. Decide which of the two makes the hiding more painful, and to what extent.</Step>
<Step>**Axis 3: ending.** The poem closes on a private agreement between two people who hide. The play closes with the public name still in use. The first consoles, the second condemns, so the audience's knowledge makes the cost of hiding larger.</Step>
</Steps>

A thesis for this plan: *In the poem and the play, a public name becomes a shield. The poem turns the shield into a shared secret, whereas the play makes the audience witness its cost, so the hiding hurts more to the extent that the audience knows more than the character.*

## Visual

| Pair | What the forms give you | Usual trap |
|---|---|---|
| Poetry and prose | duration, lineation and speaking voice; either work may cover a moment or many years | comparing length instead of what the length allows |
| Prose and drama | possible contrasts in narration, interior access, enacted dialogue and stage image; verify them in the actual works | treating stage directions as if they were narration |
| Poetry and drama | who speaks, who hears and what the audience knows; verify the actual voices | calling a dramatic monologue a lyric and ignoring the audience |
| Prose and non-fiction | narration, evidence and argument; either may be explicit or indirect | summarising the argument instead of naming the technique |
| Drama and non-fiction | the occasion, audience and purpose of speech or narration | describing what the speech says, not how it is performed |
| Poetry and non-fiction | imagery, sound and rhetoric in either work | ignoring the rhetoric in the poem |
| Two works in one form | shared or different conventions and their particular use | listing two different plots |

## How it is examined

Criterion B1 (0–5) rewards the analysis of each choice, and criterion B2 (0–5) rewards a comparison of choices. Use relevant formal choices to develop the comparative argument. Criterion C assesses focus, balance and coherent organisation, not a mandatory thesis formula about effects. General genre descriptions do not by themselves analyse the particular works.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a form in general ("a poem is short") instead of how this poem uses its shortness.
- Treating a convention of the form as the writer's choice. A play has stage directions; what the writer does with them is the choice.
- Assuming that similar narrators cannot be compared, or that genre alone proves a reader effect.
- Writing a thesis about the forms rather than the works.
- Assuming that two works of one form must use identical devices, or that a valid comparison must establish a difference.

## Summary

- Identify formal resources relevant to the actual works and question.
- Consider comparison axes such as voice, time, audience, staging and persuasion.
- Similar or different formal choices can support a comparison when their significance is analysed.
- Compare significant formal choices and explain how they answer the question; the planning aid is optional.
- Works in the same form may use shared or different conventions; analyse the choices, without imposing a difference.
