## Big idea

An authorial choice is a decision the writer made, and it earns marks only when you explain its effect on the reader and why that matters for the prompt. Name the choice, then the effect, then the link.

## Explanation

Criterion B1 asks about authorial choices: what the writer decided to do, and what that decision does. A choice is not a plot event. A character leaves home: that is an event. The writer shows the leaving through a closed door rather than a speech: that is a choice.

Every choice needs a chain of three links: the choice, its effect on the reader, and the link to the prompt. A choice without an effect is only a label. An effect without a link is only a comment. Write all three in one paragraph, and keep the chain short.

<Callout kind="define">An authorial choice is a decision a writer makes about narration, language, structure, setting or silence. Its effect is what that decision does to the reader.</Callout>

Choices fall into a few groups. Narrative distance and perspective decide how close the reader stands to a character. A <Term id="narrator"/> who tells events from a distance, or a <Term id="focalisation"/> through one character's eyes, changes what the reader trusts. When the narrator's voice merges with a character's thoughts through <Term id="free-indirect-discourse"/>, the reader hears the character without quotation marks. Dialogue decides who speaks, who stays silent and what is left unsaid. Structure decides the order of events, and a <Term id="foreshadowing"/> early in the text can change how the ending feels. <Term id="imagery"/> and <Term id="diction"/> decide what the reader notices, and <Term id="syntax"/> decides the rhythm of the sentences. <Term id="setting"/> decides where and when the events happen.

Time decides pace: one afternoon can fill a page, while a year passes in a line. Silence, such as a dash, a pause or a question with no answer, asks the reader to supply the meaning.

A narrator can also be an <Term id="unreliable-narrator"/>. When the narrator's judgement and the evidence in the text do not match, the reader must check the narrator. That gap is a choice too, and it can be explained with the same chain.

Choose two strong choices, not ten. Two chains, each with a quotation, an effect and a link, earn more than a list of five names. Where you can, take the choices from both works: a paragraph about one work only leaves the other unanalysed.

## Worked example

<Question id="p1-joyce-araby"/>

The guiding question asks how imagery shows the intensity of a feeling. Two choices in the passage do most of the work: the distance of the narrator from the boy, and the street he walks through.

<Steps>
<Step>**Choice 1: narrative distance.** The narrator is an adult who looks back. The adjective in "all my foolish blood" is his judgement from years afterwards, not the boy's view at the time. Effect: the reader feels the intensity from inside and sees its naivety from outside, so sympathy and a smile arrive together. Link: the prompt asks how the feeling is shown, and this double view shows that the feeling is real and young.</Step>
<Step>**Choice 2: setting.** The errand takes the boy through streets of "drunken men and bargaining women", among "the curses of labourers" and "the shrill litanies of shop-boys". Effect: the noise is hostile, so the feeling has to be private. Link: the feeling seems sacred because it survives the street. "These noises converged in a single sensation of life for me" shows the boy turning the noise into his own feeling.</Step>
</Steps>

The adult narrator calls the feeling "all my foolish blood", so the reader feels the intensity from inside and judges its naivety from outside. The streets are hostile to romance, with their "shrill litanies" and drunken men, so the feeling looks more fragile, and more sacred, for surviving them. Both choices answer the guiding question: the intensity is shown through the narrator's view of it and through the hostile place that tests it.

## Visual

| Choice | What to look for | Typical effect on the reader | Link to the prompt |
|---|---|---|---|
| Narrative distance | a judgement from afar: adjectives, comments | the reader feels and judges at once | how far the reader shares the feeling |
| Perspective | whose eyes the reader sees through | the reader knows only what one character sees | why one view is trusted |
| Dialogue | who speaks, who is silent, what is left out | power shifts, and the unsaid carries weight | who controls the relationship |
| Structure | the order of events, a flashback, the ending | expectations are set and then changed | how the ending changes the meaning |
| Time | a jump, a flashback, a slow scene | pace changes how heavy an event feels | what the prompt treats as important |
| Imagery and diction | a repeated image, one chosen word | the reader notices one kind of thing | which value the image carries |
| Setting | place, weather, time of day | the mood of the place reaches the character | how the place limits or frees the character |
| Silence | a dash, a pause, a question with no answer | the reader must fill the gap | what the reader is asked to supply |

## How it is examined

Criterion B1 (0–5) checks the authorial choices and their effects. The top band needs two or more choices from both works, each explained by its effect on the reader and linked to the prompt. The middle band names choices with general effects, such as "it creates tension". The low band names a feature with no effect, or retells the plot. Command terms such as "evaluate" ask for judgement: say how well a choice works, not only what it does.

## Common mistakes

- Listing features ("the writer uses imagery and dialogue") with no effect.
- Writing "it makes the reader feel sad" without saying what in the text does it.
- Calling a plot event an authorial choice. The event is not the choice; the way it is shown is.
- Analysing one work in depth and giving the other a single sentence.
- Ending the paragraph without the link to the prompt.

<Callout kind="mistake">A list of technique names is the lowest band, however accurate the names are.</Callout>

## Summary

- An authorial choice is a decision about narration, language, structure, setting, time or silence.
- Use the chain: the choice, its effect on the reader, and its link to the prompt.
- Support each choice with a quotation or a precise detail, not a general claim.
- Choose two strong choices from both works, not a list of five names.
- A plot event is not a choice, but the way a writer shows it can be.
- Silence counts: a dash or an unanswered question asks the reader to fill the gap.
