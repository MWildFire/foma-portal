## Big idea

Techniques cross literary forms, but their effects depend on context. Connect an accurate textual reference to a claim about how wording, structure or form creates meaning. A device name is a starting point, not a complete analysis.

## Explanation

A <Term id="metaphor"/> presents one thing through another; a <Term id="simile"/> makes a figurative comparison explicit, often with “like” or “as”. Those words are not a sufficient test: “she works as a doctor” names a literal role. <Term id="personification"/> attributes human qualities or actions to something non-human. Explain the actual relationship, not just its label.

A <Term id="symbol"/> carries meaning beyond its literal role when the text supports that connection. It need not recur: repetition can strengthen a reading but is not a definition of symbolism. Symbolism is the use or pattern of symbols. Allegory can sustain another level of meaning through characters and events; it need not make every detail a one-to-one code. Russian usage also includes individual allegorical images. <Term id="imagery"/> can engage sound, smell and touch as well as sight.

In <Term id="verbal-irony"/>, stated and intended meanings diverge. Establish the relevant speaker and context: an author can frame a character’s sincere words ironically without the character intending irony. <Term id="situational-irony"/> concerns a meaningful reversal of expectation; ordinary bad luck is not enough. <Term id="understatement"/> deliberately reduces the apparent scale of something, while hyperbole exaggerates. A euphemism softens or indirectly names an unwelcome subject; this can express tact or concealment, depending on context.

<Term id="diction"/> concerns word choice. <Term id="denotation"/> is literal meaning; <Term id="connotation"/> concerns associated meanings. “Home” and “house” overlap but are not identical in denotation: a home need not be a house. Compare their particular uses rather than treating them as exact synonyms. Syntax shapes sentences, while register concerns language appropriate to situation, audience and purpose. Short sentences can hurry action or arrest attention; long sentences can linger or rush. Test the sequence, punctuation and narrated action together.

Juxtaposition invites a relationship between adjacent elements. A motif recurs; <Term id="foreshadowing"/> anticipates a later event and can occur anywhere before it. A literary paradox challenges an assumption or appears contradictory without guaranteeing a proved truth. An oxymoron brings apparently incompatible terms together. Allusion draws on another text or shared knowledge, while defamiliarisation makes the familiar strange. Identify what changes in the particular passage.

<Term id="tone"/> is the attitude conveyed by a text or speaker; <Term id="mood"/> is the atmosphere invited. Neither establishes every reader’s actual feeling, and a fictional speaker’s attitude need not be the author’s own. “Love” can name a theme; an argument develops a supported statement about how this work treats it. Suspense concerns anticipation and uncertainty, while pace concerns experienced movement. A pause can build tension or release it.

## Worked example

This is an original illustrative paragraph, not an extract from a real family history.

> The house had been silent for a week. Its windows watched the empty road, and the kettle, which had sung every morning of my childhood, stayed cold on the stove. Every creak of the floor sounded like a question nobody wanted to answer.

<Steps>
<Step>**Personification.** “Its windows watched” gives the house a human action. It can make the empty setting seem alert, linking absence with continued waiting.</Step>
<Step>**Symbolic reading.** The change from a singing kettle to a cold one can suggest interrupted domestic routine. Bereavement is possible, but the paragraph does not establish a death or explain why the house is quiet.</Step>
<Step>**Simile.** A creak is compared with an avoided question: this is a figurative relationship between sound and social hesitation, not a simile merely because “like” appears.</Step>
<Step>**Synthesis.** Waiting, interrupted routine and avoidance support an uneasy atmosphere. Explain that interpretation through the details, and keep the cause of the absence open.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Current IB Paper 1 criterion B concerns analysis and evaluation of how authorial choices shape meaning; Paper 2 separates analysis/evaluation (B1) and comparison (B2). Plot selection and structure can be relevant choices. Accurate evidence and explanation matter; technical labels, short quotation quotas or a particular paragraph pattern do not automatically earn a score. Detailed references can support Paper 2 without memorised quotations. The two checks below are short diagnostic exercises, not miniature IB assessment rubrics.

## Common mistakes

- Inferring a speaker’s intention from words without considering context.
- Treating every object as a symbol, or demanding repetition before a symbol is possible.
- Assuming short syntax always speeds reading or that one interpretation describes every reader’s response.
- Naming grief, death or a motive that the supplied text does not establish.

## Summary

- Link a precise reference, the choice it illustrates and its contribution to meaning.
- Separate speaker, author, text and actual reader; they do not share one compulsory attitude.
- Establish figurative relationships and supported symbolic meanings in context.
- Use patterns of words, syntax and structure to test an interpretation, including its limits.
