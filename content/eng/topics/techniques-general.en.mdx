## Big idea

The same techniques appear in poems, novels, plays and speeches, so one method works for every form: name the device, quote it briefly and explain what it does to the reader.

## Explanation

Every form uses the same toolkit, so the same questions apply to a poem, a novel, a play or a speech. Name the technique, quote it briefly and say what it does to the reader. A technique earns analysis marks only when its effect is explained.

A <Term id="metaphor"/> says that one thing is another; a <Term id="simile"/> compares them with "like" or "as". Writers use <Term id="personification"/> to give human qualities to a thing or an idea. A <Term id="symbol"/> stands for something larger than itself, and symbolism is the wider pattern of such symbols in a text. Allegory extends this across a whole story, so that each character or event stands for an idea. <Term id="imagery"/> is the sensory language that makes a scene present.

One kind of irony is <Term id="verbal-irony"/>: a speaker says one thing and means something different, often its opposite. Another is <Term id="situational-irony"/>, which happens when events turn out the reverse of what characters or readers expect. An <Term id="understatement"/> makes a serious matter seem smaller than it is; hyperbole does the reverse. Euphemism softens a harsh fact, such as "passed away" for "died", and shows what the speaker wants to avoid.

Word choice is <Term id="diction"/>. Each word has a <Term id="denotation"/>, its literal meaning, and a <Term id="connotation"/>, the feelings it carries: "home" and "house" share a denotation but not a connotation. Syntax, the shape of sentences, and register, the level of formality, also set the pace: short clipped sentences speed a passage up, and long flowing ones slow it down.

Juxtaposition places two things side by side so that the reader compares them. <Term id="foreshadowing"/> hints at what will happen next, and a motif is an image or idea repeated across the text. A paradox states something that seems impossible but holds true; an oxymoron joins two opposites, as in "a loud silence". An allusion borrows the weight of a known text or event. Defamiliarisation makes a familiar thing seem strange, so the reader sees it again.

The difference between <Term id="tone"/> and <Term id="mood"/> matters: tone is the writer's attitude to the subject, and mood is the feeling the text creates in the reader. A tender tone can build a tense mood. Theme is the central idea a text explores, not a one-word label. Suspense and pace control how the reader moves through the text: a pause builds suspense, a sudden drop after a build-up is an anticlimax, and short sentences speed the pace.

<Callout kind="define">Tone is the attitude a text takes; mood is the feeling it creates in the reader.</Callout>

## Worked example

I wrote this original paragraph for this topic. It has three techniques to find.

> The house had been silent for a week. Its windows watched the empty road, and the kettle, which had sung every morning of my childhood, stayed cold on the stove. Every creak of the floor sounded like a question nobody wanted to answer.

<Steps>
<Step>**Personification.** "Its windows watched the empty road" gives the house a human act. The <Term id="personification"/> makes the building seem alert and anxious, so the absence of the family is felt as something being watched.</Step>
<Step>**Symbol.** The kettle "had sung every morning of my childhood" and now "stayed cold". As a <Term id="symbol"/> of ordinary warmth and daily routine, the cold kettle carries the grief without the writer naming it.</Step>
<Step>**Simile.** "Every creak of the floor sounded like a question nobody wanted to answer" is a <Term id="simile"/> because it uses "like". The silence becomes a pressure of unasked questions, and the reader feels the avoidance that fills the house.</Step>
<Step>**Link the effects.** The tone is restrained and sad, and the mood is heavy and uneasy. The three techniques work together: the paragraph never names the grief, and the reader feels it through objects and sounds.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Paper 1 and Paper 2 both reward techniques that are named precisely, quoted briefly and explained through their effect. On Paper 1 this sits in criterion B: a named device, a short quotation and a judgement of how well it works. On Paper 2 the same skill serves criteria B1 (authorial choices) and B2 (comparison): show that the writer chose the technique, then compare that choice with the other work. The command term matters: Identify and State need a name; Explain needs the effect; Analyse needs the way the technique works; Evaluate needs a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Naming a device without quoting the words it applies to.
- Calling any object a symbol. A symbol recurs and carries meaning beyond its literal use.
- Confusing tone, the writer's attitude, with mood, the reader's feeling.
- Writing "the writer uses irony" without saying what is ironic and what it means.

<Callout kind="mistake">A device name followed by "which creates atmosphere" earns no analysis marks: say which words create it and how.</Callout>

## Summary

- Name the technique, quote the words it applies to and state its effect on the reader.
- In figurative language a metaphor says "is", a simile says "like" or "as", personification gives human acts, and a symbol carries meaning beyond itself.
- Irony means saying the opposite or getting the reverse of what is expected; understatement and hyperbole move the scale down or up.
- Diction, connotation and denotation work at word level; syntax and register shape pace and formality.
- Tone is the writer's attitude, mood is the reader's feeling, and theme is the central idea.
- Structure (juxtaposition, motif, foreshadowing, anticlimax, suspense) shapes what the reader expects next.
