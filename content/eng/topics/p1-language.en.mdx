## Big idea

Criterion D concerns clear, accurate and effective language: appropriate vocabulary, grammar and register. Literary terms help when they name the writer's choice accurately; an elaborate word is not automatically better than a clear one.

## Explanation

The literary present is a useful convention: “the poet presents”, “the speaker suggests”, “the narrator reveals”. Use other tenses where the meaning needs them: a prior story event, a historical context or a change in the narrator's perspective. Keep the time relationships clear. A past-tense verb is not by itself an IB marking penalty.

Choose the verb that fits the evidence. “Suggests” and “implies” describe an indirect meaning; “reinforces” strengthens an established effect; “undercuts” weakens it. “Shows”, “is” and “has” are valid words. Replacing every instance with “suggests” can make a direct observation needlessly uncertain. Improve the explanation, rather than follow a banned-word list.

<Callout kind="define">Denotation is a word's literal meaning; connotation is its associations. Those associations depend on context, not just a dictionary label.</Callout>

“The poem is good” gives no reason for the judgement. “The fly interrupts the solemn wait with a trivial sound” names an action and contrast that can be checked against Dickinson's poem. “Elegiac” is useful only if the passage really has a mournful or commemorative quality.

Quote accurately and explain the relevant wording. A short quotation often keeps the analysis focused; a longer line can be appropriate when syntax, rhythm or a developing image is the point. There is no one- or two-word quotation quota. Fit a quotation into your sentence grammatically, or introduce a longer quotation clearly and then analyse it.

A <Term id="simile"/> explicitly compares unlike things, often using “like” or “as”. The words alone do not prove a simile: “she works as a doctor” names a role, not a figurative comparison. A <Term id="metaphor"/> presents one thing in terms of another. Identify what is compared and why it matters.

Verbal irony involves a difference between the stated and intended meaning. Situational irony involves an important reversal of expectation; dramatic irony gives the audience knowledge a character lacks. Sarcasm is a cutting or mocking manner that can use verbal irony. The categories can overlap: irony is not always sarcastic, and a sarcastic insult need not be ironic.

<Term id="diction"/> concerns word choice, <Term id="register"/> the level and character of language, and <Term id="syntax"/> sentence structure. A <Term id="hyperbole"/> exaggerates, an <Term id="understatement"/> deliberately reduces the apparent force, and a <Term id="euphemism"/> substitutes a milder expression for an uncomfortable one. Explain the particular effect rather than list labels.

## Worked example

Vague: “The poem is very good, and it shows that the room is quiet, which is a nice touch.”

<Steps>
<Step>**Specify the observation.** The room's stillness is compared with the air between the heaves of a storm. “Good” and “nice” do not explain that comparison.</Step>
<Step>**Choose relevant evidence.** Dickinson's “the stillness in the air” belongs to a comparison that continues into the storm image. Read those lines together; do not assign a fixed connotation of waiting to every use of “quiet”.</Step>
<Step>**Explain the choice.** The simile makes the stillness feel like a tense interval before a disturbance. That is a supported interpretation of this context.</Step>
<Step>**Write a clear sentence.** Dickinson's simile compares the stillness around the speaker with “the stillness in the air” before a storm, making the deathbed silence feel expectant rather than peaceful.</Step>
</Steps>

The poem does not specify the speaker's gender. A precise analysis can say “the speaker” without supplying one.

## How it is examined

<Callout kind="exam">Paper 1 criterion D has a maximum of 5 marks. The assessment concerns language across the response: clarity, accuracy, appropriate vocabulary, sentence construction and register. It is not a checklist of approved verbs, quotation lengths or mandatory terms.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- Using an impressive term with the wrong meaning.
- Replacing a vague judgement with another label but giving no evidence.
- Changing tense so that the sequence of events becomes unclear.
- Altering a quotation or leaving its relevance unexplained.
- Treating every “like” or “as” as figurative, or all irony as sarcasm.

## Summary

- Prefer clear, precise language to a word-replacement formula.
- Use the literary present where appropriate and keep time relationships clear.
- Quote enough to establish your point accurately, then analyse it.
- Name a literary choice only when the term fits.
- Distinguish kinds of irony and recognise their possible overlap with sarcasm.
