## Big idea

Criterion D rewards precise literary language: a present tense for the text, exact verbs and terms, and quotations that are short, exact and built into your own sentences.

## Explanation

Write about the text in the present tense: "the poet presents", "the speaker suggests", "the narrator reveals", even when the events in the extract are in the past. Use the past only for events in the story that happened before the moment of the extract, such as "the mother had died before the scene began". A shift from present to past makes the reader unsure what the text does and what the story did.

Choose verbs with exact meanings. "Suggests" means the text points to something indirectly. "Implies" means the reader must draw a conclusion. "Reinforces" means an earlier effect is made stronger. "Undercuts" means the text weakens or contradicts what it has just set up. Verbs such as "shows", "is" and "has" are often too weak for analysis: they state that something exists, not what it does.

<Callout kind="define">Denotation is the literal, dictionary meaning of a word; connotation is the set of associations that the word carries.</Callout>

Remove vague words. "Good", "nice", "very", "a lot", "things" and "stuff" name no quality at all. Replace them with an exact description or a term. "The poem is very sad" becomes "the poem's tone is elegiac"; "the fly is a nice touch" becomes "the fly interrupts the solemn wait with a trivial sound".

Use short quotations and build them into your own sentence, so that the grammar works. Quote the one or two words that carry the point. A full line that stands alone, followed by a comment, is hard to read, and a long quotation hides the analysis. Introduce each quotation with a verb or phrase that says what it does.

Use terms exactly. A <Term id="simile"/> compares two things with "like" or "as"; a <Term id="metaphor"/> says that one thing is another. Irony is not sarcasm: in literature, irony is a gap between what is said and what is meant, while sarcasm is usually a spoken jibe. Use a term only where it fits the device, and do not use it just to show that you know it.

Name the writer's word choice precisely. <Term id="diction"/> is the choice of words; <Term id="register"/> is the level of formality; <Term id="syntax"/> is the structure of the sentences. A <Term id="hyperbole"/> exaggerates for effect, an <Term id="understatement"/> makes something seem smaller than it is, and a <Term id="euphemism"/> softens a hard fact. Name the device exactly, then say what the choice does.

## Worked example

Here is a sentence built from vague words. Rewrite it step by step in precise literary language.

Vague: "The poem is very good, and it shows that the room is quiet, which is a nice touch."

<Steps>
<Step>**Find the vague words.** "Very" and "good" name no quality, "shows" says nothing about how the poem works, and "nice" is only a feeling. Mark them before you rewrite.</Step>
<Step>**Choose the precise words.** The <Term id="denotation"/> of "quiet" is simply silence, but its <Term id="connotation"/> is a silence that is waiting. "Shows" becomes "suggests" or "reinforces", depending on whether the quiet is a hint or a strengthening of an earlier effect.</Step>
<Step>**Integrate a quotation.** Dickinson writes that the stillness round the speaker "Was like the stillness in the air", so you can quote the short phrase "the stillness in the air" and place it inside your sentence as the object of a verb.</Step>
<Step>**Name the device exactly.** The comparison of the stillness to the air before a storm is a <Term id="simile"/>, because it uses "like". Do not call it a metaphor.</Step>
<Step>**Check the tense.** Write about the poem in the present tense: "the simile reinforces" and "the poet presents", not "the poet presented".</Step>
</Steps>

Precise: "Dickinson's simile, which compares "the stillness round my form" to "the stillness in the air", reinforces the tension of the moment before death."

|---|---|---|
| shows | suggests, implies, reveals, dramatises | "The poem shows death" becomes "The poem dramatises death as an interruption" |
| good | a named effect, such as unsettling or exact | "The imagery is good" becomes "The imagery is exact and unsettling" |
| very sad | a precise tone, such as elegiac or grief-stricken | "The poem is very sad" becomes "The poem's tone is elegiac" |
| nice | a precise judgement, such as delicate or unexpected | "a nice touch" becomes "an unexpected touch" |
| is | presents, establishes or undercuts | "The poem is about waiting" becomes "The poem establishes waiting as its central experience" |

## How it is examined

<Callout kind="exam">Criterion D is worth up to 5 marks. Examiners look at the precision of literary vocabulary, the accuracy of the tense, short quotations that are integrated into the sentence, and the correct use of terms.</Callout>

Language is assessed across the whole response, so one precise paragraph cannot make up for vague ones. Use "suggests" or "implies" when you are not certain, and "shows" only when the text really does show something. Check the spelling of the author's name and of titles, and the exact form of each term.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">The most common loss of criterion D marks is vague language that hides a good analysis.</Callout>

- Using "shows", "good" or "very" where a precise verb or adjective is needed.
- Writing about the text in the past tense: "the poet presented" instead of "the poet presents".
- Dropping a quotation into a sentence with no introduction and no comment.
- Quoting a whole line when one or two words make the point.
- Calling a simile a metaphor, or irony sarcasm.

## Summary

- Write about the text in the present tense: the poet presents, the speaker suggests.
- Use precise verbs: suggests, implies, reinforces and undercuts mean different things.
- Replace vague words such as good, very, shows and nice with an exact description or term.
- Quote one or two key words and build them into your own sentence.
- Use terms exactly: a simile uses like or as; irony is not sarcasm.
- Name a writer's word choice precisely: diction, register, syntax, hyperbole, understatement or euphemism.
