## Big idea

Criterion B2 rewards a comparison that runs through the whole answer. Each paragraph sets the two works side by side and explains why the difference matters for the prompt.

## Explanation

A comparison point has three parts. The first is a similarity or a difference: both works place a small creature in a bleak scene, or one work ends in silence and the other in a question. The second is one technique in each work that shows it, such as a <Term id="symbol"/>, a sound or a choice of narrator. The third is the so what: why the difference matters for the prompt. Without the third part, a paragraph only describes two works side by side.

<Callout kind="define">The so what is the consequence of a similarity or a difference for the prompt: it says why the comparison matters.</Callout>

Compare point by point, not work by work. Writing the whole of the first work and then the whole of the second gives two summaries and a conclusion. Each paragraph should move between the two works, so that the examiner sees both in every point.

Comparison words make the link visible. Similarity words, such as similarly, both or in the same way, show a shared choice. Difference words, such as whereas, while, unlike or in contrast, show a contrast. A comparison word does not compare by itself: the technique and the so what do that work.

Compare effects, not only techniques. Two works may share a <Term id="theme"/>, such as death or hope, yet treat it differently. Two works may both use <Term id="juxtaposition"/> or <Term id="irony"/>, yet leave the reader with different feelings. A <Term id="mood"/> is the feeling a passage creates, and a <Term id="tone"/> is the attitude behind the words. Name which one you compare.

One work may give the weather a human face through <Term id="personification"/>, while the other lets the weather mirror the speaker's feelings through <Term id="pathetic-fallacy"/>. That is a difference in technique, and it matters if the prompt asks who controls the mood.

The so what is the link to the prompt. Ask what the difference reveals about the question. If the prompt asks how a creature changes a mood, a difference in what the creature does is the answer. A difference that does not touch the prompt is a detail, not a point.

Use this invented prompt for the example: "Compare how two poems use a small creature in a still scene to change the mood. To what extent does the creature, rather than the setting, cause the change?"

<Steps>
<Step>**Choose one point.** Both poems place a small living creature in a still, bleak scene, and in both the creature changes the mood. This is a similarity in technique and in effect.</Step>
<Step>**Technique in the first poem.** The fly enters after a silence compared to "the stillness in the air / Between the heaves of storm". The dash and the line break before "There interposed a fly" make the reader wait for it.</Step>
<Step>**Technique in the second poem.** The bird is "An aged thrush, frail, gaunt, and small", and it sings in a landscape where "every spirit upon earth / Seemed fervourless as I". The irony of "So little cause for carollings" makes the joy stranger, not less real.</Step>
<Step>**The so what.** The difference matters for the prompt. The fly breaks the expected ritual of dying, so the mood collapses into anticlimax. The thrush's song opens a possibility, so the mood turns towards hope. The creature causes the change in both poems, but in opposite directions.</Step>
</Steps>

Both poets place a small living creature in a still, bleak scene. Dickinson's fly interrupts a moment that should be solemn: "There interposed a fly" comes after a silence compared to "the stillness in the air / Between the heaves of storm". Similarly, Hardy's thrush, "An aged thrush, frail, gaunt, and small", sings in a landscape where "every spirit upon earth / Seemed fervourless as I". Whereas the fly leads to an anticlimax, and the speaker's sight fails in "I could not see to see", the thrush leads to a tentative hope, because the speaker imagines that the bird knows "Some blessed Hope, whereof he knew". The difference matters for the prompt: in Dickinson the small creature breaks meaning apart, while in Hardy it opens the possibility of meaning. So the creature matters in both poems, but one interrupts while the other sings.

## How it is examined

Criterion B2 (0–5) checks the comparison itself. The top band needs points that compare throughout, each with a technique in both works and an explicit so what linked to the prompt. The middle band compares in some points only, or compares topics rather than what the writers do with them. The low band has no comparison, or two summaries side by side. Comparison words earn no marks on their own. They make the comparison visible to the examiner.

## Common mistakes

- Writing two summaries, one per work, and comparing them only in the conclusion.
- Naming a similarity ("both works are sad") with no technique in either work.
- Joining two unrelated facts with "whereas" or "while". A contrast needs a shared point.
- Comparing topics ("both have a storm") instead of what the writers do with the storm.
- Ending the paragraph without the so what, so the link to the prompt is missing.

<Callout kind="mistake">Two accurate summaries side by side are still not a comparison.</Callout>

## Summary

- A comparison point has a similarity or a difference, one technique in each work, and a so what.
- Compare point by point, not work by work.
- Use similarity words (similarly, both) and difference words (whereas, while, unlike, in contrast).
- Compare the effect on the reader, not only the technique.
- The so what links the difference to the prompt. A difference that does not touch the prompt is only a detail.
- Test the opening sentence with the thesis builder: both works, a comparison word and a claim about effect.
