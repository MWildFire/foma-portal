## Big idea

Criterion C rewards an answer that stays on the guiding question from the first sentence to the last. The thesis gives the answer, each paragraph develops one step of it, and the links show how the steps connect.

## Explanation

The introduction has one job: to answer the guiding question in one sentence. That sentence is the thesis. It must say what the extract does and what effect this has, not only what the extract is about. "Dickinson writes about death" names a topic; "Dickinson makes the moment of death an interruption rather than a climax" makes a claim that the rest of the essay can prove.

<Callout kind="define">A thesis is one sentence in the introduction that answers the guiding question and makes a claim about the effect of the extract.</Callout>

Each body paragraph develops one step of the thesis. Open it with a topic sentence that states the step and links it back to the thesis, for example: "The silence around the speaker is the first sign that the expected ending will not come." Then give evidence, explain the effect, and end the paragraph on that effect, not on a summary of the lines.

Links carry the argument from one paragraph to the next. Use phrases that show the logic: "by contrast" when the next point opposes the last, "this suggests" when a detail supports a claim, "similarly" when two points work alike, and "in turn" when one effect causes another. Avoid long runs of "furthermore" and "in addition", which add nothing.

Stay on the guiding question. Before writing each paragraph, ask whether it helps answer the question. If it only describes the extract, cut it, or connect it with a sentence that begins "This matters because". General comments about the poet or the period belong in the essay only when they change the reading.

Retelling says what happens; focus says what the writer does and why it matters. A sentence that someone could write without analysing the text is retelling. Rewrite it so that the point comes first, then the evidence, then the effect, and the effect answers the question.

Organise the paragraphs so that they build an argument. Many extracts move through an <Term id="exposition"/>, where the situation is set; a <Term id="climax"/>, where tension peaks; and a <Term id="denouement"/>, where it resolves or stays open. Your essay can follow that order when it serves the argument. Inside a paragraph, an <Term id="antithesis"/> ("not X but Y") makes a clear link, and <Term id="anaphora"/>, the repetition of words at the start of clauses, joins points through repetition. A <Term id="rhetorical-question"/> can close one paragraph and open the next, used sparingly.

## Worked example

The guiding question for the Dickinson extract is: How does Dickinson use the sound of a fly and the image of stillness to present the experience of dying? Test a retelling sentence against it first.

Retelling: "In the poem the speaker is dying, a fly comes into the room, and everyone waits for death."

Focused: "Dickinson makes the buzz of a fly the sound that interrupts the stillness of dying, so death appears less like a climax than like a small, absurd delay."

Now plan the whole response. Each step below is one paragraph, and each link tells the reader why the next point follows.

<Steps>
<Step>**Introduction: the thesis.** "Dickinson makes the moment of death an interruption rather than a climax." One sentence that answers the question: sound and stillness are both present through the word "interruption", and the sentence makes a claim about effect.</Step>
<Step>**Body 1: the expectation.** Topic sentence: "The poem begins with a stillness that promises a solemn ending." Evidence: "The stillness round my form / Was like the stillness in the air / Between the heaves of storm." The <Term id="exposition"/> sets the situation, and the effect is that the calm feels threatening, as if a storm were about to break.</Step>
<Step>**Body 2: the turn.** Topic sentence: "By contrast, the fly's buzz is small and ordinary." Evidence: "and then / There interposed a fly," with the dash and the pause before the intruder. The <Term id="climax"/> that the room has prepared for is replaced by something trivial. Link: "This suggests that the expected ending is only an expectation."</Step>
<Step>**Body 3: the failure of the senses.** Topic sentence: "The senses fail just when they are most needed." Evidence: "And then the windows failed, and then / I could not see to see." The repeated "and then" works as an <Term id="anaphora"/> that slows the reader, so the loss of sight arrives step by step.</Step>
<Step>**Conclusion: the insight.** Do not repeat the thesis. The poem offers no <Term id="denouement"/>: it ends in failed sight, not in a resolved scene. The insight is that dying appears as the loss of the power to witness it, the reverse of the drama the room expected.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

<Callout kind="exam">Criterion C is worth up to 5 marks. Examiners look for an answer that stays on the guiding question in every paragraph, a clear order of points, explicit links between paragraphs, and a conclusion that adds an insight.</Callout>

Each command term shapes the response. Discuss and evaluate ask for an argued view, so the thesis must take a position and the conclusion must weigh it. Analyse asks for the effect of each point, so every paragraph should end on an effect rather than on a description. Whatever the command term, the first sentence of each paragraph should show the reader where the answer is going.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">The most common loss of criterion C marks is an answer that retells the extract well and never answers the question.</Callout>

- A thesis that names the topic instead of answering the question.
- Paragraphs that open with a summary of the lines and reach the analysis only in the last sentence.
- Paragraphs with no link to the thesis or to the point before them.
- A conclusion that repeats the introduction word for word, or introduces a new idea.
- Drifting into general comments about the poet or the period.

## Summary

- The thesis is one sentence: it answers the guiding question and claims an effect.
- Each paragraph opens with a topic sentence that links back to the thesis.
- Link paragraphs with phrases that show the logic, such as "by contrast" or "this suggests".
- Rewrite retelling as a point, evidence and an effect that answers the question.
- Order the paragraphs so that they build an argument, not the order of the lines.
- The conclusion develops an insight; it does not repeat the introduction.
