## Big idea

A speech or non-fiction essay is an argument aimed at a particular audience. Your task is to show how the writer uses appeals, patterns of language and structure to move that audience, and where the argument turns.

## Explanation

Non-fiction and speeches are written to change what an audience thinks, feels or does. Before you name any device, ask two questions: who is the audience, and what does the writer want it to do? A speech to a crowd at a national holiday is not the same as an essay for readers who already agree. <Term id="audience"/> and <Term id="purpose"/> set the frame for every technique you name in an answer.

Classical rhetoric gives three appeals. <Term id="ethos"/> is the speaker's credibility: why should this audience trust this person? <Term id="pathos"/> appeals to emotion, such as pity, anger, pride or shame. <Term id="logos"/> appeals to reason through facts, examples and a chain of claims. Most strong passages mix all three, so say which one dominates and where it passes to another.

<Callout kind="define">Ethos, pathos and logos are the three appeals: credibility, emotion and reason.</Callout>

Writers also use patterns of language. <Term id="rhetorical-question"/> asks a question whose answer the listener is expected to supply, so the audience reaches the conclusion on its own. <Term id="anaphora"/> repeats the same opening word or phrase in successive clauses, which builds pressure. <Term id="antithesis"/> places two opposed ideas in matching grammar, so the gap between them feels fixed. <Term id="tricolon"/> is a list of three, which often sounds complete and final. Polysyndeton, with many "and"s, slows the pace and piles up feeling; asyndeton, with no conjunctions, speeds it up. Chiasmus reverses the order of a pair, as in "We must live to work, not work to live", which gives the sentence a sense of balance.

Speakers also use <Term id="direct-address"/>, as in "fellow citizens" or "you". It pulls the listener into the argument and makes the audience a participant rather than a witness. Satire mocks a target so that the audience laughs first and judges second.

The <Term id="argument"/> is the chain of claims that leads to a conclusion. Look at how it is built: a claim or question opens it, evidence supports it, a concession may give ground, and then the argument turns. The turn is often signalled by a word such as "but" or "yet", or by a shift from "they" to "we". <Term id="rhetoric"/> is the whole craft of using these means well. In the exam, your task is to name the means, say who they work on, and show where the argument changes direction.

## Worked example

<Question id="p1-douglass-fourth"/>

The guiding question asks how Douglass uses rhetorical questions, direct address and an allusion to make the audience question the meaning of the Fourth of July. This commentary takes two of his devices and the three appeals, which is the core of any non-fiction answer.

<Steps>
<Step>**Find the appeals.** Ethos: Douglass speaks as a formerly enslaved man who knows the injustice from inside, and "I am not that man" separates him from the grateful listener. Pathos: "The sunlight that brought life and healing to you, has brought stripes and death to me" makes the audience feel the cost of its own holiday. Logos: the argument moves in steps. If the principles of freedom were extended to the enslaved, he would rejoice; they are not, so he must mourn.</Step>
<Step>**Take the question.** The <Term id="rhetorical-question"/> "why am I called upon to speak here to-day?" comes before any answer, so the listeners must supply the first one. Few of them can do so without admitting that the invitation is an insult.</Step>
<Step>**Take the contrast.** The <Term id="antithesis"/> "The sunlight that brought life and healing to you, has brought stripes and death to me" sets one sunlight against two groups. The matching grammar of "to you" and "to me" makes the gap feel fixed, so the listener cannot comfortably stay on the side of "you".</Step>
<Step>**Link to the question.** Together the question and the antithesis make the audience see the holiday through the eyes of the enslaved. That is the effect the guiding question asks you to explain.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Non-fiction and speech extracts are marked on the same four criteria as every Paper 1 text, each out of 5. Criterion A checks that you understand the situation and the speaker's point. Criterion B asks for named devices and their effect on the audience, so every technique must end with what it does to the listener. Criterion C checks that the whole argument answers the guiding question. On Paper 2 the same skill becomes comparison: name an appeal that both writers use and show how each one uses it differently. Command terms: Analyse asks how the writer creates an effect; Evaluate asks how well it works; Compare asks for both similarities and differences.

## Common mistakes

- Listing devices (anaphora, tricolon, a rhetorical question) without saying what each one does to the audience.
- Calling every question "rhetorical". A real question asks for information; a rhetorical question expects an answer the listener already has.
- Describing the speaker's feelings instead of the effect on the listeners.
- Missing the turn: the point where the argument changes direction, often marked by "but", "yet" or a shift from "they" to "we".

<Callout kind="mistake">A device with no stated effect on the audience earns no analysis marks.</Callout>

## Summary

- Start with the audience and the purpose: they decide which appeals matter most.
- Ethos is credibility, pathos is emotion and logos is reason; name the one that dominates.
- A rhetorical question makes the listener supply the answer; anaphora builds pressure; antithesis fixes a contrast; a tricolon sounds complete.
- Direct address pulls the listener into the argument.
- Trace the argument: opening claim, evidence, concession, turn.
- A device earns analysis marks only when you state its effect on the listener.
