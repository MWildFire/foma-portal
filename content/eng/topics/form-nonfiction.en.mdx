## Big idea

Non-fiction includes argument, narrative, memoir, description and reflection. This lesson focuses on persuasive speeches. Analyse what the text invites an audience to think or feel, while distinguishing an intended effect from evidence of how actual listeners reacted.

## Explanation

Begin with <Term id="audience"/> and <Term id="purpose"/>. Identify the occasion and intended readers or listeners where the source provides them; avoid inventing a single response for a diverse audience. A memoir may explore an experience without making a direct appeal to action.

In Aristotle’s account, persuasion can work through the speaker’s presented character (<Term id="ethos"/>), the audience’s emotions (<Term id="pathos"/>) and proof or apparent proof in the argument (<Term id="logos"/>). A passage need not contain all three. An appeal to reason is not automatically a sound argument, and an appeal to emotion is not automatically irrational.

<Callout kind="define">Ethos concerns credibility, pathos emotional disposition, and logos reasoning or apparent proof. Explain their use in context rather than merely listing the labels.</Callout>

A <Term id="rhetorical-question"/> functions as an assertion, challenge or prompt for reflection rather than a straightforward request for new information. The speaker may answer it. <Term id="anaphora"/> repeats opening wording in successive units. <Term id="antithesis"/> contrasts ideas, often with parallel phrasing, while <Term id="tricolon"/> groups three parallel elements. Polysyndeton adds repeated conjunctions; asyndeton omits expected ones. Either can affect rhythm in several ways: read the actual syntax instead of assigning an automatic slow or fast effect. Chiasmus reverses a grammatical pattern; repeated words in reverse order are often called antimetabole.

<Term id="direct-address"/> can include, accuse, flatter or distance an audience. Satire can expose folly through ridicule or irony without guaranteeing laughter. Trace how claims, examples, concessions and turns relate; not every passage follows the same sequence. A conjunction such as “but” matters because of the ideas it connects, not just because it appears.

## Worked example

<Question id="p1-douglass-fourth"/>

Douglass delivered this speech on 5 July 1852 in Rochester to an audience assembled by an anti-slavery society. The source context does not justify treating every listener as a supporter of slavery or reporting their feelings as known facts.

<Steps>
<Step>**Follow the conditional argument.** Douglass first imagines freedom genuinely extended to those he represents. His questions about who could refuse gratitude belong to that hypothetical case. “I am not that man” denies that he would be ungrateful if those benefits were real; it does not reject gratitude as such.</Step>
<Step>**Find the turn.** “But, such is not the state of the case” rejects the hypothetical premise. He contrasts promised liberty with exclusion and violence. This is a reasoned distinction as well as an emotional appeal.</Step>
<Step>**Analyse the address and contrast.** The repeated opposition of “you” and “me” separates experiences of the same celebration. The sunlight image makes a contrast between benefit and suffering vivid. It can challenge listeners to confront exclusion; agreement or guilt is a possible response, not a measured result.</Step>
<Step>**Place the allusion.** The image of a lame person leaping, associated with Isaiah 35:6, occurs in the imagined case of liberation. Its joyful possibility sharpens the following return to the actual situation. Explain that sequence instead of treating the allusion only as an accusation.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Assuming that all non-fiction is an explicit argument.
- Giving a device a fixed effect without considering its words and context.
- Reversing a conditional argument, as though hypothetical gratitude were present gratitude.
- Treating an intended emotional appeal as proof that every listener felt it.
- Replacing analysis of language with the labels ethos, pathos and logos.

<Callout kind="mistake">Support an interpretation of effect with evidence and explain the reasoning. Alternative readings can be valid when grounded in the passage.</Callout>

## Summary

- Establish audience, purpose and genre from the available evidence.
- Distinguish credibility, emotional appeal and reasoning without forcing all three into every passage.
- Explain the particular work done by repetition, contrast, questions or address.
- Track the premises and turns of an argument.
- Intended effects are interpretations; actual audience reactions need separate evidence.
