## Big idea

Yun Dong-ju's poems and Krakauer's *Into the Wild* share four questions: who a person is (identity), what a person is called (names), how a person judges himself, and which road he takes. The forms differ, and that difference is what makes the pair useful for Paper 2. A poem gives one voice a few lines, so the reader sees a self only as far as the speaker lets it show. Krakauer's book is non-fiction: an author who investigates, interviews, quotes letters and appears in his own story. A strong answer shows, in each point, how the form shapes the self it presents.

## Explanation

Yun Dong-ju (1917–1945) was a Korean poet. He was born in 1917 in Manchuria (Buk-Gando), was arrested in 1943 for alleged anti-Japanese activity, and died in Fukuoka prison in February 1945, aged 27. His poems were published after his death, in the collection *Sky, Wind, Star, and Poem* (1948). Jon Krakauer published *Into the Wild* in 1996. The book grew out of a magazine article he wrote about Chris McCandless in 1993. McCandless graduated in 1990, cut ties with his family, took a new name and died in the Alaskan wilderness in 1992.

The three poems on the class list have not been confirmed yet, so this page uses five well-known poems from the 1948 collection: *Prologue*, *Self-Portrait*, *Counting the Stars at Night*, *An Easily Written Poem* and *Confession*. If your class studied another poem, use the same method with its lines. Before you use a poem, check that it is not your Individual Oral text: the school rule is that IO texts are not used on Paper 2. Stanza and line numbers follow the 1948 edition, except for *Self-Portrait*, which follows the line division of a 1998 article. Korean lines are quoted two at a time at most, each with our literal translation.

**The book's text.** The text of *Into the Wild* is not in the school folder, which holds only the poems anthology. So the book examples on this page come from the chapter references and summaries that the portal pages already use, and this page quotes nothing from the book. In your own answer, give a chapter for every book example, and take any quotation from your own copy.

**Form.** The voice of the poem, the <Term id="lyric-persona"/>, speaks through <Term id="imagery"/> (the well, the moon, the stars, the mirror) and direct address, which is an <Term id="apostrophe"/>: the speaker calls to his mother in *Counting the Stars at Night*. Krakauer works as an investigator. He interviews the people who met McCandless, reconstructs the journey from letters, postcards and a diary, and appears in the book as its <Term id="narrator"/> and as one of its characters. The poem shows a self from inside one voice. The book shows a person through what others say and what the author chooses to show.

<Callout kind="define">A narrator is the voice that presents the events and decides how much of them the reader is allowed to know.</Callout>

**Names and identity.** Identity is the <Term id="theme"/> that links the pair. In *Counting the Stars at Night* each star works as a <Term id="metaphor"/> for something the speaker holds: memory, love, loneliness or longing (st. 3). He calls each star a beautiful word (st. 4), then writes his name on a hill and covers it with earth (st. 7). In stanza 8 the insects that weep all night grieve over a shameful name (st. 8, l. 2: 부끄러운 이름을 슬퍼하는 까닭입니다; our literal translation: "are grieving over a shameful name"). The name is buried. In the book McCandless introduces himself as Alex and takes the name Alexander Supertramp (chapter 3), so the book sets the identity he chose against the one his family knew. The poem buries its name; the book chooses one.

**Self-judgement.** *Confession* opens in a mirror: 파란 녹이 낀 구리거울 속에 / 내 얼골이 남어있는것은 (st. 1, l. 1–2; our literal translation: "In the blue-green rusted bronze mirror, my face remains"), and it asks why that face is so shameful. The mirror is a <Term id="symbol"/> of the self, and it returns as a <Term id="motif"/> in stanzas 4 and 5. In *Self-Portrait* the speaker's view of the man in the well moves from hatred to pity to attachment (st. 2–4): 어쩐지 그 사나이가 미워져 돌아갑니다 (st. 2, l. 2; our literal translation: "Somehow I come to hate that man, and I turn back"). The book spreads its judgement across other voices: the witnesses who describe McCandless (chapters 3 and 6), and the family conflict of chapter 12, which the author presents through McCandless's anger.

**The road.** The *Prologue* describes a road that comes from outside the speaker: 그리고 나한테 주어진 길을 / 걸어가야겠다 (st. 2, l. 3–4; our literal translation: "and the road that has been given to me; I shall walk it"). The book runs the other way. McCandless chooses his road, which means leaving his family's hopes and the rules of society behind, and his letter to Ron Franz (chapters 6 and 7) urges an older man to leave his routine for the road. Even the author has a road he did not take: his father wanted him to pursue a medical career, and he did not follow that path (chapter 15). Placed side by side, the two roads form a <Term id="juxtaposition"/> of given and chosen, and that contrast is the test for any answer on this pair.

**Two deaths, two kinds of evidence.** The poet died in Fukuoka prison in 1945, and the cause of his death is not established: the theories that exist are not confirmed. The book's central figure died in the Alaskan wilderness in 1992. The official cause was starvation, but the author offers another explanation, and in chapter 18 he revises his hypothesis after lab tests found no toxins in the first samples. Do not set the two causes side by side as if they were the same kind of evidence: one is an open question of history, and the other is an investigation that the author reports and corrects.

## Worked example

Six plans follow, one for each of six bank questions. Each gives the thesis, three comparative points with their locations, and the point that earns B2. Every plan compares the form of the poem with the form of the book. Use them as a checklist, not as a script to memorise.

**Bank question p2-q02 (Discuss): identity against the community's expectations.** Thesis: in the poems a self is judged from inside its own voice; in the book it is judged through witnesses and family, so the form decides who judges. Point 1: *Self-Portrait* (st. 2–4) against McCandless's name change (chapter 3): the speaker's view of the man in the well moves from hatred to pity to attachment, while McCandless presents himself as Alex, then as Alexander Supertramp, against the family he left (chapter 12). Point 2: the mirror in *Confession* (st. 1 and 3), which asks why a youthful confession was so shameful, against the witnesses who describe McCandless (chapters 3 and 6). Point 3: the cost of the choice, the shameful name that the insects grieve over in *Counting the Stars at Night* (st. 8), against McCandless's falling out of touch with his family (chapter 12). B2: each point sets the lyric voice's judgement beside the judgement the author reports.

**Bank question p2-q06 (Discuss): transformation, sudden or gradual.** Thesis: the poems compress a change into a few stanzas that turn back on themselves, while the book makes the change gradual and reconstructs it over years. Point 1: in *Self-Portrait* the hatred of stanza 2 returns in stanza 4 after the pity of stanza 3, so the change is a cycle of return, not a straight line. Point 2: in *Counting the Stars at Night* the carefree counting of stanza 1 turns in stanza 2 to the admission that the stars cannot now all be counted, so the mood turns within two stanzas. Point 3: McCandless's change over two years begins after he learns his father's secret (chapter 12), and the letters and witnesses (chapters 3, 6 and 7) let the author reconstruct it slowly. B2: each point sets a turn inside a short lyric beside a change that the book's form spreads across its chapters.

**Bank question p2-q09 (Discuss): dialogue that exposes a conflict nobody names.** Thesis: in the poems the unnamed conflict is shown through address and repetition; in the book it is shown through a letter that the author reconstructs. Point 1: the apostrophe to "Mother" in *Counting the Stars at Night* (st. 4 and 6) speaks to a mother who is far away in Buk-Gando, and the poem does not say what that distance means to the speaker. Point 2: in *Confession* the speaker polishes his mirror night after night (st. 4), and the repeated act never says what the shame is. Point 3: the letter McCandless writes to Ron Franz (chapters 6 and 7) urges him to leave his routine for the road, a conflict about the road that neither man names directly. B2: each point sets what the form says aloud against what it leaves unspoken.

**Bank question p2-q10 (Evaluate): memory reshapes the past, and the role of time.** Thesis: in the poems memory is judged from the present within a few lines; in the book reshaping is the author's own slow work, and he corrects his own conclusions. Point 1: in *Confession* the adult speaker judges his twenty-four-year-old self (st. 2) and asks why that self made a shameful confession (st. 3): the past is reshaped by present judgement. Point 2: in *Counting the Stars at Night* the memories are stars counted in autumn (st. 1–3), and stanza 9 moves the speaker's name into a future spring, when grass grows on the hill. Point 3: Krakauer's investigation takes years and changes his theory (chapter 18); his own climb in chapters 14 and 15 is a memory of his youth, beside McCandless's journey. Judgement: the poems compress reshaping into a few lines, while the book shows it as slow work. B2: compare the speed of reshaping in the two forms.

**Bank question p2-q11 (Compare): first-person and third-person perspective.** Thesis: the poem's first person limits the reader to one speaker yet splits that speaker into a watched figure; the book's first-person author widens knowledge through interviews and documents, but his presence limits what he can know of McCandless's mind. Point 1: in *Self-Portrait* the reader knows only what the "I" sees in the well, where the man is still there, just as he was (st. 3, l. 2): a figure seen from outside. Point 2: in *Confession* (st. 5) the speaker sees the back of a sad person walking alone: the first person turns the self into a third-person figure. Point 3: chapter 1 is told through the eyes of a stranger who gives McCandless a lift, before the author appears (chapter 1); the author's presence in chapters 14 and 15 limits his knowledge, while his interviews widen it (chapters 3, 6 and 7). B2: each point puts a limit on knowledge beside its widening.

**Bank question p2-q15 (Discuss): failure to communicate as control.** Thesis: in the poems the failure to speak comes from distance and solitude, so it is not control; in the book McCandless chooses silence towards his family, and that silence is a form of control. Point 1: in *Counting the Stars at Night* the speaker addresses his mother, far away in Buk-Gando (st. 6); the poem records no reply, and distance decides the outcome. Point 2: in *Self-Portrait* the speaker looks quietly into the well (st. 1), and the man does not speak; the silence belongs to the speaker. Point 3: McCandless calls home less and less and then falls out of touch (chapter 12), while the epilogue shows his parents trying to reach runaways through a note; the silence he chooses decides who can speak. B2: each point ties the ability to speak to who decides the outcome: distance in the poems, McCandless in the book.

## How it is examined

Paper 2 is 105 minutes, as printed on the paper. Each of the five criteria is worth 5 marks, 25 in total.

- **A.** Define identity and expectation, and name the two plots of the book: McCandless's journey and the author's investigation. For the poems, name the speaker and the movement of each poem.
- **B1.** Name two or more techniques in each text and explain their effect: apostrophe, imagery and the turn between stanzas in the poems; interviews, letters, chapter order and the author's own climb in the book.
- **B2.** Compare the two works in every point and state the link. This pair is chosen for a contrast of form, so each point names a technique of the lyric and a technique of the non-fiction together.
- **C.** A thesis with a degree, for example "largely, but the book shows the cost of the choice more fully than the poems do", and an order that follows the prompt.
- **D.** Precise terms (lyric persona, apostrophe, narrator, hypothesis) and locations: stanza and line for a poem, chapter for the book. Quotations from the book are allowed in the exam only from your own copy, with the chapter.

<Callout kind="exam">An answer scores better when it treats the poem's address and the book's sources as choices of form, not as plot.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">The most common error on this pair is to read the speaker of a poem as the poet's own biography.</Callout>

- Comparing plots (who speaks to whom, who dies, where the journey goes) instead of the techniques of each form: apostrophe and imagery against narration, interviews and documents.
- Treating the lyric "I" as the poet's biography: the mother who is far away in *Counting the Stars at Night* belongs to the speaker, and the poet's life is context, not proof.
- Treating the book's author as a neutral narrator: Krakauer investigates, interviews, chooses which letters to show and appears in his own story (chapters 14 and 15).
- Stating a cause of death as proven: the poet's cause is not established, and the book's cause is the author's hypothesis, which he revises in chapter 18.
- Quoting without a location: give a stanza and line for every line of a poem, and a chapter for every point about the book.

## Summary

- The pair shares four questions: identity, names, self-judgement and the road.
- The forms differ: a lyric "I" speaks through imagery and address, while the book's author investigates, interviews and narrates.
- Given against chosen is the key contrast: the poem accepts a road from outside, while the book follows one that McCandless chooses.
- The poems give no cause of death, and the poet's death in 1945 has no established cause; the book's cause is a hypothesis that the author revises in chapter 18.
