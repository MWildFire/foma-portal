## Worked example

Four original Paper 2-style questions, not past-paper copies. Adapt the argument to the exact question; these are planning models, not finished essays to submit. Each numbered paragraph below compares both works.

### 1. How do writers complicate a person’s search for an independent identity?

**Thesis.** Both question the idea that leaving constraints produces a complete self: Yun makes loss symbolic, while Krakauer tests aspirations against circumstances and hindsight.

**Paragraph 1.** The Road K1–4 → C1–4 starts from an unnamed loss; chapter 4’s flooded-car episode (Summer Reading, p. 11) turns an escape from institutions into a practical predicament. Compare withheld meaning with a narrated chain of causes and consequences.

**Paragraph 2.** Self-Portrait K4–7 → C7–11 moves between hatred, pity and longing; Krakauer revisits his own youthful ambitions in chapter 15 (pp. 11–12). Both unsettle a confident self-image, through recurring observation in the lyric and retrospective self-criticism in the memoir passage.

**Paragraph 3.** The Liver K9–13 → C9–15 refuses temptation yet ends in punishment; chapter 17’s comparison with Franklin (p. 12) distinguishes different failures in responding to nature. Allusion and historical analogy both test ideals, but neither guarantees that a principled refusal leads to survival.

**Conclusion.** Independence is an aspiration whose costs remain visible. Preserve the difference between a metaphorical divided self and an investigated life.

### 2. Compare how a change of perspective encourages readers to revise a judgement.

**Thesis.** Both writers resist a single verdict, but the lyric changes emotional distance while non-fiction explicitly compares evidence and interpretation.

**Paragraph 1.** Self-Portrait K3–5 → C6–9 changes hostility into pity without changing the reflected man. Chapter 17 (Summer Reading, p. 12) distinguishes McCandless from Franklin after introducing their comparison. Repetition and a qualified analogy make the first judgement insufficient.

**Paragraph 2.** The Road K11–14 → C11–14 turns the gaze from wall to sky and then to the self beyond the wall. Chapter 15 (pp. 11–12) inserts the author’s experience into his account of another man. Both relocations expose a judging position; the author’s similarity does not prove access to McCandless’s thoughts.

**Paragraph 3.** The Liver K5–10 → C5–11 makes the protected organ something the speaker also offers to an eagle; chapter 4 (p. 11) juxtaposes McCandless’s ideals with the questions officials would ask. Paradox and imagined institutional scrutiny complicate an initial view of freedom.

**Conclusion.** Revision is a method of judgement, not a guarantee of neutrality. Ask what each new perspective reveals and still cannot establish.

### 3. To what extent do two works present suffering as valuable?

**Thesis.** Both connect discomfort with ideals, but neither supports the claim that suffering alone establishes moral worth.

**Paragraph 1.** The Liver K5–8 → C5–8 makes bodily loss a chosen cost through commands to the eagle. In chapter 15 (Summer Reading, pp. 11–12), Krakauer reassesses his hope that a difficult climb would solve his problems. Compare symbolic self-discipline with a retrospective challenge to redemptive adventure.

**Paragraph 2.** The Road K11–16 → C11–16 joins tears to persistence but never shows recovery. Chapter 17 (p. 12) balances criticism of preparation with an account of endurance. Both withhold a simple equation of effort and success, using an open lyric ending and a qualified argument.

**Paragraph 3.** Self-Portrait K5–8 → C8–14 allows pity and longing after hatred; chapter 15 (pp. 11–12) distinguishes attraction to danger from a wish to die. The optional eligible Cross K11–15 → C8–15 can sharpen the distinction between conditional sacrifice and actual risk.

**Conclusion.** Suffering matters through the values and choices surrounding it. Calling McCandless a willing martyr would erase the source’s distinction.

### 4. How do physical settings become tests of a person’s values?

**Thesis.** Settings do more than illustrate feelings: they reveal the limits of what a person believes, though a lyric image and a documented hazard demand different readings.

**Paragraph 1.** The Road K5–8 → C5–8 uses recurring stones, a gate and shadow to construct obstruction. Chapter 4’s flood and disabled car (Summer Reading, p. 11) expose the practical cost of rejecting official help. Compare patterned imagery with causal narration.

**Paragraph 2.** Self-Portrait K2 and K8 → C3–5 and C12–14 frames an unchanged world in a well; chapter 15 (pp. 11–12) revisits a mountain onto which the author had projected his hopes. Both test projection, but the well repeats a present image while retrospective narration judges an earlier belief.

**Paragraph 3.** The Liver K1–4 and K9–13 → C1–4 and C9–15 moves between shore, Caucasus and Dragon Palace. Chapter 17’s Franklin comparison (p. 12) contrasts attempts to dominate nature with unprepared dependence on it. Mythic geography and historical comparison expose competing models of power.

**Conclusion.** The landscape tests an interpretation of the self. Avoid treating the poem as literal travel evidence or the book’s natural environment as only a symbol.

## Common mistakes

- Replacing comparison with two separate summaries. Each paragraph needs both works.
- Calling the three poems three separate P2 works, or ignoring the IO restriction.
- Treating national identity as the only possible meaning of every Korean image.
- Confusing source K lines with class C lines; our literal translation is not the class translation.
- Claiming a lyric speaker is always the author, or that a narrator’s interpretation is a verified fact.
