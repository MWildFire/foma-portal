## Big idea

In prose, the narrator decides what the reader can know, when, and through whose eyes. Examiners reward a precise link between a narrative choice and what it makes the reader know, feel or judge.

## Explanation

Prose is read with two questions: who tells the story, and what does the telling allow us to see? Start with the narrator. A <Term id="narrator"/> is the voice that tells the story, and it is not the same as the author. In <Term id="first-person-narration"/> the narrator says "I" and takes part in the events or watches them, but the reader may infer more than the narrator recognises, and a retrospective narrator may know more than their earlier self. In <Term id="third-person-narration"/> the narrator refers to "she" or "he". A third-person narrator may be an <Term id="omniscient-narrator"/>, who knows the minds of all the characters and can comment on them, or may stay with one character.

The lens matters as much as the teller. <Term id="focalisation"/> is the perspective through which events are seen: whose eyes and mind filter what the reader gets. A third-person narrator can report a character's thoughts in <Term id="free-indirect-discourse"/>, with features of the character’s idiom within third-person narration and without a reporting tag such as "she thought". "Of course the train was late" might be such a case if the surrounding passage associates the complaint with that character; the isolated sentence is not decisive. Other techniques go deeper into the mind. <Term id="interior-monologue"/> presents a character’s unspoken thought, which may be ordered or fragmentary; <Term id="stream-of-consciousness"/> often imitates associative movement among thoughts, sensations and memories. These categories can overlap rather than form a strict opposition. An <Term id="unreliable-narrator"/> is one whose version the reader cannot fully trust.

Characterisation shows what a character is like. <Term id="direct-characterisation"/> states the qualities openly, as in "She was brave." <Term id="indirect-characterisation"/> reveals them through speech, action, thought and the reactions of others, so the reader has to infer them.

Structure is the shape of the story. The <Term id="exposition"/> provides background, which may appear at the start or later. Some plots build to a climax and denouement, but others remain open or rearrange that sequence. A flashback moves back in narrative time. The <Term id="setting"/> is the time and place, and it can mirror or contrast a character's mood. When feelings are attributed to nature, often reflecting a speaker’s or character’s state, the device is <Term id="pathetic-fallacy"/>, as in "The sky wept as she walked." A bright, indifferent landscape that sharpens grief by contrast is not pathetic fallacy.

<Callout kind="define">Focalisation concerns how information is selected or restricted through a perspective. It may follow a character, move between perspectives or remain external; it is not simply the grammatical person of the narrator.</Callout>

## Worked example

<Question id="p1-chopin-hour"/>

The guiding question asks how Chopin uses the contrast between the news and the spring outside to prepare the reader for Mrs. Mallard's reaction. Start with the narration, because it decides what the reader learns and when.

<Steps>
<Step>**Identify the narration.** The story is in the third person: the narrator calls her "Mrs. Mallard" and "she". The opening gives information beyond Mrs. Mallard’s immediate experience, but that detail alone does not prove unlimited knowledge of every mind. It reports Richards’s earlier action: "It was he who had been in the newspaper office when intelligence of the railroad disaster was received".</Step>
<Step>**Trace the focalisation.** From "She did not hear the story as many women have heard the same" the lens narrows to Mrs. Mallard, and the square outside is seen through her eyes: "She could see in the open square before her house the tops of trees that were all aquiver with the new spring life." This is <Term id="focalisation"/>: the reader follows what she perceives.</Step>
<Step>**Ask what the reader learns, and what is withheld.** The reader learns how the news was confirmed, through Richards's second telegram, and Mrs. Mallard says nothing about it. Grief and physical exhaustion have already been named. The account mixes bodily signs with interpretations of her inner state: "But now there was a dull stare in her eyes", and "It was not a glance of reflection, but rather indicated a suspension of intelligent thought." Calling the stare a suspension of thought goes beyond simply recording its visible appearance; distinguish observation from the narrator’s interpretation.</Step>
<Step>**Test the contrast.** The spring imagery initially contrasts with the reported grief and may also anticipate a response more complex than grief. Explain that changing relation rather than assuming she feels none of the vitality. A contrast alone is not <Term id="pathetic-fallacy"/>, which attributes emotion to nature.</Step>
<Step>**Set the characterisation against the action.** The narrator states her qualities directly: "She was young, with a fair, calm face, whose lines bespoke repression and even a certain strength." Her body then tells another story: "a sob came up into her throat and shook her, as a child who has cried itself to sleep continues to sob in its dreams." The reader meets <Term id="direct-characterisation"/> and <Term id="indirect-characterisation"/> side by side.</Step>
</Steps>

Chopin opens with Richards’s earlier actions, then follows Mrs. Mallard’s perceptions. The reader learns the news with the narrator's authority, while the spring outside is seen through her senses. The contrast between the open window and her dull stare prepares the reader for her response: the world’s vitality contrasts with her stillness and may anticipate a response not yet articulated in this extract. The account combines what her body shows with a narrator’s claims about thought, keeping the next response uncertain.

Criterion B assesses analysis and evaluation of relevant authorial choices and meaning; narrator reliability matters where the passage supports it, not as a compulsory topic. ## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Go beyond a label for narration: use precise evidence to explain how information and perspective shape meaning.</Callout>

- Calling a narrator "first person" because a character speaks in quotation marks. First-person narration means the narrator is the "I" who tells the story.
- Calling every third-person story "omniscient". Check whether the narrator enters only one mind.
- Confusing free indirect discourse with direct speech in quotation marks.
- Calling every description of nature pathetic fallacy. The device attributes emotion to nature, often from a speaker or character; contrast alone is insufficient.
- Giving a view on the narrator's reliability without evidence from the text.

## Summary

- Narration: who tells the story, and from what position (first or third person).
- Focalisation: the perspective and restriction of information; it can be internal, external or shifting.
- An omniscient narrator knows all minds and events, and may comment.
- Free indirect discourse blends features of a character’s idiom or evaluation with narration; interpret it in context, not from a missing thought tag alone.
- Direct characterisation states qualities; indirect characterisation shows them through action, speech and thought.
- Exposition gives background; a flashback goes back in time; the setting gives place and time.
- Pathetic fallacy attributes human emotion to nature; contrast alone does not establish it.
