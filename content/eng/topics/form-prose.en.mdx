## Big idea

In prose, the narrator decides what the reader can know, when, and through whose eyes. Examiners reward a precise link between a narrative choice and what it makes the reader know, feel or judge.

## Explanation

Prose is read with two questions: who tells the story, and what does the telling allow us to see? Start with the narrator. A <Term id="narrator"/> is the voice that tells the story, and it is not the same as the author. In <Term id="first-person-narration"/> the narrator says "I" and takes part in the events or watches them, so the reader knows only what this narrator knows. In <Term id="third-person-narration"/> the narrator refers to "she" or "he". A third-person narrator may be an <Term id="omniscient-narrator"/>, who knows the minds of all the characters and can comment on them, or may stay with one character.

The lens matters as much as the teller. <Term id="focalisation"/> is the perspective through which events are seen: whose eyes and mind filter what the reader gets. A third-person narrator can report a character's thoughts in <Term id="free-indirect-discourse"/>, in the character's own voice and without a tag such as "she thought", as in "Of course the train was late." Other techniques go deeper into the mind. <Term id="interior-monologue"/> shows private thoughts in a fairly ordered form, while <Term id="stream-of-consciousness"/> presents a continuous flow of sensations and images. An <Term id="unreliable-narrator"/> is one whose version the reader cannot fully trust.

Characterisation shows what a character is like. <Term id="direct-characterisation"/> states the qualities openly, as in "She was brave." <Term id="indirect-characterisation"/> reveals them through speech, action, thought and the reactions of others, so the reader has to infer them.

Structure is the shape of the story. The <Term id="exposition"/> gives background at the start. The plot then builds to a climax and a denouement, and a flashback moves back in time. The <Term id="setting"/> is the time and place, and it can mirror or contrast a character's mood. When nature seems to share a character's feelings, the device is <Term id="pathetic-fallacy"/>, as in "The sky wept as she walked." A bright, indifferent landscape that sharpens grief by contrast is not pathetic fallacy.

<Callout kind="define">Focalisation is the lens: the character whose perceptions the reader follows.</Callout>

## Worked example

<Question id="p1-chopin-hour"/>

The guiding question asks how Chopin uses the contrast between the news and the spring outside to prepare the reader for Mrs. Mallard's reaction. Start with the narration, because it decides what the reader learns and when.

<Steps>
<Step>**Identify the narration.** The story is in the third person: the narrator calls her "Mrs. Mallard" and "she". The opening is almost <Term id="omniscient-narrator"/>, because the narrator knows what Mrs. Mallard never witnessed: "It was he who had been in the newspaper office when intelligence of the railroad disaster was received".</Step>
<Step>**Trace the focalisation.** From "She did not hear the story as many women have heard the same" the lens narrows to Mrs. Mallard, and the square outside is seen through her eyes: "She could see in the open square before her house the tops of trees that were all aquiver with the new spring life." This is <Term id="focalisation"/>: the reader follows what she perceives.</Step>
<Step>**Ask what the reader learns, and what is withheld.** The reader learns how the news was confirmed, through Richards's second telegram, and Mrs. Mallard says nothing about it. Her feelings are not named directly. After her tears, the narrator reads her from outside: "But now there was a dull stare in her eyes", and "It was not a glance of reflection, but rather indicated a suspension of intelligent thought." The reader sees blankness, reported from outside.</Step>
<Step>**Test the contrast.** The spring outside, "the new spring life" and "The delicious breath of rain was in the air", does not share her grief; it opposes it. That sharpens the shock. It is not <Term id="pathetic-fallacy"/>, where nature mirrors a feeling.</Step>
<Step>**Set the characterisation against the action.** The narrator states her qualities directly: "She was young, with a fair, calm face, whose lines bespoke repression and even a certain strength." Her body then tells another story: "a sob came up into her throat and shook her, as a child who has cried itself to sleep continues to sob in its dreams." The reader meets <Term id="direct-characterisation"/> and <Term id="indirect-characterisation"/> side by side.</Step>
</Steps>

Chopin begins with a narrator who knows more than Mrs. Mallard, then narrows the lens to her. The reader learns the news with the narrator's authority, while the spring outside is seen through her senses. The contrast between the open window and her dull stare prepares the reader for her response: the world is full of life, and she cannot yet feel any of it. The narrator's restraint, reporting her stare from outside, keeps the reader waiting for what she will feel.

## How it is examined

Paper 1 asks how the narration shapes what the reader knows, feels or judges. Criterion A checks that you understand the situation and who is speaking. Criterion B rewards named narrative techniques, their effects, and a judgement of how far the narrator can be trusted. Criterion C checks that every paragraph answers the guiding question. Criterion D rewards precise terms such as focalisation and free indirect discourse, used accurately. The command terms you will meet most often are Analyse, Explain and Evaluate.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Naming the narration without saying what it lets the reader know is the most common loss of marks in prose.</Callout>

- Calling a narrator "first person" because a character speaks in quotation marks. First-person narration means the narrator is the "I" who tells the story.
- Calling every third-person story "omniscient". Check whether the narrator enters only one mind.
- Confusing free indirect discourse with direct speech in quotation marks.
- Calling every description of nature pathetic fallacy. The device gives nature a character's feelings; a landscape that only contrasts with a character is not pathetic fallacy.
- Giving a view on the narrator's reliability without evidence from the text.

## Summary

- Narration: who tells the story, and from what position (first or third person).
- Focalisation: the character whose eyes and mind the reader follows.
- An omniscient narrator knows all minds and events, and may comment.
- Free indirect discourse: a character's thought in the narrator's voice, with no tag such as "she thought".
- Direct characterisation states qualities; indirect characterisation shows them through action, speech and thought.
- Exposition gives background; a flashback goes back in time; the setting gives place and time.
- Pathetic fallacy gives nature a character's feelings; a contrast with the character is not pathetic fallacy.
