## Big idea

Culture is the set of shared values, customs and rituals that tells a group who belongs and how its members should behave. A writer shows culture most clearly through what people do without thinking about it, and through what a stranger finds strange.

## Explanation

Culture is one of the key concepts of the course. It covers the shared values, customs and rituals of a group, and also its language, its class structure and its religion or beliefs. Culture tells people what matters, what is polite, what is shameful and who may speak. Members learn it rather than invent it, so it is most visible when someone breaks its rules.

<Callout kind="define">Culture is the set of shared values, customs, rituals and beliefs that a group learns, uses and passes on.</Callout>

Writers make culture visible through details that a group takes for granted. A ritual is a fixed sequence of acts. Food shows who eats first and what is refused. Rules of behaviour show how to greet, when to be silent and who may interrupt. Speech shows class and region through words and accents. The setting, such as a house, a market or a courtyard, carries its own rules. Objects can hold a whole set of values at once.

A clash between cultures is often shown by <Term id="juxtaposition"/>: two sets of rules are placed side by side, so that the reader sees how each looks right to its own members and wrong to the other. The writer does not need to say which culture is better, because the contrast does the work.

Do not turn the details of a text into a claim about real people. A ritual in a story is evidence about that fictional group. It is not a rule for everyone who shares a language, a country or a religion. Examiners reward a precise detail that the text supplies, not a generalisation.

A <Term id="setting"/> is never neutral in a cultural text: the same room can mean hospitality to one group and suspicion to another. A <Term id="symbol"/> can carry a value across the whole text, so its meaning grows as the reader learns the rules that surround it.

Speech shows culture in a precise way. The <Term id="vernacular"/>, the everyday spoken language of a group, shows class, region and education. A writer who gives one character a formal voice and another a local voice shows which language the culture rewards and which it looks down on.

In drama, a <Term id="chorus"/> speaks for the community and states its values aloud. In prose and poetry a similar effect comes from a narrator who speaks for "we", so that the reader hears what the group expects of its members.

## Worked example

The ritual below is invented. It belongs to no real people, and the text names no real culture.

Every autumn, before the first frost, the family in this story sets one bowl of grain on the doorstep. No one eats from it until the youngest child has spoken the name of everyone who left the house that year. The bowl stays out all night, and anyone who quarrelled with a relative must step over it without touching the rim. The grain is not for eating. It is there so that the household can see who still belongs.

<Steps>
<Step>**Read the value.** The grain is not food. It counts belonging, and the value is that the group keeps its members in view, even those who have gone.</Step>
<Step>**Name the first technique.** The bowl of grain is a <Term id="symbol"/>: one object carries the whole value of continuity. It shows that this culture does not measure belonging by presence alone.</Step>
<Step>**Name the second technique.** The naming and the stepping over repeat each year, so they work as a <Term id="motif"/>. The repetition shows that reconciliation is a duty the culture requires, not a choice an individual makes.</Step>
<Step>**Link to culture.** Together the symbol and the motif show a culture in which belonging must be performed each year. The example concerns one fictional family and makes no claim about any real group.</Step>
</Steps>

## Visual

| What the writer shows | How it is shown | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| A shared value | A rule that everyone follows, such as a silence or a gift | The group decides what matters |
| A custom | A meal, a greeting, or the way someone stands at a door | The group repeats what it has always done |
| A ritual | A fixed time and a fixed sequence of words or actions | Belonging is performed, not assumed |
| Class | Who sits where, who serves, and whose speech is corrected | Culture decides whose voice counts |
| A clash of cultures | Two sets of rules placed side by side (juxtaposition) | Each set looks right to its own members |
| A symbol of belief | An object that an outsider reads differently | Meaning depends on the group that reads it |

## How it is examined

Culture can be used in both papers. In Paper 1 it helps explain the values in an extract. In Paper 2 it gives a shared question for two works, so the concept supports a comparison. Examiners reward a precise cultural detail, such as a ritual, a rule or an object, linked to a value and to a technique. They do not reward a general statement about a people or a country, because a text shows a fictional group, not every real one.

<Callout kind="exam">A culture paragraph earns credit when it names one specific detail, the value it carries, and the technique that shows it.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">A list of exotic details, such as food or costume, is not cultural analysis until it says what the details mean to the group.</Callout>

- Turning the details of a text into a claim about a real people or country.
- Treating culture as if it were the same thing as nationality.
- Naming a technique, such as a symbol, without saying which value it carries.
- Describing a clash between two cultures as a story of good and bad, rather than as two sets of rules.

## Summary

- Culture is the shared values, customs, rituals, language, class and religion of a group.
- Writers show culture through ritual, food, rules of behaviour, speech, setting and symbols.
- Juxtaposition shows a clash between cultures without a judgement from the narrator.
- A symbol carries a value, and a motif repeats it until it becomes a rule.
- The vernacular and the chorus show whose voice a culture allows to speak.
- Evidence is about the text's fictional group; never generalise about real peoples.
