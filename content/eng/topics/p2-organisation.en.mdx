## Big idea

Criterion C concerns focus, balance and coherent organisation. A useful plan gives each paragraph a clear role in the argument; it does not require an identical sentence pattern in every paragraph.

## Explanation

The school booklet allocates 5 of 25 Paper 2 marks to criterion C. Introduce the works and your interpretation of the question early. A thesis may take more than one sentence when the thought needs it. A short relevant account of an episode can establish evidence; a long unrelated plot summary usually delays the analysis.

One useful paragraph pattern is claim, evidence, analysis, comparison and significance for the question. The order can change. A close reading may begin with a detail, then develop the claim. One comparative point may take more than one paragraph. If you organise by work, make explicit connections as you develop the second discussion and keep the treatment balanced.

A precise episode reference can support the analysis without a quotation or memorised page number. If you quote, preserve the wording and explain its relevance. Linking words help only when the ideas themselves connect.

Order the argument logically. You may begin with the strongest claim, build towards a more complex one, or follow a structural movement in the works. “Strongest point last” is a strategy, not a criterion. Conclude by drawing together what the comparison establishes about the question. Synthesis may summarise important findings; it need not introduce a wholly new insight or finish with a rhetorical question.

<Callout kind="define">An argument is a connected case supported by evidence and reasoning. The labels in a planning pattern help you see its parts; they do not earn marks by themselves.</Callout>

## Worked example

Invented prompt: *Compare how two works use a narrator who withholds information. To what extent does withholding affect the reader's trust?*

All details below are invented. In Work A, a retrospective narrator says that they once concealed a letter to protect a sibling, then admits that this harmed someone else. In Work B, a narrator claims never to have read a letter, but later reproduces a detail known only to its reader.

<Steps>
<Step>**Provisional claim.** Withholding can have different effects: later acknowledgement of a harmful choice may invite qualified trust, while an unexplained contradiction may undermine it.</Step>
<Step>**Evidence and comparison.** A's admission does not erase the harm, but allows the reader to assess the motive and its consequences. In B, the contradiction creates a gap between the claim of ignorance and the knowledge displayed.</Step>
<Step>**Qualification.** Neither an altruistic motive nor a contradiction mechanically determines every reader's reaction. A could still be self-justifying; B's contradiction could be explained by another source of knowledge if the text supplies one. Test the reading against further details.</Step>
</Steps>

Possible paragraph: Both narrators control access to a letter, but the timing and consistency of their disclosures offer different grounds for trust. A eventually acknowledges the harm caused by the concealment, allowing the reader to test a protective motive against its cost. B instead claims ignorance while reproducing private information. Unless the work supplies another explanation for that knowledge, the contradiction invites suspicion. Thus withholding matters through the way later disclosure revises the earlier account, not merely through the amount of information omitted.

## Visual

| Part | Useful job | Flexible choice |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Establish the works, question and direction of argument | One or several thesis sentences |
| Development | Support and connect claims about both works | Point-by-point or explicitly connected blocks |
| Transitions | Show how the next idea changes or extends the previous one | Wording suited to the actual relationship |
| Conclusion | Synthesize the comparative answer | Summarise findings without merely repeating sentences |

## How it is examined

The school descriptor assesses sustained focus, balanced treatment and the logical, convincing connection of ideas across the response. It does not prescribe five paragraphs, one quotation per paragraph, a final linking sentence or one particular order of comparisons.

## Common mistakes

- Two plot summaries with no developed comparison.
- A memorised introduction that does not address the selected question.
- Linking unrelated assertions with “therefore” or “similarly”.
- Treating a layout formula as a substitute for evidence and reasoning.

## Summary

- Give each part a purpose in the argument.
- Connect precise references, analysis and relevant comparison.
- Choose a logical order; revise it if your reasoning changes.
- Use the conclusion to draw the answer together.
