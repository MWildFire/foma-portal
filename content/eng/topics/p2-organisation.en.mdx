## Big idea

Criterion C rewards an essay that moves in clear steps: an introduction that names both works and states a thesis, paragraphs that each make one comparison linked to the prompt, and a conclusion that answers the prompt instead of repeating it.

## Explanation

Criterion C, focus and organisation, is worth 5 of the 30 marks in Paper 2. The examiner asks two things: does every part of the essay serve the prompt, and can the argument be followed without effort? A strong essay has three parts: an introduction, body paragraphs and a conclusion.

The introduction is short. Name both works and their authors, then state one thesis sentence that answers the prompt: what the comparison shows, not what the works are about. If the prompt concerns the writer's aim, name each writer's <Term id="purpose"/> in one clause. Do not give a plot summary.

Each body paragraph follows the same pattern: point, comparison, evidence, so what, link to the prompt. The point is one claim that answers part of the prompt. The comparison sets the two works side by side. The evidence is one short, exact quotation or a precise detail, with its technique. The so what explains the effect. The link returns to the prompt in the final sentence. Each paragraph is a small <Term id="argument"/> with its own claim and its own proof. Put the point in the first sentence, so that the examiner finds the claim at once.

Use signposting words to show the order of the argument: similarly, by contrast, a further difference, this matters because. They guide the examiner from one step to the next. Order the paragraphs so that the strongest comparison comes last, the way a <Term id="climax"/> gathers the force of a scene.

<Callout kind="define">A thesis is one sentence that answers the prompt and states what the comparison reveals about the works.</Callout>

The conclusion answers the prompt in new words. It does not repeat the introduction, and it does not summarise the paragraphs. Ask one question: what does my comparison prove that the examiner did not know at the start? A conclusion may end with a <Term id="rhetorical-question"/>, but only if the essay has already answered it.

## Worked example

The prompt below is invented for this example. It names no real work, and the model paragraph describes two invented works.

**Prompt:** Compare how two works present a narrator who withholds information from the reader. To what extent does the withholding change the reader's trust?

<Steps>
<Step>**Read the key words.** "Withholds", "trust" and "to what extent" show that the essay must judge an effect, not only describe the silence.</Step>
<Step>**Write the thesis.** "In both works the narrator's silence is a form of control, but it earns the reader's trust in one work and destroys it in the other."</Step>
<Step>**Build the paragraph from its roles.** Point, comparison, evidence, so what and link, in that order, as the model below shows.</Step>
</Steps>

**Model paragraph** (the roles are marked in bold):

**Point:** Silence earns trust when it is loyal, and loses it when it is fear. **Comparison:** In the first work, the narrator says nothing about the letter to protect the family, so the reader trusts the silence and feels its cost. In the second, the narrator says nothing about the letter because he has not admitted to himself what it contains, and the reader stops trusting him. **Evidence:** the letter lies unopened on the table for the whole first chapter; in the essay this would be a short quotation with its page number. **So what:** The contrast shows that the narrator's motive, not the amount he hides, decides how far the reader trusts him. **Link:** The prompt asks about trust, and the motive is exactly what the reader must judge.

The point is an <Term id="antithesis"/>: two opposite states, loyal silence and fearful silence, set side by side in one sentence. The paragraph never stays with one work for long, and every sentence has a role.

## Visual

| Paragraph | Job | What it must not do |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Name both works and state one thesis that answers the prompt | Give a plot summary or list what the essay will do |
| Body paragraph 1 | Make the first comparison: point, evidence, so what, link | Describe one work and then the other without comparing them |
| Body paragraph 2 | Make the second comparison, often a difference in technique | Repeat the first paragraph with new examples |
| Body paragraph 3 | Make the strongest comparison, the one the essay builds toward | Introduce an idea that the thesis does not cover |
| Conclusion | Answer the prompt in new words, from the strongest point | Summarise the paragraphs or add new evidence |

## How it is examined

Paper 2 is worth 30 marks, and criterion C, focus and organisation, is worth 5 of them. Examiners read the essay as an argument. They check whether the introduction names both works and states a thesis, whether each paragraph makes one clear comparison, and whether the conclusion answers the prompt. Most prompts ask you to compare, discuss or evaluate, so every paragraph must make a judgement about the prompt, not list details.

<Callout kind="exam">Criterion C rewards an essay in which every paragraph has a clear point and a link to the prompt.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">A conclusion that repeats the paragraphs gives the examiner nothing new.</Callout>

- Opening with a plot summary of each work instead of a thesis.
- Writing one paragraph about the first work and one about the second, with no comparison between them.
- Using "also" and "furthermore" to join points that have no clear order, so the examiner cannot follow the argument.
- Ending the essay with a rhetorical question that the essay has not answered.
