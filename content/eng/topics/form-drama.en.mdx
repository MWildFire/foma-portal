## Big idea

A play has no narrator, so its meaning reaches us through speech, stage directions and the gap between what characters say and what they mean. Analyse the words and the staging together.

## Explanation

A play is written to be performed, so its evidence sits in two places: the dialogue and the <Term id="stage-directions"/>, the playwright's notes on action, movement and tone. Read both. Stage directions show what a character does while speaking, and often what the character does not say.

Not all speech is shared. An <Term id="aside"/> is a short line spoken to the audience, which the other characters on stage are conventionally taken not to hear. A <Term id="soliloquy"/> is a longer speech made alone, in which a character voices private thoughts. The meaning beneath the words is the <Term id="subtext"/>. When the audience knows something a character does not, the scene has <Term id="dramatic-irony"/>.

Staging is part of the meaning too. The <Term id="mise-en-scene"/> is everything visible on stage at one moment: the set, the lights, the costumes and the position of the actors. In Greek tragedy, a <Term id="chorus"/> comments on the action and speaks for the community.

Genre sets the audience's expectations. A <Term id="tragedy"/> is a serious play in which a central figure falls, usually through a flaw or an error. Comedy usually ends happily. A <Term id="comedy-of-manners"/> satirises polite society through wit and intrigue, while a <Term id="farce"/> relies on exaggerated situations and physical action. Theatre of the absurd, which emerged in the mid-twentieth century, presents life as meaningless, with illogical dialogue and circular action.

The audience is part of the design. <Term id="catharsis"/> is the emotional release that a tragedy gives its audience through pity and fear. The <Term id="fourth-wall"/> is the imaginary wall between actors and audience. When a character breaks it and speaks to the audience, the illusion is disturbed on purpose.

Structure drives the action. A <Term id="reversal"/> is a sudden change of fortune, often triggered by a discovery. <Term id="stichomythia"/> is a fast exchange of short, alternating lines, typically in an argument. When a problem is solved by an unlikely outside intervention, the device is deus ex machina, from Greek theatre, where a god was lowered onto the stage. It is often criticised as a weak solution.

<Callout kind="define">Subtext is what a character means but does not say.</Callout>

## Worked example

<Question id="p1-ibsen-doll"/>

The guiding question asks how the dialogue and the stage directions show why Nora and Helmer cannot understand each other. Read the directions first, then the lines they frame.

<Steps>
<Step>**Start with the stage directions.** Two are in square brackets. "[After a short silence.]" comes before Nora asks "Does not one thing strike you as we sit here?", so the reader sees that she is choosing her moment. "[Shaking her head.]" comes with the line "You have never loved me", so her denial is physical as well as spoken.</Step>
<Step>**Compare what is said with what is heard.** Nora says "We have been married eight years." Helmer replies "Seriously! What do you call seriously?" He hears a word, not the complaint. Both use "seriously" with different meanings: for him it means "cares", for her it means getting "to the bottom of anything". That gap is the <Term id="subtext"/>: the two are not talking about the same thing.</Step>
<Step>**Find the irony.** Helmer asks, "By us, who have loved you more than all the world?" The audience has already heard Nora say "I have had great injustice done me, Torvald". He is certain of his love, and the audience knows it is not shared. The line therefore carries <Term id="dramatic-irony"/>.</Step>
<Step>**Link the evidence to the question.** The stage directions and the quick exchange of short lines show two people using the same words for different things. Neither hears the other's meaning, which is why they cannot understand each other.</Step>
</Steps>

Ibsen builds the failure to understand into the scene itself. The silence before Nora's question makes the audience wait, while Helmer's replies move quickly past her. One word, "serious", carries two meanings, and the subtext is her complaint that he has never looked at her as a person. Helmer's certainty, "who have loved you more than all the world", is set against Nora's denial, so the audience sees the gap that the characters cannot.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Quoting dialogue without saying what the words leave unsaid is the most common loss of marks in drama.</Callout>

- Treating stage directions as decoration. Each one is evidence for an interpretation.
- Calling every short speech to the audience a soliloquy. An aside is a short line; a soliloquy is a longer speech made alone.
- Naming dramatic irony without saying what the audience knows that the character does not.
- Naming a genre from the ending alone. Check the tone, the characters and the purpose.

## Summary

- Stage directions are evidence: they show action, pauses and tone.
- Subtext is what a character means but does not say.
- An aside is a short line to the audience; a soliloquy is a longer speech made alone.
- Dramatic irony means the audience knows more than a character does.
- Tragedy, comedy of manners and farce set the expectations of a play's genre.
- Catharsis and the fourth wall describe how the audience is involved in the play.
- Reversal and stichomythia shape the action and its tension.
