## Big idea

Drama communicates through speech, action, staging and sometimes a narrator. For example, the Chorus in Shakespeare’s Henry V addresses the audience and frames the action. Do not define drama by the absence of narration. Analyse the words and the staging together.

## Explanation

A play is written to be performed, so its evidence sits in two places: the dialogue and the <Term id="stage-directions"/>, the playwright's notes on action, movement and tone. Read both. Stage directions show what a character does while speaking, and often what the character does not say.

Not all speech is shared. An <Term id="aside"/> is conventionally unheard by some other characters and may address the audience or a particular person. A <Term id="soliloquy"/> voices thoughts as self-directed speech, often while alone; length alone does not distinguish them. The meaning beneath the words is the <Term id="subtext"/>. When the audience knows something a character does not, the scene has <Term id="dramatic-irony"/>.

Staging is part of the meaning too. The <Term id="mise-en-scene"/> is everything visible on stage at one moment: the set, the lights, the costumes and the position of the actors. In Greek tragedy, a <Term id="chorus"/> comments on the action and may voice a community’s concerns; its position is not necessarily that of every citizen.

Genre sets the audience's expectations. A <Term id="tragedy"/> is a serious dramatic genre concerned with suffering and destructive conflict. A protagonist’s error can contribute to catastrophe, but a fixed personal “tragic flaw” is not a universal genre requirement. Aristotle’s account of an effective tragic plot is a particular theory, not a rule for every tragedy. Comedy usually ends happily. A <Term id="comedy-of-manners"/> satirises polite society through wit and intrigue, while a <Term id="farce"/> relies on exaggerated situations and physical action. Theatre of the absurd, which emerged in the mid-twentieth century, often stages uncertainty about meaning, failed communication or repetitive action through disrupted conventional plots. Do not reduce every play in this grouping to one authorial declaration that life is meaningless.

The audience is part of the design. <Term id="catharsis"/> names the effect associated with pity and fear in Aristotle’s account of tragedy, often explained as purification or emotional release. Treat this as a theoretical account, not a guaranteed reaction in every audience. The <Term id="fourth-wall"/> is the imaginary wall between actors and audience. When a character breaks it and speaks to the audience, the illusion is disturbed on purpose.

Structure drives the action. A <Term id="reversal"/> is a sudden change of fortune, often triggered by a discovery. <Term id="stichomythia"/> strictly refers to alternating verse-line exchanges, with related half-line and two-line forms; the term is also used more broadly for clipped alternating dialogue. Identify that broader use if the passage is prose. When a problem is solved by an unlikely outside intervention, the device is deus ex machina, from Greek theatre, where stage machinery could introduce a god. It is often criticised as a weak solution.

<Callout kind="define">Subtext is an implied meaning, tension or feeling beneath the explicit speech; it need not be a conscious intention of the speaker.</Callout>

## Worked example

<Question id="p1-ibsen-doll"/>

The guiding question asks how the dialogue and the stage directions show why Nora and Helmer cannot understand each other. Read the directions first, then the lines they frame.

<Steps>
<Step>**Start with the stage directions.** Two are in square brackets. "[After a short silence.]" comes before Nora asks "Does not one thing strike you as we sit here?", so the reader sees that she is choosing her moment. "[Shaking her head.]" comes with the line "You have never loved me", so her denial is physical as well as spoken.</Step>
<Step>**Compare what is said with what is heard.** Nora says "We have been married eight years." Helmer replies "Seriously! What do you call seriously?" He hears a word, not the complaint. Both use "seriously" with different meanings: for him it means "cares", for her it means getting "to the bottom of anything". That gap is the <Term id="subtext"/>: the two are not talking about the same thing.</Step>
<Step>**Distinguish disagreement from dramatic irony.** Helmer’s "By us, who have loved you more than all the world?" conflicts with Nora’s account of injustice. Helmer hears that accusation too. This contrast alone is not an audience-only knowledge gap and need not be labelled <Term id="dramatic-irony"/>. Analyse his defensive assumption of affection and her rejection of it.</Step>
<Step>**Link the evidence to the question.** The stage directions and the quick exchange of short lines show two people using the same words for different things. Helmer initially reduces her complaint to practical cares, while Nora explicitly challenges his understanding; the exchange exposes incompatible views of the marriage.</Step>
</Steps>

Ibsen builds the failure to understand into the scene itself. The silence before Nora's question makes the audience wait, while Helmer's replies move quickly past her. One word, "serious", carries two meanings, and the subtext is her complaint that he has never looked at her as a person. Helmer's certainty, "who have loved you more than all the world", is set against Nora's denial, so the audience can assess their conflicting assumptions; Nora is explicitly identifying the gap, not remaining unaware of it.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">When implied meaning matters to the guiding question, support your interpretation with the words, actions or stage directions; do not invent a hidden motive.</Callout>

- Treating stage directions as decoration. Each one is evidence for an interpretation.
- Calling every short speech to the audience a soliloquy. Check the addressee and theatrical convention: some characters may not hear an aside, while a soliloquy presents thoughts as self-directed speech. Length alone is insufficient.
- Naming dramatic irony without saying what the audience knows that the character does not.
- Naming a genre from the ending alone. Check the tone, the characters and the purpose.

## Summary

- Stage directions are evidence: they show action, pauses and tone.
- Subtext is an implied meaning, tension or feeling beneath the explicit speech; it need not be a conscious intention of the speaker.
- Check the addressee and who can hear an aside or soliloquy; length alone is insufficient.
- Dramatic irony means the audience knows more than a character does.
- Tragedy, comedy of manners and farce set the expectations of a play's genre.
- Catharsis and the fourth wall describe how the audience is involved in the play.
- Reversal and stichomythia shape the action and its tension.
