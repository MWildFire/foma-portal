## Big idea

Compare Yun’s collection with *Much Ado About Nothing* through the likely three: **The Road**, **Self-Portrait**, **The Liver**. The exact selection remains unconfirmed. A poem used in your Individual Oral (IO) is not eligible; a different poem from the same collection is not automatically a safe substitute. Confirm the whole work’s eligibility.

## Explanation

K = Korean original line; C = explicit line in the class English anthology, not a screen wrap. The [poems topic](#/s/eng/t/w-yun-dong-ju) explains the numbering and gives our literal translation. The class Self-Portrait uses “he”; do not build an argument on an English first-person pronoun that is not there. The collection is **one work** for this comparison.

The lyric uses repeated observation, images and address. Drama distributes knowledge among speakers, listeners and the audience. That difference must be demonstrated in a scene, not treated as a universal rule that poems conceal everything or plays reveal everything. Locators below use Folger: [2.3](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/much-ado-about-nothing/read/2/3/), [4.1](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/much-ado-about-nothing/read/4/1/) and [5.4](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/much-ado-about-nothing/read/5/4/).

## Worked example

Four original Paper 2-style questions, not past-paper copies. Adapt the argument to the exact question; these are planning models, not finished essays to submit. Each numbered paragraph below compares both works.

### 1. How do two writers make self-judgement different from public judgement?

**Thesis.** Both works make identity depend on judgement, but Yun makes its instability visible through a divided self, while Shakespeare exposes the social power of accusation.

**Paragraph 1.** Compare Self-Portrait K3–5 → C6–9, where the observer hates and pities an unchanged man, with Claudio attacking Hero in 4.1.25–33. Repeated looking and the accusatory fruit image each make a person an object of judgement; one judgement changes inwardly, the other injures publicly.

**Paragraph 2.** Compare The Road K11–14 → C11–14 with Hero defending her name in 4.1.84–91. Sky and wall externalise the speaker’s shame; dialogue lets Hero resist the identity imposed on her. Do not equate her innocence with a confession of guilt in the poem.

**Paragraph 3.** Compare The Liver K5–10 → C5–11 with Benedick choosing to challenge Claudio in 4.1.343–350. Apostrophe separates accepted pain from rejected temptation; dialogue separates friendship from an obligation to defend Hero. Both complicate passive submission to judgement.

**Conclusion.** Judgement shapes identity in both, but an unsettled lyric conscience and a public conflict with practical consequences are not equivalent.

### 2. Compare how repeated acts of looking or listening change a person’s beliefs.

**Thesis.** Both writers stage interpretation as an active process; repetition can deepen uncertainty in Yun and manufacture confidence in Shakespeare.

**Paragraph 1.** Self-Portrait K4–7 → C7–11 repeats departure and return; Benedick’s response to overhearing in 2.3.223–230 turns reported feeling into apparent certainty. Contrast a cyclic lyric pattern with dramatic irony: the audience knows the conversation was staged.

**Paragraph 2.** The Road K9–12 → C9–12 repeats morning and evening before a look at the sky; Hero’s denial meets the accusers’ claim to have seen and heard her in 4.1.91–95. In both, sensory experience needs interpretation; a confident witness is not automatically reliable.

**Paragraph 3.** Self-Portrait K8 → C12–14 returns to the scene with a memory simile; the Friar predicts that an altered story will change Claudio’s memory in 4.1.225–227. Compare the poem’s unresolved image with the play’s deliberate management of pity.

**Conclusion.** Looking again does not guarantee truth: the lyric leaves judgement mobile, while the play reveals how belief can be organised by others.

### 3. How do writers distinguish an ethical commitment from mere compliance?

**Thesis.** Both works test loyalty by its cost, yet Yun’s symbolic commands remain unresolved while Shakespeare brings loyalty into a dispute between people.

**Paragraph 1.** The Liver K4–8 → C4–8 both guards the organ and invites the eagle to eat. Benedick’s initial refusal of Beatrice’s demand in 4.1.302–305 similarly exposes conflicting loyalties. Paradox and interrupted dialogue prevent an effortless moral choice.

**Paragraph 2.** The Liver K9–10 → C9–11 refuses the turtle; Beatrice challenges masculine honour in 4.1.315–338. Direct address in both targets an authority, but the poem uses mythic figures while the play exposes a social restriction on action.

**Paragraph 3.** The Road K15–16 → C15–16 makes continued searching a reason to live; Benedick’s promise in 4.1.346–350 gives loyalty a practical test. Alternatively use the eligible spare Prologue K7–8 → C8–9. Compare commitment expressed as continuing movement with commitment expressed as a challenge.

**Conclusion.** Ethical agency involves choosing which claim to accept, not praising suffering by itself. The play’s demand for violence also deserves scrutiny.

### 4. Compare how barriers between people or selves shape a work’s ending.

**Thesis.** Yun’s poems leave division visible at the end; Shakespeare offers restoration while allowing the audience to question what that restoration costs.

**Paragraph 1.** The Road K7–8 and K13–16 → C7–8 and C13–16 never opens the gate; Hero’s exclusion in 4.1.25–33 creates a social barrier rather than a literal wall. Compare the sustained spatial metaphor with public rejection that drives the plot.

**Paragraph 2.** Self-Portrait K7–8 → C11–14 adds longing and memory without merging observer and image; Hero’s return in 5.4.65–68 restores a visible person but recalls the survival of slander. A returned figure need not erase the earlier wound.

**Paragraph 3.** The Liver K11–13 → C12–15 ends with sinking and repeated Prometheus; the Friar’s proposed apparent death in 4.1.225–250 aims to change judgement. Contrast mythic suffering without narrated rescue with a dramatic device designed to enable social repair.

**Conclusion.** The forms create different kinds of closure: lyric recurrence preserves a question, while theatrical recognition offers an outcome that can still be evaluated critically.

## How it is examined

**105 minutes: 10 / 80 / 10 / 5.** Ten to choose the question, check eligibility and plan; eighty to write; ten to check thesis, evidence and conclusion; five spare. Follow the typing practice advice in the general P2 guide.

School mock criteria: **A, B1, B2, C, D — 5 each, total 25**, as in the school materials. A needs an answer to the question and accurate knowledge; B1 analyses choices and effects; B2 develops comparisons; C organises a sustained argument; D requires precise expression. Give a location for every example. A conclusion should weigh the difference your paragraphs have established.

## Common mistakes

- Replacing comparison with two separate summaries. Each paragraph needs both works.
- Calling the three poems three separate P2 works, or ignoring the IO restriction.
- Treating national identity as the only possible meaning of every Korean image.
- Confusing source K lines with class C lines; our literal translation is not the class translation.
- Claiming a lyric speaker is always the author, or that a narrator’s interpretation is a verified fact.
