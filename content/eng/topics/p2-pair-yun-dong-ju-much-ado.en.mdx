## Big idea

Yun Dong-ju's poems and Shakespeare's *Much Ado About Nothing* both ask what happens when a person's sense of self meets the judgement of others. In the poems the judging voice is often the speaker's own. In the play it belongs to a community: the Prince, Hero's father, a villain and the wedding guests. The pair works well for Paper 2 because the forms differ. A lyric poem has one "I" and no stage directions, so the reader knows only what the speaker reports. A play shows its plot through dialogue, stage directions and overheard talk, so the audience knows more than any one character. The strongest answers name the technique of each form and say what it lets the reader or the audience know.

## Explanation

Yun Dong-ju (1917–1945) was a Korean poet. He died in a Fukuoka prison in February 1945, aged 27, and the cause of his death is not established. The five poems on this page come from his collection *Sky, Wind, Star and Poem* (1948). If your class studies other poems, the same techniques apply. Four of the five are in the class anthology file; *Confession* is not. This page uses only our own literal translations, never the class translation.

**Names and identity.** In *Counting the Stars at Night* (별 헤는 밤) the speaker names the stars one by one and speaks to his absent mother: "어머님, 나는 별 하나에 아름다운 말 한마디씩 불러 봅니다." (stanza 4, line 1; our literal translation: "Mother, I call each star by a beautiful word."). This is an apostrophe <Term id="apostrophe"/>: he addresses someone who cannot answer. Later he writes his name on a hill and covers it with earth, "내 이름자를 써 보고 / 흙으로 덮어 버리었습니다" (stanza 7, lines 3–4; our literal translation: "I write my name on the hill where the starlight falls and cover it with earth."). The insects that weep all night are grieving over a "shameful name" (stanza 8, lines 1–2). In *Much Ado* a name is also at the centre of a scene: "Is it not Hero? Who can blot that name / With any just reproach?" (4.1.84–85), and Claudio answers, "Hero itself can blot out Hero's virtue" (4.1.86–87).

**Shame and reputation.** The *Prologue* (서시) opens with a wish: "한점 부끄럼이 없기를," (stanza 1, line 2; our literal translation: "let there be not one speck of shame"). Claudio's charge against Hero starts with appearance: "Give not this rotten orange to your friend" (4.1.32), then "She's but the sign and semblance of her honor" (4.1.33). In *Confession* (참회록) the speaker looks into a bronze mirror and asks why his face is so shameful: "어느 王朝의 遺物이기에 / 이다지도 욕될가" (stanza 1, lines 3–4; our literal translation: "As if it were a relic of some dynasty, why is it so shameful?"). Both works tie shame to a surface: an orange or a mirror.

**The self watched from outside.** In *Self-Portrait* (자화상) the speaker goes alone to a well and finds "그리고 한 사나이가 있습니다" (stanza 2, line 1; our literal translation: "And there is a man."). He hates that man, then pities him, then finds him dear: "어쩐지 그 사나이가 미워져 돌아갑니다" (stanza 2, line 2; "Somehow I come to hate that man, and I turn back."), "돌아가다 생각하니 그 사나이가 가엾어집니다" (stanza 3, line 1; "Going back, I think of him, and that man becomes pitiable to me."), and "돌아가다 생각하니 그 사나이가 그리워집니다" (stanza 4, line 2; "Going back, I think, and that man becomes dear to me."). The play also turns a character's feeling, but through overheard talk. Benedick says "This can be no trick" (2.3.223) and then believes the plot: "Love me? Why, it must be requited!" (2.3.226–227).

**Form.** The lyric speaks in one voice and has no stage directions. Its meaning comes through the lyric persona <Term id="lyric-persona"/> and imagery <Term id="imagery"/>: the well, the moon and the stars, and a line that returns, "I turn back" (stanza 2, line 2; stanza 4, line 1). The play shows the same tension through stage directions <Term id="stage-directions"/> and overheard talk, so the audience knows what the characters do not, which is dramatic irony <Term id="dramatic-irony"/>.

<Callout kind="define">A lyric persona is the speaker of a poem, the voice that says "I". It is not the poet, so the "I" of a poem should not be read as Yun Dong-ju's own biography.</Callout>

## Worked example

Six plans follow, one for each of six bank questions. Each gives the thesis, three comparative points with their locations in both works, and the point that earns B2. Use them as a checklist, not as a script to memorise.

**Bank question p2-q02 (Discuss): identity against the community's expectations.** Thesis: in both works a judgement turns identity into a test, but the poems keep that test inside one voice. Point 1, names: Hero's name is defended and then attacked (4.1.84–87), while the speaker of *Counting the Stars at Night* calls his own name shameful (stanza 8, lines 1–2) and buries it (stanza 7, lines 3–4). Point 2, judgement: Claudio's public rejection at the altar (4.1.25–32) against the speaker's private judgement of his face in *Confession* (stanza 1, lines 3–4). Point 3, cost: the slander outlives Hero's apparent death (5.4.68), against the speaker's wish for "not one speck of shame" in the *Prologue* (stanza 1, line 2). Form: the play shows judgement in public speech, the lyric in one "I" and its images. B2: each point sets a public judgement in the play beside a private one in the poem, and names the form that carries it.

**Bank question p2-q06 (Discuss): transformation, sudden or gradual.** Thesis: the play makes turns sudden, prepared by overhearing; the poems make change gradual through repetition. Point 1: Benedick turns within one scene, from "This can be no trick" (2.3.223) to "Love me? Why, it must be requited!" (2.3.226–227). The speaker of *Self-Portrait* changes slowly instead: hate, then pity, then attachment (stanza 2, line 2; stanza 3, line 1; stanza 4, line 2). Point 2: Beatrice turns suddenly too, bidding farewell to "maiden pride" (3.1.113–115), against the poem's repeated "I turn back" (stanza 2, line 2; stanza 4, line 1). Point 3: Hero's reversal in 5.4 (5.4.65–68) against the speaker who polishes his mirror "night after night" in *Confession* (stanza 4, lines 1–2). Form: stage time compresses change; the stanzas spread it out. B2: each point sets a sudden turn in the play beside a gradual one in the poem, and names the form that sets the pace.

**Bank question p2-q09 (Discuss): dialogue that exposes a conflict nobody names.** Thesis: the play's dialogue hides love behind wit; the poem's address to an absent mother leaves the conflict unnamed. Point 1: Beatrice would rather "hear my dog bark at a crow than a man swear he loves me" (1.1.129–130); *Counting the Stars at Night* addresses its mother directly: "Mother, I call each star by a beautiful word" (stanza 4, line 1). Point 2: Leonato's command, "I charge thee do so, as thou art my child" (4.1.80), makes the conflict over honour public; the poem's "Mother, / and you are far away in Buk-Gando" (stanza 6, lines 1–2) names only the distance. Point 3, form: Benedick answers what he overhears, "This can be no trick" (2.3.223); the lyric has no one to answer, only "And there is a man" (stanza 2, line 1). B2: each point sets what is said beside what is meant, and whether the form gives a reply.

**Bank question p2-q10 (Evaluate): memory reshapes the past, and the role of time.** Thesis: in the play one scene reshapes Hero's past; in the poem memory is a repeated labour, so time works differently in each. Point 1: the Friar's plan keeps Hero's apparent death going, "as it must be so maintained" (4.1.225), so that pity replaces judgement: "Shall be lamented, pitied, and excused" (4.1.227). Point 2: "She died, my lord, but whiles her slander lived" (5.4.68): the slander outlives the woman it destroys. Point 3: in *Confession* the speaker polishes his mirror "night after night" (stanza 4, lines 1–2), and only "the back of a sad person" appears in it (stanza 5, lines 2–3), so the past returns in pieces. Form: the play compresses its time into a few scenes; the poem repeats its time. Judgement: the play reshapes quickly, while the poem shows reshaping as slow work that never ends. B2: compare the speed of reshaping in both works.

**Bank question p2-q11 (Compare): first-person and third-person perspective.** Thesis: the play has no narrator, so its perspective comes from what characters overhear; the poem has one "I" who reports and watches, so its knowledge stays within one view. Point 1: Benedick (2.3.223) and Beatrice (3.1.113–117) overhear the plot, so the audience knows what they do not know. Point 2: the speaker of *Self-Portrait* says "And there is a man" (stanza 2, line 1) and reports only what he sees in the well (stanza 1, lines 3–4), so the reader knows only what the "I" reports. Point 3: in *Confession* the mirror shows only "the back of a sad person" (stanza 5, lines 2–3), not a face the speaker can read. Form: overhearing widens the audience's knowledge; the lyric's single "I" narrows the reader's. B2: each point puts a limit on knowledge beside its widening.

**Bank question p2-q15 (Discuss): failure to communicate as control.** Thesis: in the play Hero's denial has no force, because others decide what is believed; in the poems the speaker chooses silence and so controls what others can read. Point 1: Hero's denial, "I talked with no man at that hour, my lord" (4.1.91), against the accusers' "Did see her, hear her, at that hour last night" (4.1.95). Point 2: Claudio answers the question about Hero's name with a public charge (4.1.86–87). Point 3: the speaker of *Counting the Stars at Night* buries his name under earth (stanza 7, lines 3–4); in *An Easily Written Poem* (쉽게 씌어진 시) he holds out a hand to himself "in tears and comfort" (stanza 10, lines 1–2). Form: the play shows control through speech and testimony, the lyric through burial and self-address. B2: each point ties the power to speak to who decides the outcome.

## How it is examined

Paper 2 may pair texts in the same or different forms, and this pair uses a play and a poetry collection. Paper 2 is 105 minutes printed, so plan each answer before you write. Each criterion is worth 5 marks, 25 in total, as in the school materials:

- **A.** Define identity and judgement, and say who judges in each work: the community in the church scene (4.1.25–32), and the speaker's own feelings in the poems.
- **B1.** Name two or more techniques in each text and explain their effects: stage directions and overhearing in the play; apostrophe, imagery and repetition across stanzas in the poems.
- **B2.** Compare the two works in every point and state the link. The contrast of form earns B2 most easily when each point names the technique of both forms.
- **C.** A thesis that gives a degree, for example "largely, but the poems show judgement from inside, where the play shows it from outside", and an order that follows the prompt.
- **D.** Precise terms (lyric persona, apostrophe, dramatic irony, stage directions) and quotations with their location: act, scene and line for the play; stanza and line for the poems.

<Callout kind="exam">In the exam, quote the translation you studied, with its stanza and line. The Korean lines on this page only help you find the place in the text.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- Comparing plots (a wedding, a death, a walk to the well) instead of techniques: overhearing, apostrophe, repetition across stanzas.
- Quoting the play without act, scene and line, or quoting a poem without its stanza and line.
- Treating the poem's "I" as the poet's own biography. The speaker of *Self-Portrait* is a lyric persona, not a record of Yun Dong-ju's life.
- Presenting the poet's death as proven. He died in a Fukuoka prison in February 1945, but the cause is not established, and the theories about injections and experiments are not confirmed.
- Calling the poems "just emotional" without naming imagery, apostrophe or repetition.

<Callout kind="mistake">A quotation with no location is a claim without evidence: give act, scene and line for the play, and stanza and line for the poem.</Callout>

## Summary

- The pair shares one question: how a person's sense of self meets the judgement of others.
- The forms differ: the play shows its plot through dialogue, stage directions and overheard talk, while the lyric speaks through one "I" with no stage directions.
- Names, shame and the self watched from outside appear in both works, from Hero's name to the speaker's "shameful name".
- The key contrast is knowledge: overhearing lets the audience know more than the characters, while the lyric lets the reader know only what the "I" reports.
- For B2, every point names a technique of both forms and a location in each text.
- Learn the points from our translations, but in the exam quote the translation you studied.
