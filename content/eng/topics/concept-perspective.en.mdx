## Big idea

Perspective is the position from which a text lets the reader see: who narrates, how close that voice stands to events, what it knows, and what it keeps back.

## Explanation

Perspective is the point of view a text takes, and its first question is who is speaking. A <Term id="narrator"/> is the voice that tells a story or a poem, and no narrator is neutral: every word and detail is chosen. In <Term id="first-person-narration"/> the narrator says "I" and takes part in the events. In third-person narration the narrator stands outside and says "he", "she" or "they".

Distance matters as much as person. A narrator can be close to events or far from them in time. A retrospective narrator looks back, knows how things ended and can explain what they meant. An immediate viewpoint stays in the moment, so the reader shares the uncertainty of the person who lives through the events. The gap between the two is where a writer creates irony and emotional effect.

Focus controls what the reader knows. <Term id="focalisation"/> is the character through whose perception the reader experiences events. When focus is limited, the reader sees only what that character notices. An <Term id="omniscient-narrator"/> can enter every mind, move across time and place, and comment freely. That gives the reader a wider view, and it gives the narrator the authority to judge.

Some narrators cannot be trusted. An <Term id="unreliable-narrator"/> gives an account that the reader must test, because of youth, bias, self-deception or lack of knowledge. The reader compares what the narrator says with what the text shows, and the gap between them teaches the reader about the narrator.

<Callout kind="define">An unreliable narrator is one whose account the reader cannot take at face value.</Callout>

<Term id="free-indirect-discourse"/> blends the narrator's voice with a character's thoughts. A third-person sentence takes on the character's rhythm and vocabulary without a phrase such as "she thought". The reader hears the character from inside, while the narrator keeps control of the frame.

A poem has its own perspective. The speaker of a lyric is not necessarily the poet: the <Term id="lyric-persona"/> is the voice the poem creates, with its own situation and its own limits. The reader asks who this speaker is, what has made them speak and what they choose to reveal.

Finally, perspective shapes sympathy. When the reader sees through a character's eyes, they tend to share that character's feelings. When a narrator hides a character's motives, the reader judges that character. Several perspectives, such as alternating narrators or a series of letters, let the reader piece together a fuller truth than any single voice gives.

## Worked example

Read two short paragraphs. Both were written for this lesson and describe the same afternoon in a kitchen. The narrator is the same person at two different moments in life.

Grandma let me stir the soup, which was my job, and I stirred so hard that some of it slopped onto the cooker. She laughed. Then she sat down slowly on the stool by the window and stayed there with her hands in her lap. Mum told me to go and play. The clock in the hall was ticking, nobody was cooking, and I was sure the spilled soup was the reason everyone had gone quiet.

Looking back, I can see that afternoon more clearly than I wanted to. I had been allowed to stir the soup, I had spilled it, and my grandmother had laughed. What I did not understand was that she had read a letter that morning and had decided to wait until the soup was ready before telling anyone. Her stillness at the window was not a joke that had gone on too long. It was the first sign of the illness that would take her within the year.

<Steps>
<Step>**Identify the narrator.** Both paragraphs use "I", so the narrator is the same person at two times. The difference lies not in the person but in the distance from the events.</Step>
<Step>**Test what each version knows.** The child sees the stillness and assumes the spilled soup is the cause. The adult knows about the letter. The child's focalisation limits the reader to the child's guess.</Step>
<Step>**Name the technique in the adult version.** "Looking back" and "what I did not understand" show retrospective narration: the adult controls what the reader is told and reveals what the child could not know.</Step>
<Step>**Explain the effect on the reader.** The child version creates sympathy with the child's guilt; the adult version turns that guilt into understanding. The reader feels the gap between the two and sees how time changes a perspective.</Step>
</Steps>

## Visual

| Perspective | What the reader sees | What stays hidden |
|---|---|---|
| Child, immediate, first person | The spilled soup, the stillness and the child's guilt | The letter and the reason for the silence |
| Adult, retrospective, first person | The whole afternoon, with the letter and the adult's later understanding | The adult's own feelings in that moment, which the adult does not describe |
| Omniscient third person | The thoughts of everyone in the kitchen | The narrator's own opinion, if the narrator never states it |
| Unreliable narrator | The narrator's version of events | The truth the narrator distorts or forgets |
| Lyric persona | The speaker's feelings and reasons | The situation outside the poem |

## How it is examined

Perspective is often the core of "how" questions. Examiners reward a named perspective (narrator, focalisation, retrospective or immediate viewpoint), a quotation that shows it, and an effect on what the reader knows and feels. "Identify" asks you to name the perspective. "Explain" asks why the writer chooses it. "Analyse" and "Evaluate" ask how far the perspective controls the reader, and whether the reader's sympathy is earned. A precise term such as focalisation earns more credit than a general phrase such as "the narrator sees things".

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Writing "the narrator" without saying whether the narrator is first person, third person or a lyric speaker.</Callout>

- Confusing the narrator with the author.
- Assuming that a first-person narrator is always reliable.
- Describing what a character sees without saying what the reader is kept from seeing.
- Treating several perspectives as one: say whose view each section gives.
- Naming a perspective without explaining its effect on sympathy or on what the reader knows.

## Summary

- Perspective is the position from which the text lets the reader see.
- Name the narrator and the person they speak in: first, third, or the lyric persona.
- Distance matters: an immediate viewpoint and a retrospective one see the same events differently.
- Focalisation limits what the reader knows; an omniscient narrator widens it.
- An unreliable narrator makes the reader test every claim against the text.
- Perspective controls sympathy: the reader shares what it shows and judges what it hides.
