## Big idea

A short plan helps build a comparative argument. This exercise uses ten minutes to decode the prompt, choose a suitable pair, propose a thesis and organise supporting points.

## Explanation

The suggested practice budget is 10 minutes planning, 80 writing, 10 checking and 5 reserve within the printed 105 minutes. All stages fit within that printed clock. The five steps below may overlap: revise a thesis when the evidence changes it.

<Steps>
<Step>**Decode the prompt.** Underline the command term and the key words. "Compare" asks for similarities; "Contrast" for differences; "Compare and contrast" for both; "Discuss" asks you to weigh more than one view; "Evaluate" asks for a judgement of how well something works. The key words, such as "sympathy" or "narrative voice", show what to look for in each work.</Step>
<Step>**Choose a pair.** Pick two eligible works that support a meaningful comparison addressing the prompt. The key here is the <Term id="narrator"/>: similar narrators can still create different effects, while contrasting narrative perspectives may offer another productive comparison. Choose works you know in enough detail to support an argument with precise references; memorised quotations are optional.</Step>
<Step>**Write one thesis sentence.** It answers the prompt and makes one claim about how the two works differ or agree in effect. A label such as <Term id="theme"/> names a topic, not a claim: the thesis should make a specific claim responsive to the question.</Step>
<Step>**Choose supporting comparison points.** Three is a useful target for this exercise, not a compulsory number. Connect precise references with analysis of relevant choices and their significance for the question. An event can support analysis of structure or characterisation; retelling it alone does not supply that analysis.</Step>
<Step>**Choose the structure and keep time.** Point by point: each paragraph compares both works on one point, which keeps the comparison tight. Blocks can also work if later discussion explicitly connects with earlier analysis. At about ten minutes, review the remaining time and begin writing; do not let a search for a perfect plan consume the essay.</Step>
</Steps>

Another anchor is <Term id="focalisation"/>: who sees the events, and how much the reader is allowed to know. If one work is told through a single mind and the other moves from mind to mind, that difference belongs in the plan, and the paragraphs will follow it.

<Callout kind="exam">Paper 2 is worth 25 marks, as in the school materials, in 105 minutes. The IB papers from May 2023 to November 2025 print 30 marks. The ten-minute plan is a practice recommendation within that time, not a marked exam section.</Callout>

## Worked example

Here is an original prompt of my own. All details of the pair below are invented for this example; narrator types alone do not establish those details or effects.

> Compare and contrast how two works use a narrator who misreads what happens around them. To what extent does the gap between the narrator's understanding and the reader's understanding shape the reader's response?

- **Decode.** Command: Compare and contrast (both similarities and differences). This invented prompt requests both; criterion B2 itself does not impose that requirement. Key words: "misreads", "gap", "reader's response". The second sentence asks for a judgement of degree, so the thesis must take a position.
- **Pair.** A novel with a retrospective first-person narrator, and a short story narrated by a child who misunderstands adult events. The distance differs: one narrator knows more than the events show, and the other knows less than the reader comes to understand.
- **Thesis.** In both works the gap between narrator and reader makes misreading the reader's main route to meaning, but the novel uses the gap to produce regret and the story uses it to produce unease.
- **Point 1 (difference).** Hindsight with irony in the novel, set against the child's naive report in the story, both read through diction. The effect is regret against unease.
- **Point 2 (similarity).** A <Term id="motif"/> recurs in both works, and each narrator's words are set against what the reader sees. The effect is that meaning grows beyond what either narrator says.
- **Point 3 (so what).** The endings. The novel closes with an adult verdict, while the story stops at the child's last misreading and leaves the judgement to the reader. This answers the "to what extent" part of the prompt, and the <Term id="tone"/> of each ending follows from that choice.
- **Structure.** Point by point, one paragraph per point, with the thesis returned to in the conclusion.
- **Time.** Two minutes to decode, two to choose the pair, two for the thesis, three for the points and one for the structure: ten in total.

## How it is examined

Paper 2 is marked on five criteria, each out of 5. Criterion A checks that your decoding of the prompt has become an understanding of the prompt and of both works. Criteria B1 and B2 reward the authorial choices and the comparison that the plan set up. Criterion C assesses the focus, balance and coherent development of the written answer, even if you revise the plan, and criterion D rewards the language of the essay. The plan itself is not marked, but its results are visible in the thesis, the comparison points and the order of the paragraphs.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming that a shared narrator type rules out a useful comparison, or that different types guarantee one.
- Writing a thesis that describes the topic instead of making a claim about effect.
- Retelling plot events without analysing how their selection, arrangement or presentation shapes meaning.
- Spending 25 minutes on the plan and leaving too little time for the essay.
- Planning two separate essays about two works, with no comparison between them.

<Callout kind="mistake">Without a provisional argument it is easier to drift into summary. You can refine that argument as you plan and write.</Callout>

## Summary

- Practise a ten-minute plan; manage all stages within the printed 105 minutes.
- Decode the command term and the key words before you choose anything.
- Choose two eligible works whose details support the actual question.
- One thesis sentence makes a claim about effect, not a statement about the topic.
- Build enough developed comparative points to support the argument; three is a practice target.
- Point by point is a useful scaffold; connected blocks can also support a coherent comparison.
