## Big idea

The ten-minute plan decides how well the essay can compare. Decode the prompt, choose a suitable pair, state one thesis and build three comparison points before you write a word.

## Explanation

The plan is the most useful part of Paper 2. With a clear plan, each of your 106 writing minutes has a job, and the essay compares from the first paragraph. The plan has five steps, always in this order.

<Steps>
<Step>**Decode the prompt.** Underline the command term and the key words. "Compare" asks for similarity and difference; "Discuss" asks you to weigh more than one view; "Evaluate" asks for a judgement of how well something works. The key words, such as "sympathy" or "narrative voice", show what to look for in each work.</Step>
<Step>**Choose a pair.** Pick two works that differ in the technique the prompt asks about. The key here is the <Term id="narrator"/>: two works with the same kind of narrator give you little to compare, while a narrator who knows more than the events show, set against one who knows less, gives real contrast. Choose works you know well enough to quote.</Step>
<Step>**Write one thesis sentence.** It answers the prompt and makes one claim about how the two works differ or agree in effect. A label such as <Term id="theme"/> names a topic, not a claim: the thesis must say what the topic does to the reader.</Step>
<Step>**Choose three comparison points.** Each point sets a technique from one work beside a technique from the other. That is a <Term id="juxtaposition"/> of the two works, and it can show similarity or difference. Each point must link back to the thesis. A plot event is not a point; a technique and its effect is.</Step>
<Step>**Choose the structure and keep time.** Point by point: each paragraph compares both works on one point, which keeps the comparison tight. Work by work: all of one work, then the other, which is safer but often weak as a comparison. Stop planning at 10 minutes, whatever the state of the plan.</Step>
</Steps>

Another anchor is <Term id="focalisation"/>: who sees the events, and how much the reader is allowed to know. If one work is told through a single mind and the other moves from mind to mind, that difference belongs in the plan, and the paragraphs will follow it.

<Callout kind="exam">Paper 2 is worth 25 marks in 105 minutes, or 131 with your 25% access time. The plan takes 10 minutes either way, and the examiner sees its results in the thesis, the comparison points and the order of the paragraphs.</Callout>

## Worked example

Here is an original prompt of my own. The plan uses a suitable pair, described only by type.

> Compare how two works use a narrator who misreads what happens around them. To what extent does the gap between the narrator's understanding and the reader's understanding shape the reader's response?

- **Decode.** Command: Compare (similarity and difference). Key words: "misreads", "gap", "reader's response". The second sentence asks for a judgement of degree, so the thesis must take a position.
- **Pair.** A novel with a retrospective first-person narrator, and a short story narrated by a child who misunderstands adult events. The distance differs: one narrator knows more than the events show, and the other knows less than the reader comes to understand.
- **Thesis.** In both works the gap between narrator and reader makes misreading the reader's main route to meaning, but the novel uses the gap to produce regret and the story uses it to produce unease.
- **Point 1 (difference).** Hindsight with irony in the novel, set against the child's naive report in the story, both read through diction. The effect is regret against unease.
- **Point 2 (similarity).** A <Term id="motif"/> recurs in both works, and each narrator's words are set against what the reader sees. The effect is that meaning grows beyond what either narrator says.
- **Point 3 (so what).** The endings. The novel closes with an adult verdict, while the story stops at the child's last misreading and leaves the judgement to the reader. This answers the "to what extent" part of the prompt, and the <Term id="tone"/> of each ending follows from that choice.
- **Structure.** Point by point, one paragraph per point, with the thesis returned to in the conclusion.
- **Time.** Two minutes to decode, two to choose the pair, two for the thesis, three for the points and one for the structure: ten in total.

## How it is examined

Paper 2 is marked on five criteria, each out of 5. Criterion A checks that your decoding of the prompt has become an understanding of the prompt and of both works. Criteria B1 and B2 reward the authorial choices and the comparison that the plan set up. Criterion C rewards a structure that follows the plan, and criterion D rewards the language of the essay. The plan itself is not marked, but its results are visible in the thesis, the comparison points and the order of the paragraphs.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a pair with no real difference, such as two works with the same kind of narrator.
- Writing a thesis that describes the topic instead of making a claim about effect.
- Making comparison points out of plot events rather than techniques and their effects.
- Spending 25 minutes on the plan and leaving too little time for the essay.
- Planning two separate essays about two works, with no comparison between them.

<Callout kind="mistake">A plan with no thesis cannot compare: the essay will describe two works one after another.</Callout>

## Summary

- Spend 10 minutes on the plan, no more: decode, choose, thesis, points, structure.
- Decode the command term and the key words before you choose anything.
- Choose a pair that differs in the technique the prompt asks about.
- One thesis sentence makes a claim about effect, not a statement about the topic.
- Three comparison points, each a pair of techniques that links back to the thesis.
- Point by point is usually the stronger structure.
