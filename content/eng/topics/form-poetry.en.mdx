## Big idea

A poem works through its sound, rhythm, form and voice. Each formal choice changes how the reader hears the speaker, so a strong answer always links a device to a meaning.

## Explanation

Poetry is read with the ear as well as the eye. Four layers do most of the work: sound, rhythm and metre, form, and voice. Name the layer first, then say what it does to the meaning.

Sound repeats or echoes sounds. <Term id="alliteration"/> repeats the same consonant at the start of nearby stressed words. Assonance repeats a vowel sound, and consonance repeats a consonant inside words. <Term id="onomatopoeia"/> imitates a sound, such as a buzz or a hiss. Repetition draws the ear to the words it links, so always ask what the reader is meant to notice.

<Term id="metre"/> is the regular pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables. The commonest English metre is the <Term id="iamb"/>, one unstressed syllable followed by one stressed syllable. Five iambs make an iambic pentameter, ten syllables with the stress on every second one. An end-stopped line closes with punctuation that stops the sense. A caesura is a pause inside a line. An <Term id="enjambment"/> is a sentence that runs over the end of a line with no punctuation at the break.

Form is the shape of the poem on the page: its lines, its stanzas and its rhyme scheme. A <Term id="stanza"/> is a group of lines. A <Term id="quatrain"/> has four lines and a <Term id="couplet"/> has two. A <Term id="sonnet"/> has fourteen lines and a <Term id="volta"/>, a turn in the argument, which in an Italian sonnet comes at the start of the sestet. A <Term id="ballad"/> tells a story in short stanzas with a steady rhythm of four stresses and three. An ode praises its subject in a formal address; an elegy mourns a loss. A poem can also break its own pattern, and the break is often where the meaning turns.

Voice is the person who speaks. The speaker is a <Term id="lyric-persona"/>, a voice the poet creates, and it is not automatically the poet. An apostrophe addresses someone absent or dead, which changes the intimacy of the voice. A <Term id="conceit"/> is an extended, surprising metaphor that the poem develops over several lines. Who speaks shapes how far the reader trusts the feeling.

<Callout kind="define">A rhyme scheme is written with letters. ABAB means that lines 1 and 3 rhyme, and lines 2 and 4 rhyme.</Callout>

## Worked example

<Question id="p1-hardy-thrush"/>

The guiding question asks how Hardy changes the speaker's mood. Form is one of his tools, so start with the shape of the stanzas, then follow the sound and the voice.

<Steps>
<Step>**Count the form.** There are four stanzas of eight lines each, and each one follows the same <Term id="rhyme-scheme"/>, ABABCDCD. In the first stanza, "gate" and "desolate" are A, "gray" and "day" are B, "sky" and "nigh" are C, and "lyres" and "fires" are D.</Step>
<Step>**Test the metre.** The lines alternate between four stresses and three. "I LEANT up-ON a COP-pice GATE" has four stresses; "When FROST was SPEC-tre-GRAY" has three. The rhythm is iambic, built from <Term id="iamb"/>s. The four-three alternation is the rhythm of a <Term id="ballad"/>, but Hardy spreads it over eight lines.</Step>
<Step>**Notice that the rhymes loosen.** In the third stanza, "among" and "evensong" share only the -ong sound, and "small" and "soul" share only the final l. Only "plume" and "gloom" rhyme fully. A reader can connect these looser, slant rhymes with the speaker's uncertain hope. That is an interpretation, not a fact of the text.</Step>
<Step>**Follow the sound.** In the first stanza, "Winter’s dregs made desolate" and "The tangled bine-stems scored the sky" use <Term id="alliteration"/>: the d and the sk sounds return in stressed words. The b sound returns in the third stanza, in "The bleak twigs overhead" and "In blast-beruffled plume" (lines 18 and 22).</Step>
<Step>**Track the voice.** Lines 17 to 20 form one sentence, with no stop until the semicolon after "illimited": "At once a voice outburst among / The bleak twigs overhead / In a full-hearted evensong / Of joy illimited;". The <Term id="enjambment"/> carries the song across the line breaks. The "I" who "leant upon a coppice gate" is a <Term id="lyric-persona"/>, a speaker that the poem creates.</Step>
<Step>**Name the figure.** Lines 9 to 12 build a funeral out of the landscape: "The Century’s corpse outleant", "His crypt the cloudy canopy", "The wind his death-lament". This is a <Term id="conceit"/>: an extended metaphor that links the landscape with a funeral across four lines.</Step>
</Steps>

Hardy's form holds the poem together while its sound loosens. The steady alternation of four and three stresses suggests an order the speaker wants to trust, but the rhymes weaken in the third stanza, where the thrush sings. The change of mood is carried by the form as well as by the bird: the song arrives in one unbroken sentence, and its rhymes do not quite close, which leaves the speaker's hope open.

## How it is examined

In Paper 1, form earns marks only when it serves the guiding question. Criterion B rewards a named device, its effect and a judgement of how well it works. Criterion A checks that you understand the speaker and the situation. Criterion D rewards precise terms such as metre, enjambment and stanza, and short quotations woven into your sentences. In Paper 2, form is one of the authorial choices (criterion B1), and the same terms help you compare two poems, or a poem and a prose text. The command terms you will meet most often are Analyse, Explain and Compare.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Naming a device without saying what it does to the reader is the most common loss of marks in poetry.</Callout>

- Calling any poem with rhymes a sonnet. A sonnet has fourteen lines.
- Writing a rhyme scheme without showing it. Write the letters and give one pair of rhyming words.
- Saying a poem is in iambic pentameter without quoting a line that proves it.
- Treating every line that ends without punctuation as an enjambment. The sentence must run on into the next line.
- Listing sound devices without linking them to mood or meaning.

## Summary

- A poem works through sound, rhythm, form and voice. Link each one to a meaning.
- Metre is the regular pattern of stresses. An iamb is one unstressed syllable followed by one stressed syllable.
- A rhyme scheme is written as letters: ABAB means that lines 1 and 3 rhyme.
- Enjambment runs a sentence over the end of a line; a caesura is a pause inside a line.
- Form names the shape: a quatrain has four lines, a couplet two, a sonnet fourteen.
- The lyric persona is the speaker the poem creates; an apostrophe speaks to someone absent.
- A conceit is an extended metaphor developed over several lines.
