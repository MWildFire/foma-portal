## Big idea

A poem works through its sound, rhythm, form and voice. Each formal choice changes how the reader hears the speaker, so a strong answer always links a device to a meaning.

## Explanation

Poetry is read with the ear as well as the eye. Four useful entry points are sound, rhythm and metre, form, and voice. Name the layer first, then say what it does to the meaning.

Sound repeats or echoes sounds. <Term id="alliteration"/> repeats the same consonant at the start of nearby stressed words. Assonance repeats vowel sounds; consonance repeats consonant sounds, including at word endings. <Term id="onomatopoeia"/> imitates a sound, such as a buzz or a hiss. Repetition draws the ear to the words it links, so always ask what the reader is meant to notice.

<Term id="metre"/> in the English accentual-syllabic verse considered here is an underlying pattern of stresses and syllables, with possible variations. A common English metrical foot is the <Term id="iamb"/>, one unstressed syllable followed by one stressed syllable. Five iambic feet give the underlying pattern of iambic pentameter, often ten syllables; substitutions and an extra unstressed ending are possible. For example, Hamlet’s “To be or not to be—that is the question” can be scanned as five iambic beats plus the final unstressed syllable of “question”. An end-stopped line closes a grammatical or sense unit, often marked by punctuation. A caesura is a pause inside a line. An <Term id="enjambment"/> carries a sentence or phrase beyond a line ending without terminal closure. Compare the syntax with the line break; absence of punctuation alone does not establish it.

Form is the shape of the poem on the page: its lines, its stanzas and its rhyme scheme. A <Term id="stanza"/> is a group of lines. A <Term id="quatrain"/> has four lines and a <Term id="couplet"/> has two. A <Term id="sonnet"/> conventionally has fourteen lines, with established patterns and later variations. A <Term id="volta"/> is a turn of thought or mood, often near the start of the sestet in a Petrarchan sonnet, not obligatorily at that point. A <Term id="ballad"/> is a narrative form often using short stanzas; traditional English ballad metre commonly alternates four and three beats. An ode is often a sustained address or meditation; in modern literary usage an elegy often mourns a loss. A poem can also break its own pattern, and the break is often where the meaning turns.

Voice is the person who speaks. The speaker is a <Term id="lyric-persona"/>, a voice the poet creates, and it is not automatically the poet. An apostrophe addresses an absent or dead person, an object or an abstraction, which changes the intimacy of the voice. A <Term id="conceit"/> is an ingenious or surprising comparison, often developed at length; length alone does not define it. Who speaks shapes how far the reader trusts the feeling.

<Callout kind="define">A rhyme scheme is written with letters. ABAB means that lines 1 and 3 rhyme, and lines 2 and 4 rhyme.</Callout>

The guiding question asks how Hardy changes the speaker's mood. Form is one of his tools, so start with the shape of the stanzas, then follow the sound and the voice.

<Steps>
<Step>**Count the form.** There are four stanzas of eight lines each, and each one follows the same <Term id="rhyme-scheme"/>, ABABCDCD. In the first stanza, "gate" and "desolate" are A, "gray" and "day" are B, "sky" and "nigh" are C, and "lyres" and "fires" are D.</Step>
<Step>**Test the metre.** The lines alternate between four stresses and three. "I LEANT up-ON a COP-pice GATE" has four stresses; "When FROST was SPEC-tre-GRAY" has three. The rhythm is iambic, built from <Term id="iamb"/>s. The four-three alternation is the rhythm of a <Term id="ballad"/>, but Hardy spreads it over eight lines.</Step>
<Step>**Compare approximate rhymes.** In the third stanza, "among" /əˈmʌŋ/ and "evensong" /ˈiːvənˌsɒŋ/ share the final /ŋ/ but have different vowels in the cited standard British pronunciations. "Small" and "soul" are also approximate, while "overhead" and "illimited" are a looser pair. "Plume" and "gloom" form a full rhyme. Compare sounds, not the shared spelling “ong”; accent can affect the analysis. The rhyme-scheme letters mark pairings, not a guarantee that every pair is a perfect sound match in every accent. A reader can connect these slant rhymes with the speaker's uncertain hope. That is an interpretation, not a fact of the text.</Step>
<Step>**Follow the sound.** In the first stanza, "Winter’s dregs made desolate" and "The tangled bine-stems scored the sky" use <Term id="alliteration"/>: the d and the sk sounds return in stressed words. The b sound returns in the third stanza, in "The bleak twigs overhead" and "In blast-beruffled plume" (lines 18 and 22).</Step>
<Step>**Track the voice.** Lines 17 to 20 form the opening clause of a sentence that continues through line 24. The semicolon after "illimited" is a pause within it: "At once a voice outburst among / The bleak twigs overhead / In a full-hearted evensong / Of joy illimited;". The <Term id="enjambment"/> carries the song across the line breaks. The "I" who "leant upon a coppice gate" is a <Term id="lyric-persona"/>, a speaker that the poem creates.</Step>
<Step>**Name the figure.** Lines 9 to 12 build a funeral out of the landscape: "The Century’s corpse outleant", "His crypt the cloudy canopy", "The wind his death-lament". This is a <Term id="conceit"/>: an ingenious comparison that develops the landscape as a funeral for the century through corpse, crypt and lament; its four-line span alone does not define it.</Step>
</Steps>

Hardy’s regular stanza lengths and rhyme positions coexist with approximate sound matches. The steady alternation of four and three stresses can suggest formal continuity, while the approximate rhymes in the bird’s stanza can complicate an impression of complete closure. The change of mood is carried by the form as well as by the bird: the song arrives in a sentence with internal pauses, while approximate rhymes can be read alongside the uncertainty of the final stanza. The speaker entertains the possibility of hope without claiming to share the bird’s knowledge.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Support interpretations of sound or form with the poem’s words and structure. Avoid giving every device a fixed emotional effect.</Callout>

- Calling any poem with rhymes a sonnet. The conventional sonnet has fourteen lines, but examine its structure as well; later variants exist.
- Writing a rhyme scheme without showing it. Write the letters and give one pair of rhyming words.
- Saying a poem is in iambic pentameter without quoting a line that proves it.
- Treating every line that ends without punctuation as an enjambment. The sentence must run on into the next line.
- Listing sound devices without linking them to mood or meaning.

## Summary

- A poem works through sound, rhythm, form and voice. Link each one to a meaning.
- In the English verse discussed here, metre supplies an underlying stress pattern, with variations. An iamb is one unstressed syllable followed by one stressed syllable.
- A rhyme scheme is written as letters: ABAB means that lines 1 and 3 rhyme.
- Enjambment runs a sentence over the end of a line; a caesura is a pause inside a line.
- Form names the shape: a quatrain has four lines, a couplet two, a sonnet fourteen.
- The lyric persona is a created speaking voice; apostrophe can address a person, object or abstraction.
- A conceit is an ingenious or surprising comparison, often elaborated; explain the connection and its development, not a minimum number of lines.
