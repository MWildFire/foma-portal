## Big idea

Criterion B rewards a chain: a named technique, what it does to the reader, and a judgement of how well it works in this context. A device with no effect earns nothing.

## Explanation

Criterion B rests on a chain with three links. The device is the named technique, for example a <Term id="simile"/>, a <Term id="metaphor"/>, a <Term id="juxtaposition"/> or a change in <Term id="syntax"/>. The effect is what the technique does to the reader: the feeling it creates, the idea it makes clear or the expectation it sets up. The evaluation judges how well the effect works, for whom, and in which context.

Apply the so-what test after every device. Write the effect, then ask "so what?" If the answer is only "the reader feels something", push further until it says something about the text: "the reader feels trapped, so the character's ambition seems hopeless". If you cannot answer the question, drop the device. Three techniques explained in depth serve the answer better than nine that are only named.

Search the text by cluster, so that you look at the whole extract and not only at the first striking image. Sound asks what rhythm, repetition or <Term id="pace"/> does. Image asks what the <Term id="imagery"/>, <Term id="metaphor"/> or <Term id="symbol"/> makes the reader see, and how that connects to the theme. Structure asks where the text turns, and whether <Term id="foreshadowing"/> prepares the reader for the turn. Perspective asks whose view the reader shares and how far it can be trusted. Dialogue asks what the speakers say and what they avoid saying. Syntax asks what the shape of the sentences does. Diction asks why this word was chosen rather than a near synonym, and what its <Term id="connotation"/> adds.

<Callout kind="define">Evaluation is a judgement, supported by evidence from the text, about how well a technique achieves its effect in this passage.</Callout>

Choose devices by their effect, not by their number. Pick the three or four techniques that do the most work in the extract and leave the rest out. Name them exactly: a <Term id="simile"/> uses like or as, while a <Term id="metaphor"/> says that one thing is another. Then write the chain in order: device, effect, so what, evaluation.

A judgement needs a reason. The word "effective" on its own says nothing. Write instead: "the image works because the reader has already seen the storm", or "the device is less convincing here, because the sentence repeats an idea that is already clear". A qualified judgement, with one strength and one limit, shows the examiner that you have weighed the device rather than praised it.

## Worked example

Here is an original paragraph written for this topic. Work through it with the chain, one device at a time.

The lift stopped between the fourth and fifth floors. The light above the door flickered once, like a held breath, and went out. Nadia pressed the button again and again, as if the machine were a stubborn child. Somewhere above her, a radio was playing a summer song, thin and far away.

<Steps>
<Step>**Simile: "like a held breath".** The device is a <Term id="simile"/>: the flicker of the light is compared to a held breath. The effect is suspense, because the reader waits with Nadia while the light hangs between on and off. The evaluation: the comparison works because it ties the failure of the light to a tense, physical moment, but the phrase is familiar, so the device works through the situation rather than through surprise.</Step>
<Step>**Simile and diction: "as if the machine were a stubborn child".** This is another <Term id="simile"/>, and the <Term id="diction"/> of "stubborn" suggests obstinacy and helplessness. The effect is that Nadia's frustration shows through her behaviour, without a word about her feelings. The evaluation: effective, because the reader sees her state of mind, although a second comparison in the same paragraph begins to crowd the first.</Step>
<Step>**Juxtaposition: the song and the lift.** The summer song, "thin and far away", is placed against the stuck lift, a <Term id="juxtaposition"/> of two worlds. The effect is that ordinary life is close but out of reach, and the lift becomes a place of isolation. The evaluation: this is the strongest device in the paragraph, because it moves from the mechanical problem to the character's situation, although the contrast between inside and outside is a familiar one.</Step>
</Steps>

Now run the so-what test on the strongest device. The juxtaposition means that Nadia is cut off from ordinary life, so the paragraph is about isolation, and the broken lift is only the way that isolation appears.

## How it is examined

<Callout kind="exam">Criterion B is worth up to 5 marks. Examiners look for named techniques, precise effects and a judgement on how well each device works in this text. A list of devices without effects earns little.</Callout>

Analyse asks you to examine a device in detail and say what it does. Evaluate asks for a judgement on how well it works, with reasons. Criterion B needs both: the analysis gives the effect, and the evaluation gives the judgement. Practise the chain on whole passages:

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">The most common loss of criterion B marks is a device that is named correctly but never explained.</Callout>

- Listing techniques without saying what any of them does to the reader.
- Describing the effect in general terms, such as "it is sad", instead of the reader's response to the technique.
- Calling a device effective without giving a reason.
- Confusing a simile with a metaphor: a simile uses "like" or "as".
- Analysing too many devices in a short answer, so that none is developed.

## Summary

- Use the chain: device, effect, so what, evaluation.
- Ask "so what?" after each effect until the answer says something about the text.
- Search by cluster: sound, image, structure, perspective, dialogue, syntax and diction.
- Name each technique exactly: a simile uses like or as, a metaphor does not.
- Judge each device: how well it works, for whom and in which context, with a reason.
- Choose three or four strong devices over a long list.
