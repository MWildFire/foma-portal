## Big idea

Criterion B concerns how textual features and authorial choices shape meaning. Explain the choice, connect it to a precise detail and develop its significance. Merely naming a technique is not developed analysis; there is no automatic zero for one undeveloped observation.

## Explanation

Criterion B rests on a chain with three links. The device is the named technique, for example a <Term id="simile"/>, a <Term id="metaphor"/>, a <Term id="juxtaposition"/> or a change in <Term id="syntax"/>. The effect is what the technique does to the reader: the feeling it creates, the idea it makes clear or the expectation it sets up. Evaluation develops the significance of the choice in its context: how it contributes to meaning, tension, perspective or ambiguity. It need not grade the writer as successful or unsuccessful.

Apply the so-what test after every device. Write the effect, then ask "so what?" If the answer is only "the reader feels something", push further until it says something about the text: "the reader feels trapped, so the character's ambition seems hopeless". If you cannot answer the question, drop the device. Three techniques explained in depth serve the answer better than nine that are only named.

Search the text by cluster, so that you look at the whole extract and not only at the first striking image. Sound asks what rhythm, repetition or <Term id="pace"/> does. Image asks what the <Term id="imagery"/>, <Term id="metaphor"/> or <Term id="symbol"/> makes the reader see, and how that connects to the theme. Structure asks where the text turns, and whether <Term id="foreshadowing"/> prepares the reader for the turn. Perspective asks whose view the reader shares and how far it can be trusted. Dialogue asks what the speakers say and what they avoid saying. Syntax asks what the shape of the sentences does. Diction asks why this word was chosen rather than a near synonym, and what its <Term id="connotation"/> adds.

<Callout kind="define">Evaluation is a supported judgement about how an authorial choice contributes to meaning in this passage. A strengths-and-weaknesses verdict on every device is not required.</Callout>

Choose devices by their effect, not by their number. Pick the three or four techniques that do the most work in the extract and leave the rest out. Name them exactly: a <Term id="simile"/> uses like or as, while a <Term id="metaphor"/> says that one thing is another. Then write the chain in order: device, effect, so what, evaluation.

A judgement needs a reason. The word "effective" on its own says nothing. Write instead: "the image works because the reader has already seen the storm", or "the device is less convincing here, because the sentence repeats an idea that is already clear". Explain why the choice matters to this reading. Discuss a limitation or ambiguity when the text supports it; do not invent a weakness merely to make the paragraph look balanced.

## Worked example

Here is an original paragraph written for this topic. Work through it with the chain, one device at a time.

The lift stopped between the fourth and fifth floors. The light above the door flickered once, like a held breath, and went out. Nadia pressed the button again and again, as if the machine were a stubborn child. Somewhere above her, a radio was playing a summer song, thin and far away.

<Steps>
<Step>**Simile: "like a held breath".** The device is a <Term id="simile"/>: the flicker of the light is compared to a held breath. The effect is suspense, because the reader waits with Nadia while the light hangs between on and off. Its significance is that the mechanical failure is experienced through a bodily image: the pause makes Nadia’s uncertainty tangible.</Step>
<Step>**Simile and diction: "as if the machine were a stubborn child".** This is another <Term id="simile"/>, and the <Term id="diction"/> of "stubborn" suggests obstinacy and helplessness. The effect is that Nadia's frustration shows through her behaviour, without a word about her feelings. Its significance is that Nadia treats an unresponsive machine like someone resisting her; the personifying comparison makes frustration visible through action.</Step>
<Step>**Juxtaposition: the song and the lift.** The summer song, "thin and far away", is placed against the stuck lift, a <Term id="juxtaposition"/> of two worlds. The effect is that ordinary life is close but out of reach, and the lift becomes a place of isolation. Its significance is that the ordinary song continues beyond her confinement, linking the setting to isolation rather than merely reporting a fault in the lift.</Step>
</Steps>

Now run the so-what test on the strongest device. The juxtaposition means that Nadia is cut off from ordinary life, so the paragraph is about isolation, and the broken lift is only the way that isolation appears.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Check that named choices are analysed through precise evidence rather than left as a list.</Callout>

- Listing techniques without saying what any of them does to the reader.
- Describing the effect in general terms, such as "it is sad", instead of the reader's response to the technique.
- Calling a device effective without giving a reason.
- Confusing a simile with a metaphor: a simile uses "like" or "as".
- Analysing too many devices in a short answer, so that none is developed.

## Summary

- Use the chain: device, effect, so what, evaluation.
- Ask "so what?" after each effect until the answer says something about the text.
- Search by cluster: sound, image, structure, perspective, dialogue, syntax and diction.
- Name each technique exactly: a simile uses like or as, a metaphor does not.
- Explain the significance of each selected choice in context, with evidence; a compulsory strengths-and-weaknesses verdict is unnecessary.
- Choose three or four strong devices over a long list.
