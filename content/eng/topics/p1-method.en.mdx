## Big idea

Paper 1 asks for an argument, not a summary. You analyse one unseen extract with a clear technical or formal focus, and every paragraph develops that focus.

## Explanation

Paper 1 gives you two unseen extracts from different forms, each with a guiding question. You answer one of them in 75 minutes. The paper is worth 20 marks: four criteria, each worth 5 marks. The printed Literature SL instructions allow the supplied guiding question or an alternative technical or formal focus. For these practice tasks, use the supplied question as a useful starting point. The six stages below are a planning method, not a compulsory essay template.

1. **Read the guiding question first.** Underline the words that tell you what to do: "how", "to what extent", "present", "explore". Decide what your answer must explain.
2. **Read the extract for sense.** Work out who is speaking, to whom, where and when, and what changes from the beginning to the end.
3. **Annotate.** Read again with a pencil. Mark the techniques (for example <Term id="imagery"/>, sentence length, sound, punctuation, layout) and the places where they change. Note only what you can use in an argument.
4. **Write a thesis.** One sentence that answers the guiding question and makes a claim about effect.
5. **Plan three or four paragraphs.** Each paragraph makes one point that supports the thesis. Order the points so that they build the argument, not in the order of the lines.
6. **Write, then finish with an insight.** Each paragraph follows the same chain: point, quotation, technique, effect, link back to the question. The conclusion develops the strongest idea in your thesis; it does not retell the extract.

<Callout kind="exam">Paper 1 rewards a focused analysis. You may follow the guiding question or choose another technical or formal aspect supported by the extract. Retelling alone does not analyse how choices shape meaning.</Callout>

## Worked example

<Question id="p1-dickinson-fly"/>

Start with the guiding question. The key words are "sound" and "stillness": the answer has to explain how the two work together.

<Steps>
<Step>**Annotate the first stanza.** The stillness around the speaker is compared to "the stillness in the air / Between the heaves of storm". The <Term id="simile"/> makes the calm feel like a threat, as if a storm were gathering. Mark the comparison.</Step>
<Step>**Annotate the third stanza.** The dash after "assignable," and the line break after "and then" make the reader wait for the intruder. The <Term id="enjambment"/> is the technique to note: the sentence runs on from "and then" into "There interposed a fly", which puts the fly into a line of its own.</Step>
<Step>**Annotate the fourth stanza.** The phrase "blue, uncertain, stumbling buzz" is a sound that also moves. The <Term id="onomatopoeia"/> of "buzz" is the sound of the fly, while "stumbling" shows its clumsy flight. The last line, "I could not see to see", turns the failure of sight into an echo.</Step>
<Step>**Write the thesis.** Dickinson makes death <Term id="anticlimax"/>: the smallest sound in the room interrupts the moment that should be the most solemn of a life.</Step>
<Step>**Plan four points.** (1) The stillness and the simile of the storm. (2) The expected ritual of the eyes, the breaths and the king, where “the king” may evoke death or a religious expectation; justify the reading from the words rather than treating one identification as certain. (3) The fly as an interruption. (4) The failure of the senses in the last stanza.</Step>
</Steps>

Model paragraph for point (1): The poem opens with a silence that promises a solemn ending. The stillness around the speaker is compared to "the stillness in the air / Between the heaves of storm", so the calm feels threatening, as if a storm were about to break. Against this pause, the fly's buzz is small and ordinary, and the dash after "assignable," and the line break after "and then" make the reader wait for it. The effect is that death becomes an interruption rather than the climax the room has prepared for.

## How it is examined

Each of the four criteria is worth 5 marks. Understanding and interpretation checks that you understand the extract and its situation. Analysis and evaluation concerns how textual features and authorial choices shape meaning, including structure, characterisation and perspective. Focus and organisation concerns a coherent, organised response to your chosen focus. Language concerns clear, accurate, effective expression and an appropriate register. Accurate terminology and well-integrated quotations can help communicate analysis; neither is a checklist or a compulsory quotation length. See the [IB 2026 examiner instructions, Paper 1, criteria A–D](https://ibpublishing.ibo.org/exinst/apps/exinst/index.html?chapter=1&doc=EX_instructions_2026_e&part=8).

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Check that your response develops its chosen focus rather than only retelling the extract.</Callout>

- Retelling the extract line by line instead of answering the question.
- Quoting without explaining how relevant words or choices shape meaning. Name a technique when the term helps you explain it precisely.
- Writing a thesis that describes the topic ("Dickinson writes about death") instead of making a claim about effect.
- Spending 30 minutes on the first paragraph and leaving no time for the conclusion.

## Summary

- Read the guiding question first. Use it, or state a different technical or formal focus; sustain the focus you choose.
- Read for sense, then annotate the techniques and the places where they change.
- A thesis is one sentence that answers the question and makes a claim about effect.
- Plan three or four paragraphs. Each one is a point, a quotation, a technique, an effect and a link to the question.
- Budget the 75 minutes: 10 for reading and choosing, 5 for annotation, 10 for the thesis and plan, 45 for writing, 5 for checking. You type in the exam, so practise typing a response against the clock.
- The conclusion develops your insight; it does not retell the extract.
