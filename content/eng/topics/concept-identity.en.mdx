## Big idea

Identity in literature is the question of who a person is, and who others say they must be. A text shows identity most clearly when the inner self and the outer role pull in different directions.

## Explanation

Identity is one of the key concepts of the course, and it can appear in both papers. In literature, identity is shaped by the self (how a character sees themselves), by others (how family, friends and strangers name and judge them) and by the roles imposed on them. Writers return to these questions again and again, because identity is never settled once and for all.

<Callout kind="define">Identity is a person's sense of who they are, together with the labels and expectations that other people place on them.</Callout>

Several forces press on identity. The family hands down roles, such as the child who must be the responsible one. Class and money decide which futures are possible. Gender and nation set what is expected of a person and what they are allowed to claim. Language and names carry belonging: a name spoken correctly can make a person feel at home, and a name changed or refused can make them feel foreign. Memory holds the past from which identity is built. The <Term id="protagonist"/> is usually the character under the most of these pressures, so the protagonist's choices show the cost of each one.

The central conflict of identity lies between the inner self and the outer role. A character may behave as the family expects while privately believing something else, and the gap between the two is where a writer shows identity most clearly. A <Term id="foil"/> helps here: a character who makes the opposite choice, such as a sibling who leaves, shows what the protagonist has given up.

Identity links closely to technique. The <Term id="narrator"/> decides how much the reader knows about the self. A narrator who is honest about their own identity shows it from inside, while a narrator who describes others may reveal a self they do not admit. In <Term id="first-person-narration"/>, the "I" is both the subject of the story and the place where identity is tested, because the speaker must describe who they are in their own words. Dialogue shows identity from outside: the name a character is given, and the answers others expect from them, can reveal a role that the character has not chosen.

In poetry, identity often appears through a voice that is not quite the poet. A <Term id="lyric-persona"/> is the speaker of a poem, and the persona's identity can differ from the poet's. This allows a writer to explore a role without claiming it.

## Worked example

The paragraph below is original and invented. It names no real work and no real character. It shows a character's identity under pressure, and it names two techniques.

Marta is named by her family before she speaks, and the name carries a job: she is the one who keeps the shop. Her thoughts return again and again to a phrase her mother has used, "someone has to keep the shop", and the repetition shows how far the role has entered her thinking, so that she no longer hears it as a demand. Her cousin, who left the business years ago and now signs letters with a different surname, appears as a foil: he is the version of Marta she has not dared to become. The contrast makes the cost of belonging visible. Marta keeps the family's name, and in doing so she gives up the one she would choose.

<Steps>
<Step>**Find the pressure.** The family's expectation is the outer role, and the phrase "someone has to keep the shop" is the force the identity must answer.</Step>
<Step>**Name the first technique.** The <Term id="interior-monologue"/> repeats the mother's phrase inside Marta's own thoughts. The repetition shows that the role has become her own thought, so the reader sees the identity from inside.</Step>
<Step>**Name the second technique.** The cousin is a <Term id="foil"/>. His different choice makes Marta's loss visible, because the reader can compare the two identities side by side.</Step>
<Step>**Link to identity.** The paragraph argues that belonging costs the self. That claim answers a concept question directly, and the same method works in an essay about any two works.</Step>
</Steps>

## Visual

| Concept question | What it looks like in a text | A technique to name |
|---|---|---|
| Who does the character believe they are? | The character describes themselves, then contradicts the description | First-person narration; an unreliable narrator |
| Who names the character, and how? | A name is repeated, changed or refused by others | Repetition; a motif |
| What do the family or the nation expect? | A voice from outside appears inside the character's thoughts | Interior monologue |
| Where does the self conflict with the role? | Two characters take different paths from the same origin | A foil |
| Who speaks for the poem? | The speaker's identity differs from the poet's | A lyric persona |

## How it is examined

Identity can be used in both papers. In Paper 1 it sits inside the analysis of one extract. In Paper 2 it gives a shared question for two works, so the concept supports a comparison rather than replacing it. Examiners reward a claim about identity that is backed by a named technique and a detail from the text. They do not reward a list of pressures with no technique attached.

<Callout kind="exam">A concept paragraph earns credit when it asks a precise question about identity, answers it through a technique and shows the effect on the reader.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Naming the concept is not the same as using it.</Callout>

- Saying that a character has an identity without saying what it is or how the text shows it.
- Listing the pressures, such as family, class, gender and nation, with no technique attached to any of them.
- Treating identity as the character's name alone.
- Writing as if identity is fixed, when most texts show it changing under pressure.

## Summary

- Identity asks who a person is, and who others say they must be.
- The central conflict is between the inner self and the outer role.
- Family, class, gender, nation, language, names and memory press on identity.
- The protagonist shows the cost of these pressures most clearly.
- A foil shows what a character gives up by making a different choice.
- Link identity to a technique: a narrator, a voice, an interior monologue or a lyric persona.
- Use the concept in general terms, then support it with evidence from the two works.
