## Big idea

The five poems in this topic show a lyric voice that judges itself. The speaker looks into a well, into a mirror or up at the autumn sky, sees himself, and asks who he is and what he must do. For Paper 2, read each poem in its own form: one speaker, stanzas, line breaks and repetition. Then compare it with a play, where meaning comes from dialogue and stage directions, and with a non-fiction text, where a narrator reports. Stanza and line numbers give you exact references for that comparison.

## Explanation

Yun Dong-ju was born on 30 December 1917 in Myeongdong village in Buk-Gando, Manchuria, near Jilin. He studied at Yonhi College in Seoul, then at Rikkyo in Tokyo and at Doshisha in Kyoto. Korea was then under Japanese rule. In 1943 he was arrested for alleged anti-Japanese activity and sentenced to two years in prison. He died in Fukuoka Prison on 16 February 1945, aged 27. The cause of death is not established: the theories about saltwater injections and medical experiments are not confirmed. His collection 하늘과 바람과 별과 시 (Sky, Wind, Star, and Poem) was published in January 1948, after his death.

The class file, the school's anthology, has an English translation of sixteen poems. It names no translator and no publisher, so we cannot say which published edition it follows. Only the Prologue has Korean text in that file. The Korean lines below come from the 1948 edition and other sources, and the English after each line is our own literal translation. The file has no page numbers, so cite the poem's title, stanza and line. Confession is not in the file at all.

<Callout kind="define">A lyric persona is the speaker of a lyric poem: a created voice that is not always the poet. In these poems the speaker can split into a watcher and the one who is watched.</Callout>

### Prologue (서시) — if you studied this poem

The speaker looks up at the sky and wishes for not one speck of shame (stanza 1, line 2). Across three stanzas he moves from that wish to a decision: he will love all things that are dying and walk the road he has been given. The key images are the sky, the wind in the leaves and the stars. The sky and the stars work as a <Term id="symbol"/> of an ideal he wants to reach. The stanzas have four, four and one lines, so the short last line, in which the stars are brushed by the wind, ends the poem quietly. Themes: Freedom & Control, because the road is given, not chosen, and Identity, because the speaker defines himself by what he promises. Concepts: identity and perspective, since he looks up rather than down.

**Korean, stanza 2, lines 3–4:** 그리고 나한테 주어진 길을 / 걸어가야겠다. Our literal translation: "and the road that has been given to me / I shall walk it."

### Self-Portrait (자화상) — if you studied this poem

The speaker walks alone to a well in a field and looks in. The water shows the moon, the clouds, the sky, a blue wind and autumn, and then "a man" (stanza 1). He hates the man and turns back. On the way back he pities him, and when he looks again the man is still there, just as he was. He hates him again and then finds him dear (stanzas 2–4). Stanza 5 brings the scene back, and the man is there "like a memory". The key images are the well, the moon in the water and the autumn sky. The well and "that man" return across the poem, so they work as a <Term id="motif"/>. Treating the man in the water as someone else splits the speaker into a watcher and a watched self, which is the work of a <Term id="lyric-persona"/>. Themes: Identity, because the self he judges is his own reflection, and Truth & Deception, because the image stays the same while his feelings change. Concept: perspective, since the view from the well changes with the viewer.

**Korean, stanza 2, line 2:** 어쩐지 그 사나이가 미워져 돌아갑니다. Our literal translation: "Somehow I come to hate that man, and I turn back."

**Korean, stanza 4, line 2:** 돌아가다 생각하니 그 사나이가 그리워집니다. Our literal translation: "Going back, I think, and that man becomes dear to me."

### Counting the Stars at Night (별 헤는 밤) — if you studied this poem

The speaker looks up at the autumn sky, with no worries, and seems able to count all its stars (stanza 1). He cannot count them all, because each star has been engraved in his heart (stanza 2). Each star stands for a memory, a love, a loneliness, a longing, a poem or his mother (stanza 3). The stars named this way are a <Term id="metaphor"/>: each one carries a word that matters to him. Speaking straight to his mother, who is far away in Buk-Gando, is an <Term id="apostrophe"/> (stanzas 4 and 6). Near the end he writes his name on a hill, covers it with earth, and hopes that spring will make the grass grow thick over it, "like something to be proud of" (stanzas 7–9). The key images are the autumn sky, the stars and the grass over the buried name. Themes: Identity, because the stars are what make him who he is, and Freedom & Control, because he chooses to bury his own name and hopes that spring will change it. Concepts: identity and culture, through names, words and a mother far away.

**Korean, stanza 4, line 1:** 어머님, 나는 별 하나에 아름다운 말 한마디씩 불러 봅니다. Our literal translation: "Mother, I call each star by a beautiful word."

**Korean, stanza 6, line 2:** 그리고 당신은 멀리 북간도에 계십니다. Our literal translation: "and you are far away in Buk-Gando."

### An Easily Written Poem (쉽게 씌어진 시) — if you studied this poem

The speaker is a student far from home, in a six-mat room that he calls "not my country". The night rain whispers outside the window (stanza 1). He holds a tuition envelope that someone has sent him, warm with the smell of sweat and love, and goes to hear an old professor's lecture (stanzas 3–4). He asks what he is hoping for, and whether he is only sinking alone (stanza 6). Then he judges his own work: life is hard to live, yet a poem written this easily is a shameful thing (stanza 7). The poem is dated June 1942. In the class file it is titled A Poem Written Easily. The key images are the night rain, the six-mat room and the warm envelope. The rain and the room come back in stanza 8, so they work as a <Term id="motif"/>. Calling an easy poem shameful is a <Term id="paradox"/>: the ease that should be a gift is treated as a fault. Themes: Identity, because the speaker doubts himself as a poet, and Freedom & Control, because he depends on money someone sends him, in a room that does not feel like his own. Concepts: identity and creativity, since the poem is about the act of writing.

**Korean, stanza 7, lines 2–3:** 시가 이렇게 쉽게 씨워지는것은 / 부끄러운 일이다. Our literal translation: "that a poem is written this easily / is a shameful thing."

### Confession (참회록) — if you studied this poem

The speaker looks at his face in a blue-green rusted bronze mirror and asks why it is so shameful (stanza 1). He shortens his confession to one line, "twenty-four years and one month" (stanza 2, line 2), and asks what joy he hoped for while living. He imagines writing another confession and asks why he made such a shameful one at that young age (stanza 3). Night after night he polishes the mirror with his palms and soles (stanza 4). In stanza 5 the mirror shows not his face but "the back of a sad person walking alone under some meteorite". The key images are the rusted mirror, "a relic of some dynasty" (stanza 1, line 3), the meteorite and a figure seen from behind. The mirror works as a <Term id="symbol"/> of self-judgement and of the past, and the rust and the meteorite are <Term id="imagery"/> of time and distance. Themes: Truth & Deception, because he cannot see his own face and sees only a back, and Power, because his face seems to belong to an old dynasty. Concept: perspective, since he sees himself from behind. The poem is dated 1942.

**Korean, stanza 5, lines 2–3:** 슬픈 사람의 뒷모양이 / 거울 속에 나타나 온다. Our literal translation: "the back of a sad person / appears in the mirror."

## Worked example

**The prompt.** Compare how two of these poems show a speaker who sees and judges himself. Refer to the stanza and line for each reference.

**Plan (optional).**

<Steps>
<Step>Thesis: in both poems the self becomes an image that can be seen and judged, so identity depends on perspective.</Step>
<Step>Point 1, *Self-Portrait*: the well keeps the same image while the feeling changes (stanza 2, line 2; stanza 4, line 2).</Step>
<Step>Point 2, *Confession*: the mirror shows a back that the speaker cannot recognise as his own face (stanza 5, lines 2–3).</Step>
<Step>Point 3, the difference: the water shows a feeling that moves, while the mirror shows a truth he cannot face.</Step>
</Steps>

**Model paragraph.**

> In *Self-Portrait* the speaker goes to a well and meets a man whom he first hates and then finds dear (stanza 2, line 2; stanza 4, line 2). The image in the water never changes, so the judgement depends on his feeling, and the self he judges is unstable. In *Confession* the speaker also judges his own face, but the mirror shows him something he did not ask for: "the back of a sad person" (stanza 5, lines 2–3: 슬픈 사람의 뒷모양이 / 거울 속에 나타나 온다). He cannot see his face, so his judgement rests on a view he cannot check. Both poems turn the self into an image that can be looked at from outside. The difference lies in the kind of image: the well shows a man who stays the same while feelings move, and the mirror shows a sad figure the speaker can see only from behind.

**Why it works.** The paragraph makes one comparison and carries it to the end. Each reference has its stanza and line, and it quotes only one pair of Korean lines; the other references use our own translation.

## How it is examined

- **Poem and play.** A play gives you dialogue, entrances, exits and stage directions, and a character may overhear someone else's talk. A poem gives you one lyric voice, the stanza, the line break and the repetition, with no stage directions. When you compare the two, say where the meaning comes from in each: the speaker's words and images in the poem, the speech and the staging in the play.
- **Poem and non-fiction.** A non-fiction text has a narrator or author who reports, and its evidence is interviews and documents that can be checked. A poem's speaker is a created voice, not a reporter. Use the poet's life as context, not as proof of what the speaker feels.
- **The form decides the evidence.** Name the form in each point: a stanza and line in a poem, a speech and a stage direction in a play, a narrator's report or a document in non-fiction.

## Common mistakes

- Presenting the cause of death as proven. The sources say it is not established, and the theories about injections and experiments are not confirmed.
- Using the 1946 date in the school file for *Counting the Stars at Night*. The 1948 edition and other sources give 1941.
- Quoting the English translation from the school file without a stanza and line. The file has no page numbers, so cite the stanza and line.
- Reading all five poems only as patriotic. Here they are about names, shame, memory and self-judgement.
- Calling *Self-Portrait* a diary. The speaker is a created voice who looks at "that man" in the water, and the poem describes one scene at a well.

<Callout kind="mistake">A quotation with no stanza and line is a claim without evidence: always give the stanza and line, and say which translation you use.</Callout>
