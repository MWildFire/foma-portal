## Explanation

**How to read references.** K1 means line 1 of the Korean original as transcribed in the linked 1948 edition; C1 means line 1 of the English class anthology, counting explicit line breaks, not screen wraps. Titles, dates and blank lines do not count. Long prose-like lines count as one. K and C can differ because translators split, combine or reorder lines. Each quotation below is **our literal translation**, not the unnamed class translation. Use the C locator to find your studied wording. Translation can change pronouns, rhythm and stanza breaks: analyse the edition you actually use.

**Context, not a decoding key.** Yun Dong-ju wrote in Korean under Japanese colonial rule. The collection appeared after his death, in 1948. Read shame, divided identity and sacrifice through the images and voice before adding national context. A lyric persona is a constructed speaker; it is not automatic evidence of what the author personally felt. Source texts: [Korean Wikisource, 1948 edition](https://ko.wikisource.org/wiki/하늘과_바람과_별과_시_(1948년)). Date variants for Self-Portrait and Confession were checked against the [1955 edition](https://ko.wikisource.org/wiki/하늘과_바람과_별과_시_(1955년)).

### Likely your three — selection unconfirmed

#### **The Road — if not IO**

**Date and situation:** September 1941; the source prints an impossible 31 September, so the day is uncertain. Written under Japanese colonial rule, the poem stages a search for something unnamed; reading that loss as national identity is an interpretation, not an explicit statement.

**Structure and voice:** 16 lines in seven stanzas (4/2/2/2/2/2/2); the class also has 16 lines. A searching speaker moves from uncertainty (K1–4) through the wall and gate (K5–10) to shame before the sky (K11–12). K14 reveals a self left beyond the wall. Repeated reasons in K13–16 turn walking into a purpose without showing recovery.

**Images and methods:** the repeated stones make the barrier feel endless; morning/evening reversal makes time circular. The empty road and the withheld lost object invite both psychological and colonial readings. Sky, tears and the divided self connect identity with self-judgement; the poem does not identify a specific guilty act.

**Short evidence — our literal translation:**
- K1 → C1: “I have lost [something].” The object remains unstated in Korean.
- K5 → C5–6: “stone and stone and stone, endlessly joined”. Repetition makes obstruction material.
- K12 → C12: “the sky is shamefully blue”. The colour triggers the speaker's shame.
- K14 → C14: “because I remain beyond the wall”. The searcher and the lost self occupy different sides.

**Pair it:** with *Much Ado* 4.1, compare a self separated by a wall with Hero separated from her public reputation; metaphor and public accusation create different barriers. With *Into the Wild*, compare this unresolved search with the flooded-car episode (chapter 4; Summer Reading Work, p. 11). A symbolic wall differs from a physical flood and the choices about official help; neither proves freedom impossible.

[Original](https://ko.wikisource.org/wiki/하늘과_바람과_별과_시_(1948년)/길).

#### **Self-Portrait**

**Date and situation:** 1939. The 1948 transcription dates it February; the 1955 edition gives September. Keep the year, not an unqualified month. The scene is a solitary visit to a well, within Yun's colonial-era writing; no official or explicit national conflict appears in the scene.

**Structure and voice:** six verse paragraphs, eight long explicit lines in the archived 1948 setting; the class has fourteen lines in five blocks. The final paragraph repeats the landscape of the second. Korean often omits the grammatical subject; the class translation uses “he”. Describe a solitary observer and a reflected man, not an obligatory first-person pronoun in your English edition.

**Images and methods:** the well frames a miniature sky; the unchanged reflection contrasts with the observer's hatred, pity and longing. Repeated departure and return enact an unsettled judgement, not a final cure. The last image adds memory to the landscape: the reflection is both present and emotionally distant. Identity and self-reproach are direct concerns; colonial alienation is a contextual reading that needs this textual evidence.

**Short evidence — our literal translation:**
- K3 → C6: “And there is a man.” The indefinite figure distances the self.
- K4 → C7: “somehow that man becomes hateful”. The reason is withheld.
- K5 → C8–9: “that man becomes pitiable”. The return finds no change in him.
- K7 → C11: “I come to miss that man”. Longing is more precise than resolved affection.
- K8 → C12–14: “a man is there like a memory”. The ending adds a simile.

**Pair it:** with Benedick's overhearing in *Much Ado* 2.3, compare a judgement changing around a stable image with a belief changed by staged speech. With Krakauer's self-assessment in *Into the Wild* chapter 15 (Summer Reading Work, pp. 11–12), compare judgement altered by distance. The lyric repeats observation; the non-fiction passage uses hindsight. Neither perspective is automatically objective.

[Original](https://ko.wikisource.org/wiki/하늘과_바람과_별과_시_(1948년)/자화상).

#### **The Liver**

**Date and situation:** 29 November 1941. The poem combines the Korean hare-and-turtle story with Prometheus. In the class note the hare escapes an attempt to take its liver for the Dragon King. The poem deliberately puts the hare in the Caucasus, mixing traditions; do not correct its hybrid myth into a conventional retelling.

**Structure and voice:** thirteen lines in six stanzas (2/2/2/2/2/3); the class has fifteen lines in six blocks. Invitations to protect the liver (K1–4) turn into an address to the eagle that consumes it (K5–8), then refusal of the turtle (K9–10) and a lament for Prometheus (K11–13). The “let us” voice shifts to “I” and direct addresses.

**Images and methods:** the vulnerable organ links bodily integrity with conscience. Guarding it yet feeding it to an eagle creates a paradox: the speaker refuses one demand while accepting another cost. Apostrophe makes the eagle, turtle and Prometheus contrasting interlocutors. National identity under colonial power is a plausible reading of resistance to the Dragon Palace; it is not a fixed key equating every animal with a named country. The sinking Prometheus is this poem's alteration of the myth.

**Short evidence — our literal translation:**
- K2 → C2: “spread the damp liver to dry”. Exposure makes vulnerability visible.
- K4 → C4: “let us guard the liver”. Circular movement suggests vigilance.
- K6 → C6: “come, tear and eat, without worry”. The command invites the painful act.
- K8 → C8: “I must grow lean; but”. The turn prepares a refusal.
- K10 → C10–11: “never again fall for the Dragon Palace's lure”. Resistance is selective.

**Pair it:** with Beatrice's demand and Benedick's decision in *Much Ado* 4.1, compare ethical loyalty that costs comfort or friendship. With *Into the Wild* chapter 17 (Summer Reading Work, p. 12), compare the symbolic cost with the qualified evaluation of McCandless’s ideals and preparation. Do not equate McCandless's death with deliberate martyrdom, or confuse all suffering with moral achievement.

[Original](https://ko.wikisource.org/wiki/하늘과_바람과_별과_시_(1948년)/간).

### Spares — only if studied and eligible

#### **The Cross**

**Date and situation:** 31 May 1941. The church, cross and named Christ establish a Christian frame within colonial-era Korean writing. The poem imagines a possible sacrifice; it does not recount a crucifixion of its speaker.

**Structure and voice:** fifteen lines in five stanzas (3/2/2/4/4), as in the class line count, though the translation reorders lines. Sunlight reaches the cross; a question makes its height seem inaccessible (K1–5). Waiting and whistling (K6–7) lead to the conditional K11 and the imagined response K12–15. A question gives way to a vow, not to a completed action.

**Images and methods:** vertical distance makes the ideal difficult to reach; the silent bell suspends a call to action. Suffering and happiness coexist in Christ, while blood flowering under a darkening sky combines beauty with violence. The conditional keeps sacrifice open and prevents a simple heroic reading. Colonial resistance is a possible context, not a stated political programme.

**Short evidence — our literal translation:**
- K5 → C5: “how could one climb up?” The question marks distance.
- K7 → C7: “whistling, lingering about”. The pause is ordinary and hesitant.
- K11 → C8: “if a cross were granted”. This is a condition.
- K13 → C14–15: “blood blooming like a flower”. The simile transforms suffering.

**Pair it:** with Benedick's costly choice in *Much Ado* 4.1, distinguish an imagined commitment from a dramatic decision to act. With Krakauer's reflection on risk in *Into the Wild* chapter 15 (Summer Reading Work, pp. 11–12), distinguish a poem's conditional self-offering from a real journey that ended in death. Neither the poem nor the book authorises calling McCandless a willing martyr.

[Original](https://ko.wikisource.org/wiki/하늘과_바람과_별과_시_(1948년)/십자가).

#### **Prologue**

**Date and situation:** 20 November 1941, before the collection's posthumous publication in 1948. The poem presents an ethical aspiration under colonial rule; its sensitivity to shame is not proof of a particular wrongdoing.

**Structure and voice:** nine lines in two stanzas (8/1) in the 1948 source, not three. The class has ten English lines in two DOCX paragraphs (9/1); plain-text extraction does not leave a blank line between them. K1–4 recall the wish for innocence and past distress; K5–8 make future commitments. The last isolated line returns to the stars and wind, leaving vulnerability alongside resolve.

**Images and methods:** the sky provides a measure of conscience; tiny movements of leaves expose extreme sensitivity. Singing stars, loving dying beings and walking a given road bring ideal, compassion and duty together. The final wind touches even the stars: the ideal does not remove disturbance. Modal changes from wishing to deciding structure the voice.

**Short evidence — our literal translation:**
- K2 → C2: “may there be no speck of shame”. It is a wish, not a claim of perfection.
- K4 → C4: “I suffered”. Past distress underlies the promise.
- K6 → C7: “I should love all that is dying”. Duty extends beyond the self.
- K7–8 → C8–9: “the road given to me / I must walk”. Resolve accepts an obligation.

**Pair it:** with Beatrice and Benedick's obligations in *Much Ado* 4.1, compare an inward vow with loyalty tested in dialogue. With *Into the Wild* chapter 4 (Summer Reading Work, p. 11), compare the poem’s accepted obligation with the tension between McCandless’s ideals and institutional rules. The poem's given road complicates the book's chosen journey; neither work reduces freedom to doing anything one wants.

[Original](https://ko.wikisource.org/wiki/하늘과_바람과_별과_시_(1948년)/서시).

### Earlier poems — optional, if studied

#### **Counting the Stars at Night**

Composed **1941-11-05**; the collection was published in **1948**. The class file's **1946** must not be used as its composition date. Thirty explicit lines in ten source stanzas become thirty lines in eight class blocks. A speaker names memories and absent people through stars, addresses the mother, buries a name, then imagines spring. K5–9 give three reasons counting remains unfinished: approaching morning, another night and unfinished youth; it is not simply an inability to remember. K16 → C16 contains the address to the mother; K23–24 → C23–24 bury the name. K26 → C26, our literal translation: “grieving over a shameful name”. The last four lines make renewal conditional on winter passing, not a completed recovery. Pair the name with Hero's public reputation or McCandless's self-renaming. [Original](https://ko.wikisource.org/wiki/하늘과_바람과_별과_시_(1948년)/별_헤는_밤).

#### **An Easily Written Poem**

Composed **1942-06-03**; the class title is *A Poem Written Easily*. Twenty-one lines and ten stanzas in both versions. Rain and a six-mat room frame a student's isolation, financial dependence and discomfort with writing. K9–10 say the childhood friends have been lost; do not assume he deliberately rejected them. K14–15 → C14–15, our literal translation: “a poem being written so easily / is shameful”. The paradox measures literary ease against difficult life. The lamp and self-handshake in K18–21 shift from shame towards consolation and a hoped-for new age. Compare this divided self with Benedick's reassessment or Krakauer's reflective presence; a handshake with oneself is not reconciliation with everybody. [Original](https://ko.wikisource.org/wiki/하늘과_바람과_별과_시_(1948년)/쉽게_씨워진_시).

#### **Confession**

**1942**; absent from the class anthology, so there is **no C locator**. Sixteen lines in five stanzas. A corroded bronze mirror connects personal shame with historical inheritance. K8–11 project a future confession that will judge the present one; this is not an older speaker already looking back on a completed youthful confession. K14–16 end with a solitary figure. K15–16, our literal translation: “the back of a sad person / appears in the mirror”. Polishing the mirror is an attempted ethical task, not proof that the self is purified. Compare the unstable judgement with the play's revised public story or Krakauer's reconstruction, while distinguishing imagined future revision from retrospective narration. [Original](https://ko.wikisource.org/wiki/하늘과_바람과_별과_시_(1948년)/참회록); the 1948 transcription's erroneous footer is not used as the date.

## Worked example

**Own practice prompt:** How can repeated movement make a search for identity remain unresolved?

**Model of a comparison, not a submission to memorise:** In *The Road*, the morning/evening reversal (K9–10 → C9–10) makes travel circular rather than progressive. The self beyond the wall (K14 → C14) explains why walking continues, but the poem does not cross the barrier. In *Much Ado* 2.3, Benedick's movement between observing and interpreting staged conversation does produce a new conviction. Shakespeare lets the audience know its constructed cause; Yun leaves the lost object unnamed. Thus repetition can expose uncertainty even when a dramatic scene seems to resolve it.

## Common mistakes

- Using an IO text without checking eligibility, or assuming another poem from the same collection is automatically allowed.
- Mixing K and C numbers, or describing the class Self-Portrait as necessarily first-person.
- Calling Prologue three stanzas in the 1948 source; it has two, 8+1 lines.
- Turning the Cross's conditional into an actual sacrifice, or treating Liver's hybrid myth as a factual mistake.
- Reducing every image to a single political meaning, or treating the speaker as the author.
- Quoting the unnamed class translation here. Our translations are study aids; verify your studied wording before quoting it in an answer.
