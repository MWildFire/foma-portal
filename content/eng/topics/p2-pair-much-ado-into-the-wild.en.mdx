## Big idea

Shakespeare's comedy and Krakauer's non-fiction investigation both ask what happens when a person's chosen identity meets the expectations of the community around them. They work well as a Paper 2 pair because their forms differ: a play shows the trick on stage, while a book builds its case through an author who investigates and reports. The comparison is strongest when each point names the technique of its own form.

## Explanation

Both works place a character between a self they choose and a community that has other plans for them. In *Much Ado About Nothing* those plans are a wedding, a reputation and a set of tricks. In *Into the Wild* they are a family's hopes and the rules of society that Christopher McCandless leaves behind.

A play has no narrator. The audience learns through <Term id="stage-directions"/> and overheard talk, and it sees the characters' mistakes before they do, which is <Term id="dramatic-irony"/>. Non-fiction has an author who is present in the text: Krakauer narrates, interviews the people who met McCandless, quotes his letters and appears in the story himself as the <Term id="narrator"/>.

<Callout kind="define">A narrator is the voice that tells the story and controls what the reader learns, and when.</Callout>

**Identity.** Identity is the <Term id="theme"/> that links the pair. Benedick and Beatrice defend images of themselves, the bachelor and the witty woman, until the community's plans overturn them. McCandless introduces himself as Alex and later takes the name Alexander Supertramp, so the book sets the identity he chose against the one his family knew. In both works the chosen self is tested by the people who judge it.

**Power.** Don Pedro arranges the match for Claudio, and Leonato's authority as Hero's father decides how the church scene unfolds: "I charge thee do so, as thou art my child" (4.1.80). In the book the family's power works through expectation. McCandless gives his father an expensive telescope before he leaves, and the author recalls that his own father wanted him to take up medicine (chapters 12 and 15). Nature is a second power in the book: storms force the author to turn back on the Devils Thumb (chapters 14 and 15).

**Freedom and Control.** The Friar's plan takes Hero's story out of her hands. Her apparent death must be kept up, "as it must be so maintained" (4.1.225), so that pity replaces judgement: "Shall be lamented, pitied, and excused" (4.1.227). McCandless chooses freedom from society and loses control over his own fate. Without a map he could not know that cabins stood only a few hours away (chapter 18). The epilogue turns his choice into a warning, with a note urging runaways to contact their families.

**Truth and Deception.** In the play the audience sees the plotters at work, so the gap between belief and fact is the source of dramatic irony. Benedick says "This can be no trick" (2.3.223) and then believes what he has overheard (2.3.226–227). In the book the truth is an investigation. The author first blamed wild sweet pea for the death, scientists found no toxins in the samples, and in chapter 18 he revised the hypothesis to a mould on wild potato seeds. In both works truth comes through partial access.

**Perspective and representation.** The two plots of the play form a <Term id="juxtaposition"/>: the wit of Beatrice and Benedick runs beside the trouble of Hero and Claudio. The book also sets two lives side by side, McCandless's journey and the author's own climb in chapters 14 and 15, and the bus works as a <Term id="symbol"/>, the place where McCandless's body was found. Representation matters in both. Hero's name becomes the accusation, "Is it not Hero? Who can blot that name" (4.1.84–85), while McCandless is represented by witnesses such as Wayne Westerberg (chapter 3) and Ron Franz (chapters 6 and 7).

## Worked example

Six plans follow, one for each of six bank questions. Each gives the thesis, three comparative points with their locations, and the point that earns B2. Use them as a checklist, not as a script to memorise.

**Bank question p2-q02 (Discuss): identity against the community's expectations.** Thesis: in both works the community turns a chosen identity into a public test. Point 1: Benedick's and Beatrice's self-images against the plans for marriage (1.1.241–242; 2.3.230–231), and McCandless's name against his family (chapter 3; chapter 15). Point 2: the judgement of others, Hero's public rejection at the altar (4.1.25–32) and the witnesses who speak about McCandless (chapters 3 and 6). Point 3: the cost of the choice, Hero's reputation after the church scene and McCandless's falling out of touch with his family (chapter 12). B2: each point sets the play's public scene beside the book's family scene.

**Bank question p2-q06 (Discuss): transformation, sudden or gradual.** Thesis: the play makes each change sudden within a scene, though the trick prepares it; the book makes the change gradual and reconstructs it over years. Point 1: Benedick's turn within one scene (2.3.223–231) and Beatrice's in the next (3.1.113–117). Point 2: Hero's public collapse in one scene (4.1.84–87) and her reversal in 5.4 (5.4.63–68). Point 3: McCandless's change over two years, which begins after he learns his father's secret (chapter 12). B2: each point places a sudden turn beside a gradual one.

**Bank question p2-q09 (Discuss): dialogue that exposes a conflict nobody names.** Thesis: in the play dialogue hides love behind wit; in the book the conflict is rebuilt from interviews and letters. Point 1: Beatrice's wit in the exchange with Benedick, where she says she would rather hear her dog bark at a crow than a man swear he loves her (1.1.129–130). Point 2: the church dialogue, where Leonato's command "I charge thee do so, as thou art my child" (4.1.80) makes the conflict over honour public. Point 3: the letter McCandless writes to Ron Franz, urging him to leave his routine for the road (chapters 6 and 7), which shows a conflict about the road that neither man names directly. B2: each point sets what is said beside what is meant.

**Bank question p2-q10 (Evaluate): memory reshapes the past, and the role of time.** Thesis: in the play one scene reshapes Hero's past; in the book reshaping is the author's own work of investigation, and he corrects his own conclusions. Point 1: the Friar's plan to keep Hero's apparent death going (4.1.225–227) makes time an instrument of reputation. Point 2: "She died, my lord, but whiles her slander lived" (5.4.68) shows a slander outliving the person it destroys. Point 3: Krakauer's investigation takes years and changes his theory (chapter 18). Judgement: the play compresses the reshaping into a few scenes; the book shows it as slow work that the author has to correct. B2: compare the speed of the reshaping in both works.

**Bank question p2-q11 (Compare): first-person and third-person perspective.** Thesis: the play has no narrator, so its perspective comes from what characters overhear and what the stage directions show; the book has an author who narrates in the first person and widens the reader's knowledge with other voices. Point 1: Benedick (2.3) and Beatrice (3.1) overhear the plot, so the audience knows what they do not know. Point 2: the first chapter of the book is told through the eyes of a stranger who gives McCandless a lift, before the author appears in the story (chapter 1). Point 3: Krakauer's presence in chapters 14 and 15 limits what he can know of McCandless's mind, while his interviews widen it. B2: each point puts a limit on knowledge beside its widening.

**Bank question p2-q15 (Discuss): failure to communicate as control.** Thesis: in the play Hero's denial has no force because others control the story; in the book McCandless chooses silence towards his family, which is also a form of control. Point 1: Hero's denial, "I talked with no man at that hour, my lord" (4.1.91), against the accusers' testimony (4.1.95). Point 2: Claudio refuses to hear the answer and names Hero publicly (4.1.84–87). Point 3: McCandless calls home less and less and then falls out of touch (chapter 12); the epilogue shows the parents trying to reach runaways through a note. B2: each point ties the ability to speak to who decides the outcome.

## How it is examined

Paper 2 may pair texts in the same or different forms, and this pair uses both a play and a non-fiction book. Each criterion is worth 5 marks, 25 in total, as in the school materials.

- **A.** Define identity and expectation, and name the two plots of each work: the two couples in the play, and the journey and the investigation in the book.
- **B1.** Name two or more techniques in each text and explain their effect: stage directions and overhearing in the play; first-person narration, interviews and documents in the book.
- **B2.** Compare the two works in every point and state the link. A pair chosen for a contrast of form earns B2 most easily when each point names the technique of both forms.
- **C.** A thesis that gives a degree, for example "largely, but the book shows the cost of the choice more fully than the play does", and an order that follows the prompt.
- **D.** Precise terms (dramatic irony, narrator, hypothesis, juxtaposition) and quotations with their location: act, scene and line for the play, chapter for the book.

Quotations from the book are allowed in the exam, but take them from your own copy and give the chapter.

## Common mistakes

- Retelling both works in order and comparing only in the last paragraph.
- Comparing plots (who falls in love, who dies) instead of techniques (overhearing, the narrator's control).
- Reading the book as a novel and forgetting that its author narrates and investigates.
- Stating the cause of McCandless's death as a fact; the book gives a hypothesis and revises it.
- Quoting without a location: act, scene and line for the play; chapter for the book.

## Summary

- The pair shares one question: how a chosen identity meets the expectations of a community.
- The forms differ: a play hides the truth from its characters, while a non-fiction book reconstructs it.
- Identity, power, freedom and control, and truth and deception appear in both works.
- The key contrast is perspective: the play has no narrator, while the book's author narrates and investigates.
- Representation links the two: Hero's name becomes the accusation, and McCandless is represented by others.
- For B2, every point names the technique of both works.
