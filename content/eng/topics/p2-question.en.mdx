## Big idea

Paper 2 rewards an answer to the prompt, not a summary of two works. Criterion A checks that you read the prompt exactly and know both works well enough to answer it.

## Explanation

Read the wording of each prompt: it may include a command term, a phrase asking for a judgement, and key words. Not every prompt contains all three in a fixed form. The IB command-term glossary distinguishes "Compare" (similarities), "Contrast" (differences), and "Compare and contrast" (both). Read any second instruction too. Separately, Paper 2 criterion B2 can reward relevant similarities and/or differences; it requires no quota or balance of both. See the [Language A: Literature guide, command terms, printed p. 71](https://xmltwo.ibo.org/publications/EAD/2026_EN/GR1/GR1-A-Lit_EN_24.pdf#page=77) and [IB 2026 examiner instructions, Paper 2, criterion B2](https://ibpublishing.ibo.org/exinst/apps/exinst/index.html?chapter=1&doc=EX_instructions_2026_e&part=8). "Discuss" asks for a balanced review, with several views and a conclusion you support with evidence. "Evaluate" asks you to weigh strengths and limitations and reach a judgement.

<Callout kind="define">A command term is the verb in a prompt that tells you what kind of answer to write.</Callout>

"To what extent" and "how far" ask for a judgement about degree. The answer is rarely yes or no. Write a position such as "largely, but not entirely", and then show why. "How the writers show" asks you to analyse the means of presentation, including broad choices of structure, characterisation or perspective as well as a specific <Term id="literary-device"/>. Naming a technique in every point is not an exam quota.

Some prompts set two ideas or forces in tension. If yours does, examine their relationship; do not impose this shape on a question that asks something else. A contrast in a question is not itself evidence of <Term id="juxtaposition"/> in either literary work.

Key words carry the meaning of the prompt. Clarify an ambiguous key word when needed to establish your reading; a dictionary-style definition in the first paragraph is not compulsory. Some key words are literary terms. A <Term id="theme"/> is an idea the work explores. A <Term id="motif"/> is a repeated image or idea that develops. A <Term id="symbol"/> carries a meaning beyond its object. <Term id="tone"/> is the attitude expressed by the text or its speaker, and <Term id="connotation"/> is the set of associations a word carries.

If the prompt asks why a writer does something, answer with the <Term id="purpose"/>: the reason, and its effect on the reader.

Knowing a work well enough does not mean retelling it. For each work, prepare three things: the plot shape (the start, the turning point and the ending, one line each); the characters, and how each relates to the key words; and two or three exact references, a short quotation or a precise moment with its location. These references make a claim credible.

An answer also needs an <Term id="argument"/>: a claim about the prompt that the rest of the answer supports. A response that answers a different question is easy to spot. It starts with what happens, moves to what the characters feel, and ends with a general comment. The examiner sees a summary, not an argument, even when the facts are right. The fix is simple: test each point by asking which word of the prompt it answers.

<Callout kind="exam">Accurate plot knowledge helps, but an unrelated summary does not provide a persuasive interpretation of the works in relation to the question. The whole response is assessed; there is no separate pass threshold for one sentence.</Callout>

## Worked example

<Question id="p2-q02"/>

Decode the prompt step by step before you plan anything else.

<Steps>
<Step>**Command term.** "Discuss" asks for a balanced review: several views, the strengths and limits of each, and a conclusion supported by evidence. A list of what happens in each work discusses nothing.</Step>
<Step>**"How two works show".** The verb "show" asks for means. Analyse relevant authorial choices rather than only retell events. There is no fixed count of named techniques.</Step>
<Step>**"The tension between".** The prompt sets two forces side by side. Address their relationship across the argument; not every sentence or paragraph must mechanically repeat both terms.</Step>
<Step>**Key words.** "Identity" is who the character is, to themselves and to others. "Expectations" is what the community asks of the character: a role, a manner of behaviour, a name. Establish how you interpret their relationship; define a term if its ambiguity matters.</Step>
<Step>**"The community that surrounds them".** In each work this is a concrete group: a family, a neighbourhood, a school, a court. Decide which group it is before you plan, so that each point has a real force to push against.</Step>
</Steps>

A response that answers a different question might open like this: "Both works are about a person who wants to be free, and both end sadly." On its own the sentence does not yet develop an interpretation: it answers "what are the works about?" and not "how do they show the tension?" An opening that answers the prompt names both sides and the place where they meet: "In both works the community decides who the character is allowed to be, so identity appears in the gap between that decision and the self."

Before the exam, prepare a card for each work. Invented example: "Start: a woman returns to her village after a scandal. Turning point: the council asks her to change her name. End: she signs the form without speaking." Add two exact references to each card, with their location in the text.

## Visual

| Phrase in the prompt | What it asks for | What your plan needs |
|---|---|---|
| Discuss | a balanced review and a supported conclusion | two views, and a conclusion that weighs them |
| Compare | similarities, with reference to both works | analyse significant similarities explicitly |
| Contrast | differences, with reference to both works | analyse significant differences explicitly |
| Compare and contrast | both similarities and differences | address both as requested; no numerical quota |
| Evaluate | strengths, limits and a judgement | a claim about how well a choice works |
| To what extent, how far | a degree, not yes or no | a phrase such as "largely, but not entirely" |
| How the writer shows | the means: choices, techniques, effects | analysis of how relevant choices shape meaning |
| Why | the writer's purpose and its effect | a claim about purpose, linked to the effect |

## How it is examined

Criterion A (0–5) checks your understanding of the prompt and of both works. The school descriptor rewards perceptive knowledge and persuasive interpretation in relation to the question. The preparation cards and definition suggestions here are methods, not additional descriptors or automatic mark thresholds. Length earns nothing here: an exact answer to the prompt beats a long summary of the works. Each of the five Paper 2 criteria is worth 5 marks, as in the school materials.

## Common mistakes

- Summarising both works before the first point, so the question is not answered until page two.
- Treating "to what extent" as a yes or no question, with no degree in the conclusion.
- Leaving an important ambiguity unresolved so the argument changes meaning without explanation.
- Knowing one work well and the other only from a memory of its plot.
- Giving inaccurate or irrelevant references. Quotations and memorised line numbers are not compulsory in this closed-book paper.

## Summary

- Read the command term first: it sets the task.
- "To what extent" and "how far" ask for a degree, so write a judgement, not a yes or no.
- Clarify important ambiguities and keep the argument responsive to the actual wording.
- Prepare each work as a card: its shape, its characters and two exact references.
- An answer that retells the works answers a different question, even when the facts are right.
- Test each point: which word of the prompt does it answer?
