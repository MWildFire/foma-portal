## Big idea

Paper 2 rewards an answer to the prompt, not a summary of two works. Criterion A checks that you read the prompt exactly and know both works well enough to answer it.

## Explanation

Every prompt has a command term, a judgement phrase and key words. The command term sets the task. "Compare" asks for similarities and differences, with both works in view throughout. "Discuss" asks for a balanced review, with several views and a conclusion you support with evidence. "Evaluate" asks you to weigh strengths and limitations and reach a judgement.

<Callout kind="define">A command term is the verb in a prompt that tells you what kind of answer to write.</Callout>

"To what extent" and "how far" ask for a judgement about degree. The answer is rarely yes or no. Write a position such as "largely, but not entirely", and then show why. "How the writers show" asks for means, so every point must name a choice or a technique, such as a <Term id="literary-device"/>, not only the content.

The prompt also places two forces side by side. That <Term id="juxtaposition"/> is the shape of the answer: each point keeps both sides in view and shows the pull between them.

Key words carry the meaning of the prompt. Define them in your first paragraph, in your own words, so that every following point tests the same terms. Some key words are literary terms. A <Term id="theme"/> is an idea the work explores. A <Term id="motif"/> is a repeated image or idea that develops. A <Term id="symbol"/> carries a meaning beyond its object. <Term id="tone"/> is the writer's attitude, and <Term id="connotation"/> is the set of associations a word carries.

If the prompt asks why a writer does something, answer with the <Term id="purpose"/>: the reason, and its effect on the reader.

Knowing a work well enough does not mean retelling it. For each work, prepare three things: the plot shape (the start, the turning point and the ending, one line each); the characters, and how each relates to the key words; and two or three exact references, a short quotation or a precise moment with its location. These references make a claim credible.

An answer also needs an <Term id="argument"/>: a claim about the prompt that the rest of the answer supports. A response that answers a different question is easy to spot. It starts with what happens, moves to what the characters feel, and ends with a general comment. The examiner sees a summary, not an argument, even when the facts are right. The fix is simple: test each point by asking which word of the prompt it answers.

<Callout kind="exam">A perfect summary of both works cannot pass criterion A if the prompt is not answered.</Callout>

## Worked example

<Question id="p2-q02"/>

Decode the prompt step by step before you plan anything else.

<Steps>
<Step>**Command term.** "Discuss" asks for a balanced review: several views, the strengths and limits of each, and a conclusion supported by evidence. A list of what happens in each work discusses nothing.</Step>
<Step>**"How two works show".** The verb "show" asks for means. Each point must name a choice or a technique, not only the content.</Step>
<Step>**"The tension between".** The prompt sets two forces side by side. Keep both in every point, and show the pull between them.</Step>
<Step>**Key words.** "Identity" is who the character is, to themselves and to others. "Expectations" is what the community asks of the character: a role, a manner of behaviour, a name. Define both in your first paragraph.</Step>
<Step>**"The community that surrounds them".** In each work this is a concrete group: a family, a neighbourhood, a school, a court. Decide which group it is before you plan, so that each point has a real force to push against.</Step>
</Steps>

A response that answers a different question might open like this: "Both works are about a person who wants to be free, and both end sadly." The examiner gives criterion A a low mark, because the sentence answers "what are the works about?" and not "how do they show the tension?" An opening that answers the prompt names both sides and the place where they meet: "In both works the community decides who the character is allowed to be, so identity appears in the gap between that decision and the self."

Before the exam, prepare a card for each work. Invented example: "Start: a woman returns to her village after a scandal. Turning point: the council asks her to change her name. End: she signs the form without speaking." Add two exact references to each card, with their location in the text.

## Visual

| Phrase in the prompt | What it asks for | What your plan needs |
|---|---|---|
| Discuss | a balanced review and a supported conclusion | two views, and a conclusion that weighs them |
| Compare | similarities and differences in both works | every point names both works |
| Evaluate | strengths, limits and a judgement | a claim about how well a choice works |
| To what extent, how far | a degree, not yes or no | a phrase such as "largely, but not entirely" |
| How the writer shows | the means: choices, techniques, effects | a named technique in each point |
| Why | the writer's purpose and its effect | a claim about purpose, linked to the effect |

## How it is examined

Criterion A (0–10) checks your understanding of the prompt and of both works. The top band needs defined key words, both works known in their shape, characters and references, and an answer that stays on the question from the first sentence to the last. Length earns nothing here: an exact answer to the prompt beats a long summary of the works. Each of the five Paper 2 criteria is worth 5 marks.

## Common mistakes

- Summarising both works before the first point, so the question is not answered until page two.
- Treating "to what extent" as a yes or no question, with no degree in the conclusion.
- Defining the key words only in the conclusion, or not at all.
- Knowing one work well and the other only from a memory of its plot.
- Quoting without a location, or without a link to the prompt.

## Summary

- Read the command term first: it sets the task.
- "To what extent" and "how far" ask for a degree, so write a judgement, not a yes or no.
- Define the key words in your first paragraph, and keep them in every point.
- Prepare each work as a card: its shape, its characters and two exact references.
- An answer that retells the works answers a different question, even when the facts are right.
- Test each point: which word of the prompt does it answer?
