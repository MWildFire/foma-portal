## Big idea

In a literary text, creativity is an effect on the reader: the writer makes the familiar strange, and the reader has to work out what the new way of seeing asks of them.

## Explanation

Creativity is a key concept about two sides of one act. The writer's side is craft: choosing a form, an image or a structure that makes the reader see something anew. The reader's side is response: the point where a text does not behave as expected, and the reader has to change how they read in order to make sense of it. In an essay, write about the second side. Do not praise the author as "creative". Explain what the text does to the reader.

Begin with <Term id="defamiliarisation"/>. Viktor Shklovsky called this technique *ostranenie* in 1917: the writer describes something ordinary as if it were new, so the reader notices what habit had hidden.

<Callout kind="define">Defamiliarisation is the technique of describing something familiar so that it seems strange, and the reader sees it again.</Callout>

Fresh images are the most direct way to do this. A new image links two things the reader would not expect to link, and the link tells the reader something about both. An image that returns at key points becomes a <Term id="motif"/>. An image that stands for a larger idea becomes a <Term id="symbol"/>.

Creative writing also works with convention. Every form sets expectations: a sonnet builds towards a turn, a fairy tale promises a rescue, a letter has a greeting and a signature. A writer can use a <Term id="literary-device"/> to meet an expectation or to break it. A break is creative when the reader has to notice it, rebuild the rule that was broken, and decide why the writer broke it. A <Term id="paradox"/> is a small version of this: a statement that seems to contradict itself and still points at a truth. An <Term id="oxymoron"/> sets two contradictory words side by side, so the reader must hold both meanings at once.

A writer can also create by pointing backwards. An <Term id="allusion"/> asks the reader to recognise an earlier text. The creative move lies in the difference: the new text takes an old story and reframes it, so the reader compares the two and sees both differently.

The reader's job is practical. An unusual form slows the reading down. Ask three questions. What does the form make me expect? What happens when that expectation fails? What effect does the delay produce: surprise, unease, humour, recognition or a new understanding? The answers are the creativity you should write about.

## Worked example

Read this original paragraph. It was written for this lesson and quotes no published text.

Every morning the kettle announced itself with a thin complaint, rose to a roar and fell silent. I had heard this sound for years without listening to it. Today it seemed to clear its throat before it spoke, as if the water had been saving something all night and had finally decided to say it.

<Steps>
<Step>**Name the familiar object.** A kettle is something the reader sees every day and never notices. The paragraph starts from routine, which is where defamiliarisation begins.</Step>
<Step>**Find the strange move.** The kettle is given a voice: it seems to "clear its throat" before it speaks. This is personification, a literary device that makes an object act like a person.</Step>
<Step>**Explain the effect.** The reader stops hearing a machine and starts hearing a speaker. The sound itself is ordinary, so the strangeness comes from a new frame that the reader must accept before the paragraph makes sense.</Step>
<Step>**Link the effect to the question.** The paragraph makes the reader pay attention to routine. The writer's creative act is to make a repeated sound feel like news.</Step>
</Steps>

## Visual

| Convention | How it can be broken | Effect on the reader |
|---|---|---|
| A sonnet builds towards a turn (volta) | The turn comes late, or the poem never resolves | The reader expects closure and feels it withheld, so the poem seems deliberately open |
| A fairy tale promises a rescue | The heroine acts for herself | The reader must revise the story they know and decide who holds the power |
| A letter has a greeting and a signature | The letter has no signature, or is addressed to whoever finds it | The reader becomes part of the text and must decide who the letter is for |
| A narrative runs in time order | The narrator begins at the end | Suspense moves from what happens to why it happens |

## How it is examined

Creativity can be the subject of a question, or it can frame your answer to any question about a writer's choices. In both cases the examiner rewards an explanation of the effect on the reader: the expectation the text sets up, the way it breaks or uses that expectation, and what the reader must do next. Command terms set the depth. "Identify" and "State" ask you to name a device or a convention. "Explain" and "Analyse" ask for the effect and its link to the question. "Evaluate" asks how well the break works, which needs a judgement supported by a quotation.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">The most common error is to praise the author instead of describing the effect on the reader.</Callout>

- Writing "Shakespeare is very creative" instead of explaining what a choice does.
- Naming a device without naming the convention it breaks or uses.
- Treating unusual as automatically good: a break needs an effect that you can explain.
- Describing a strange image literally, without saying what the reader notices or feels.
- Using "creative" or "unique" as filler words that add no analysis.

## Summary

- Creativity, as a key concept, is an effect on the reader as well as a quality of the writer.
- Defamiliarisation makes the familiar strange, so that the reader sees it again.
- Fresh images, motifs and symbols carry a new way of seeing through the whole text.
- A broken convention makes the reader rebuild the rule and ask why it was broken.
- Allusion creates meaning by reframing an earlier text; paradox and oxymoron hold two meanings together.
- Always finish with the effect: what the reader must notice, feel or work out.
