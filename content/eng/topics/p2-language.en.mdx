## Big idea

Criterion D rewards an essay whose sentences say exactly what the writer means: precise literary vocabulary, the present tense for analysis, and short quotations built into the grammar of your own sentences.

## Explanation

Criterion D, language, is worth 5 of the 25 marks in Paper 2. The examiner reads your language for three things: precise vocabulary, accurate grammar and tense, and quotations that belong to your sentences. The register should be formal and clear, and the sentences should be easy to follow on one reading.

Choose words that name the exact action or quality. Vague words such as "good", "nice", "shows" and "very" tell the examiner very little. Replace them with words that carry the meaning: "persuasive", "unsettling", "suggests", "insists". Name the writer's <Term id="diction"/> when the choice of words matters, and then say what that choice does. A simple test helps: if a word could describe any text, it is too vague. "Interesting" fits every novel, while "unsettling" fits only a text that really unsettles its reader.

Words also carry a <Term id="connotation"/>: the feelings and associations beyond their dictionary sense. Two words for the same action can differ in connotation, and that difference is often the argument. When a word choice is part of your point, explain its connotation, not only its literal meaning.

Write about the works in the present tense: the narrator hides, not hid, because the work exists now. Use the past tense only for events inside the story. Keep sentences short and clear, with one idea each and the subject and verb close together. A sentence that runs past three lines usually holds two ideas, so split it and give each idea its own evidence. Signpost each step with a short phrase, such as "this suggests" or "the effect of this is", so that the examiner can follow the line of thought. Keep the <Term id="register"/> of the essay formal and precise: no slang, no contractions, no "a lot" or "stuff".

<Callout kind="define">An integrated quotation is a short, exact quotation that is built into your own sentence, so that the sentence reads as one grammatical unit.</Callout>

## Worked example

The sentence below is invented for this example. It names no real work, so the quotation in it is invented too.

**Weak version:** The writer shows that the narrator is very sad. He says "I was alone", which proves it.

**Precise version:** The narrator's flat admission, "I was, I suppose, alone", is an <Term id="understatement"/>: the calm words suggest a loneliness too painful to name directly.

<Steps>
<Step>**Replace the vague verb.** "Shows" becomes "suggests", because the reader infers the sadness rather than being told it. "Proves" claims more than a reading can establish, so it goes.</Step>
<Step>**Remove the vague intensifier.** "Very sad" gives the examiner no sense of the feeling. The precise version names its form: calm words that hide pain. The word "very" is no longer needed.</Step>
<Step>**Integrate the quotation.** In the weak version the quotation stands outside the sentence and the sentence needs a second sentence to explain it. In the precise version the quoted words sit inside the grammar of one sentence, exact and in the writer's order.</Step>
<Step>**Keep the <Term id="syntax"/> clear.** The precise sentence has one subject, the narrator's admission, and one main verb, "is". The examiner can follow the idea at a single reading.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Paper 2 is worth 25 marks, and criterion D, language, is worth 5 of them. Examiners credit an essay that uses precise literary vocabulary, keeps the present tense for analysis, integrates short quotations and uses terms accurately. Prompts ask you to compare, discuss or evaluate, so your verbs must carry the judgement: "suggests", "undermines", "argues". A verb such as "is about" carries no judgement at all.

<Callout kind="exam">Criterion D rewards accuracy: a precise word, an integrated quotation and a correctly used term earn more than a long sentence full of vague words.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">A quotation that stands outside the sentence is not integrated, however accurate it is.</Callout>

- Using "good", "nice", "shows" and "very" as the main words of evaluation.
- Writing the analysis in the past tense, as in "the narrator hid", instead of "the narrator hides", except for events inside the story.
- Dropping a full line of text into the essay and explaining it afterwards, when a short phrase built into your own sentence would do the work better.
- Naming a technique, such as understatement, without saying what it does to the reader.

## Summary

- Replace vague words such as "good", "nice", "shows" and "very" with precise vocabulary.
- Write the analysis in the present tense, and keep the tense steady within each sentence.
- Build short, exact quotations into your own sentences, so that each sentence reads as one unit.
- Explain the connotation of a word when the choice of word is part of your argument.
- Use a literary term accurately, and say what it does in the same sentence.
- Keep the register formal, precise and easy to follow on one reading.
- Signpost each step of the analysis, so that the examiner can follow the line of thought.
