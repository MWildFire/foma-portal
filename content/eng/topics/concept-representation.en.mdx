## Big idea

Representation is the choice a writer makes in showing people, groups, places and ideas, and a reader can evaluate that choice. Every text shows some people clearly and leaves others outside the frame.

## Explanation

Representation is a key concept because a text never shows a group neutrally. Every representation is built from choices: who is shown, who speaks, what is selected and what is left out. The reader can test those choices against the text, which is why a representation can be questioned as well as admired.

<Callout kind="define">Representation: the choices a text makes in showing people, groups, places and ideas, and the effect those choices have on how the reader sees them.</Callout>

Start with who is shown and who speaks. A text can give a group a voice, or it can show that group only through an outsider's eyes. <Term id="focalisation"/> decides whose perception the reader shares: a character's inner view, an outsider's view or a wider view. An <Term id="omniscient-narrator"/> can move between many minds and so present a group from several sides, but it can also deliver one judgement from above. An <Term id="unreliable-narrator"/> tells the story in a way the reader cannot fully trust, so the representation itself becomes a subject for questions.

Next, look at what is selected and what is left out. If a text gives a group three habits and no work, no family and no doubts, the omissions are as much a choice as the details. Stereotype and complexity are the two poles to test. A stereotype fixes a group in one trait and repeats it. A complex representation gives individuals who contradict the type, so the reader meets people rather than a label. Neither is automatically good: a stereotype may be a deliberate distortion, and a complex portrait may still hide what matters.

Images are the third tool. <Term id="imagery"/> builds a place or a group through the senses: the colour of a street, the way people stand, the sound of a market. A <Term id="symbol"/> carries a larger idea, so one object can stand for a whole community. Images can be fair or unfair: they can make a group look dignified or dangerous, and the reader should ask which effect the writer wants.

Finally, every representation has an <Term id="audience"/>: the readers the writer imagines. That audience shapes what is explained, what is assumed and what is praised or criticised. When you question a representation, ask four things: who is given a voice, whose view is missing, what the text leaves out, and what the writer gains by presenting the group this way. A strong answer tests the representation with evidence from the text, not with a general opinion about the group.

## Worked example

Both versions below describe the same group: night-shift cleaners in an office block. Both are original. The first is written from outside the group, the second from inside it.

Outside: "The cleaners arrive at ten, when the lights are going out: a silent line of grey overalls and tired faces. They push their trolleys past the empty desks, humming to themselves and never complaining, the way people do when they have nothing left to ask for."

Inside: "I come in at ten, when the lights are going out on the corner offices and their owners have gone home. My feet hurt before I start. I know every floor: the fourth smells of coffee, the sixth of printer toner. I am saving for my daughter's school trip, so I count the bins the way other people count money."

<Steps>
<Step>**Identify the two viewpoints.** Both versions describe the same group. In the first, an observer stands outside it and speaks of "the cleaners" in the third person. In the second, one cleaner speaks with "I".</Step>
<Step>**Technique one: point of view.** The <Term id="focalisation"/> of the outside version turns the group into a type, and the closing generalisation, "the way people do", settles the question before the reader can ask it. The inside version lets the reader share one person's perception and learn a motive: saving for a school trip.</Step>
<Step>**Technique two: detail.** The outside version uses imagery that makes the workers one shape: "a silent line of grey overalls". The inside version selects sensory detail, "the fourth smells of coffee, the sixth of printer toner", so that each floor, and the person who works on it, has a history.</Step>
<Step>**Show what each version does.** The first represents the cleaners as a stereotype: quiet, uncomplaining and asking for nothing. The second represents them as complex people with a body, a goal and a sharp mind. Both are choices. The reader can ask which one the writer needs, and what the other version leaves out.</Step>
</Steps>

## Visual

| Question | Where to look | Stereotype or complexity |
|---|---|---|
| Who is shown? | Names, details, the range of people in the text | One type repeated is a stereotype; named people with mixed traits show complexity |
| Who speaks? | The narrator, the focaliser, or the group's own voice | An outsider speaking for the group invites a stereotype; the group speaking for itself shows more complexity |
| What is left out? | Work, family, doubts, power and the group's own reasons | Missing sides that the text never explains are a choice you can question |

## How it is examined

Representation can appear in Paper 1 when the guiding question asks how a text presents people, a group or an idea, and in Paper 2 when two works show a group in different ways. A strong answer does three things: it says who is shown and who speaks; it names the technique that builds the representation, such as focalisation, the narrator, imagery or symbol; and it judges, with evidence, how far the representation is complex or stereotyped. Command terms such as evaluate and discuss ask for a judgement that weighs evidence on both sides. A short command such as identify asks for the choice and the place in the text where it appears.

<Callout kind="exam">A claim about representation earns marks only when a quotation or a technique supports it.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Do not treat a representation as the truth about a group. A text is one choice among others, and the question is how that choice works.</Callout>

- Treating the text's view of a group as the real view of that group, instead of asking who is speaking.
- Calling a representation a stereotype without a quotation or a technique to show it.
- Ignoring the narrator: writing "the author shows" when the text is told through a character with a limited view.
- Writing about the group in general terms, such as "the poor" or "the young", instead of the specific people and details the text chooses.

## Summary

- Representation is the choice a text makes in showing people, groups, places and ideas.
- Ask who is shown, who speaks, what is selected and what is left out.
- Focalisation and the narrator decide whose view the reader shares.
- A stereotype fixes a group in one trait; a complex representation gives individuals who contradict the type.
- Imagery and symbol build the image of a place or a group through detail.
- Question a representation: who gains from it, whose view is missing, and what the audience is expected to believe.
