## Big idea

The seven course concepts are useful lenses for reading a Paper 2 question. Start with the actual question: it does not require you to assign exactly two concept labels or to define them in the opening paragraph.

## Explanation

Identity, culture, creativity, communication, perspective, transformation and representation connect different parts of the course. A question may involve several of them. The words and relationships in the question decide what your answer must address; our labels do not replace those words.

For example, a question about how a village treats a stranger may invite a cultural reading, an analysis of representation, or both. A convincing cultural argument can analyse the writers' choices. It is not automatically a misreading because this portal assigned representation as its first tag.

Use a concept to generate a question about the writing. For identity, ask how a character defines themselves and how others define them. For perspective, distinguish who speaks from whose knowledge or perceptions restrict the account. For transformation, consider not only changes within the story but also adaptation, translation, relationships between texts and changes in readers' understanding. Communication includes the relationship between a work and its readers as well as exchanges between characters. These are starting points, not exhaustive definitions.

<Callout kind="define">A concept is an interpretative lens. An answer still needs accurate references, analysis and a response to the question.</Callout>

## Worked example

Invented prompt: *Discuss how two works represent a family whose traditions are being lost. To what extent does communication between generations contribute to that loss?*

<Steps>
<Step>**Identify the task.** Compare the portrayal of the family and assess the contribution of communication. Do not answer only with a list of traditions.</Step>
<Step>**Try useful lenses.** Representation and communication are useful here; culture and transformation may also help. There is no uniquely required pair of labels.</Step>
<Step>**Test a possible claim.** In invented Work A, a grandmother teaches a recipe through gestures and it survives. In invented Work B, adults forbid children to join the preparation and the recipe is forgotten. Non-verbal teaching is communication too: the contrast is participation versus exclusion.</Step>
<Step>**Plan the analysis.** Compare how a narrated sequence of gestures in A and interrupted dialogue in B present that contrast. A provisional thesis is: both families value continuity, but A presents participation as a means of preserving it, while B shows how exclusion can defeat the elders' stated aim. Check other causes before deciding the degree of communication's contribution.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

The school booklet allocates 5 marks each to A, B1, B2, C and D. A concerns knowledge and interpretation in relation to the question; B1 concerns how authorial choices shape meaning; B2 concerns comparative analysis. No mark is awarded merely for naming a concept. There is no requirement to define exactly two concepts in paragraph one. Explain an ambiguous key word when that helps establish your interpretation, then support the argument throughout the answer.

## Common mistakes

- Answering a familiar topic instead of the particular question.
- Treating our first and second tags as an official hierarchy.
- Listing cultural customs without analysing how the works present them.
- Assuming that naming a concept supplies evidence or earns a mark.
- Predicting the next paper from the archive's tag frequencies.

## Summary

- Read the command and all important relationships in the actual question.
- Use any helpful concepts; exactly two labels are not compulsory.
- Turn a lens into an analytical question, then test a claim against both works.
- Support your reading with precise references and authorial choices.
- Archive tags describe this portal's interpretation, not IB requirements or predictions.
