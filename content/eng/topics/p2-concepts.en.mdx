## Big idea

Every Paper 2 prompt leans on a key concept, and often on two. Name them before you plan anything: the concept tells you what the examiner will look for in every point you write.

## Explanation

The course names seven key concepts: identity, culture, creativity, communication, perspective, transformation and representation. The examiners do not tag their prompts with them. We therefore read the 52 prompts of the thirteen papers from May 2023 to November 2025 and named the concept each prompt leads with and the one it supports. These are our own readings, not an IB figure, but the pattern is clear enough to plan around.

The main concept is the one the prompt asks you to show. It is the object of the verb, as in "represent a family". The second concept qualifies, explains or tests the main one. It usually comes after "through", "by" or "to what extent". Write both into your plan before you choose any quotation.

A concept is not a topic. "Identity" does not mean "a character who is unsure of themselves". It is the question of who a person is, how they define themselves and how others define them. A concept gives your points the question they must answer, so a point that does not touch the concept is a detail, not evidence.

The two concepts must work together in the thesis. Representation with culture asks what the writers show of a group's shared rules. Representation with communication asks how the writers let a message travel, and where it breaks. A thesis that names only one concept leaves the other one unanswered.

Representation leads most often, and culture supports most often. This is the most useful pattern in the data. If a prompt names a group, a place or a custom, ask what the writers show about it. Culture is usually the context that gives another concept its force, so it rarely leads.

## Worked example

Invented prompt, for the method only: *Discuss how two works represent a family whose traditions are being lost. To what extent does the way the generations communicate drive that loss?*

<Steps>
<Step>**Main concept: representation.** The family is what the writers show. "Discuss" asks for a balanced view, so each work has to show the loss in its own way.</Step>
<Step>**Second concept: communication.** "The way the generations communicate" is the force the prompt tests. "To what extent" asks for a degree: communication may be one cause among several.</Step>
<Step>**The content the lenses act on.** "Traditions" is culture, and "being lost" is transformation. Neither leads, but both must appear in your points, because the prompt is about how a tradition changes.</Step>
<Step>**A claim.** Largely, but not entirely. The way the generations speak to each other matters, and so does the form in which a tradition is passed on.</Step>
</Steps>

A point for this plan, with invented works: in the first work a recipe is passed from grandmother to child without any words, through gestures, and it survives. In the second, the same recipe is taught as a lesson, and it becomes an exhibit. The representation is the difference: a living practice in one work, a display in the other. So communication is one cause of the loss, and the form of transmission is another.

## How it is examined

Criterion A (0–5) rewards a prompt read with both concepts in view. The top band defines each concept in the first paragraph, in your own words, follows both through every point and answers the degree the prompt asks for. An answer that names a concept only in the introduction loses marks. So does an answer that reads a prompt about representation as a question about culture. A concept earns nothing by name; the points that show it earn the marks.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a concept as a topic: "identity is about a girl who is not sure of herself".
- Reading only the main concept and ignoring the second one, which often holds the argument.
- Writing the same answer to every prompt on one concept. The verb and the second concept change the task.
- Leading with culture when the prompt leads with representation or communication.
- Defining the concept in the conclusion, not in the first paragraph.
