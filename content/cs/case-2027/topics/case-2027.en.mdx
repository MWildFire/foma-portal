## Big idea

Section B asks you to apply the whole course to one real system. The 2027 case is about generative AI for images. A design company compares models that create pictures from a text prompt, from a given input such as a label or a sketch, or from random noise. You read the scenario, choose the concepts that explain it and judge the trade-offs.

## Explanation

**What section B is.** On higher level, section B is worth 24 of the 80 marks of Paper 1. You answer one question on the case study, in parts (a) to (d). The last part is the longest answer.

**What the case covers.** The company wants to produce pictures more efficiently for ads, concept art and digital media. It compares three families of models: diffusion models, GANs and hybrid models. Diffusion models and the ethics are for both levels. GANs and hybrid models are for higher level only.

- **Ways to generate images.** *Text-to-image* generation turns a prompt into a picture; DALL·E and Stable Diffusion are the examples given. *Conditional* generation follows a given input, such as a class label (for example "landscape"), a sketch or a segmentation map; image-to-image translation, such as black-and-white to colour, is one conditional task. *Unconditional* generation uses no input and relies on patterns learned from the training data; it suits exploratory art and synthetic datasets.
- **Diffusion models** (PDF page 4, lines 35–46, and Figure 1). The model starts from random noise, which is called noise injection. A denoising network, usually a CNN, removes the noise in several steps. Figure 1 shows eight denoising steps. The content depends on the training data: a model trained on animal photographs produces animals, and one trained on city photographs produces urban scenes. Results can be photorealistic, but the many steps need a lot of computing, so optimisation matters. Modern models are built on the DDPM (denoising diffusion probabilistic model), which describes adding noise during training and removing it during generation.
- **GANs** (higher level only; PDF page 5, lines 48–59, and Figure 2). A generator maps a D-dimensional noise vector to an image. A discriminator compares the image with real images. The two networks are trained against each other: each improves in response to the other, which is the adversarial dynamic. This gives sharp images, but training is sensitive to instability and to mode collapse, in which outputs lose most of their variety.
- **Hybrid models** (higher level only; PDF page 5, lines 60–75). A hybrid combines model types to overcome the limits of one. Variational autoencoders (VAEs) learn a latent space: a compact, organised map in which similar images lie close together. Flow-based models learn an exact, reversible mapping between noise and images, so each image can be traced.
- **How the studio judges a model** (PDF page 6, lines 77–95). It checks output quality, computational efficiency, training stability, flexibility and scalability, and consistency: the same character across images (methods built on embeddings are one option) and adherence to the requested style.
- **Ethics and law** (PDF page 6, lines 97–105). Dataset curation means keeping copyrighted material and intellectual property rules in mind. The studio must assess datasets for bias and be open with clients and audiences about the use of AI.
- **Challenges** (PDF page 7, lines 108–116). All students must manage the cost of the denoising process and the ethics of large datasets, including bias mitigation. Higher level students must also balance the generator and the discriminator, and evaluate how a hybrid model can balance the strengths of diffusion models, GANs, VAEs and flow-based models.

## Worked example

Practice scenario (our own, not the case): a studio trains a model only on photographs of mountains and uses it to make posters for a city tourism campaign. The client asks for city skylines at dusk.

Practice question: Explain why the posters may not match the brief. Suggest one change to the training data that would help. Aim for four points.

<Reveal title="Model answer">1. The model learns patterns only from its training data, so with mountain photographs alone it has no good model of city skylines. 2. The training data shapes the generated content, so the posters tend to show mountains even when the brief asks for a city. 3. Dataset curation would help: add licensed photographs of city skylines at dusk, and check the new set for gaps. 4. After retraining, test the outputs against the brief, including the lighting and the style.</Reveal>

A weak answer such as "the model is bad" names no data, no mechanism and no fix, so it earns at most one point.

## How it is examined

Section B questions are short or long, and the command term sets the kind of answer. What a full answer needs:

- **Describe**: a detailed account of what something is and how it works, with an example if it helps.
- **Outline**: a brief summary of the main points, without reasons.
- **Identify**: choose the correct answer or answers from the possibilities; no reason is needed.
- **Explain**: a detailed account that stresses the why, with reasons or causes.
- **Distinguish**: clear differences between two concepts; listing facts about each is not enough.
- **Compare**: similarities and differences between two or more items, referring to all of them.
- **Construct**: information as a labelled diagram, a list or a logical form.
- **Suggest**: a proposed solution, with the reason for the choice.
- **Justify**: valid reasons or evidence, linked to the scenario.
- **Discuss**: a balanced review of several arguments, ending with a supported view.
- **Calculate**: the working in steps, then the answer with its unit.
- **Evaluate** and **To what extent**: strengths against limitations, then a conclusion on how far a claim holds.

Typical stems look like "Describe the features of ...", "Outline a consequence of ..." or "To what extent ...". Count the parts a stem asks for. The questions in the exam are built on the booklet that you are given; the practice questions here carry their own scenario, so you can practise without the file.

<Callout kind="tip">There is no negative marking, so never leave a mark blank. If a question stops you, write your best partial answer, note it and return later.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Writing general theory with no link to the scenario. Application marks need the names, data and users from the question.</Callout>

- Saying that a diffusion model copies its training images. It creates new images, and the training data shapes what they look like.
- Calling diffusion cheap because its images are good. Its many steps need significant computing, which is a key practical limit.
- Describing GAN training as stable. The case warns that GAN training is delicate and can slide into instability or into <Term id="p1-case-mode-collapse"/>.
- Mixing up the two networks of a GAN: the generator creates images, and the discriminator judges them.
- Answering Discuss or To what extent with advantages only. These need both sides and a conclusion.
- Studying VAEs and flow-based models in depth. Higher level students need deep knowledge of diffusion models, GANs and hybrid models, and only general knowledge of VAEs and flow-based models.

## Summary

- Section B: 24 of the 80 marks in Paper 1 on higher level; one question on the case, in parts (a) to (d).
- The case is a design company choosing generative models for images; the booklet is the school file.
- Diffusion models start from noise and remove it step by step with a denoiser, usually a CNN. The training data shapes the content, and the process is costly but photorealistic.
- Higher level only: GANs (a generator against a discriminator, with instability and mode collapse) and hybrid models (VAE latent spaces and flow-based reversibility).
- Judge a model by quality, computational efficiency, training stability, flexibility and scalability, and consistency.
- Ethics: dataset curation (copyright and intellectual property), bias and fairness, and transparency about AI.
- For longer answers, weigh the benefits and limitations, then give a conclusion you can justify.
