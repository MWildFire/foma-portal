## Big idea

Section B tests whether you can use the whole course on one real system. You read a case study, choose the concepts that explain it, and judge the trade-offs. The skill is applying theory to a new scenario and justifying each decision, not memorising the case.

## Explanation

**What section B is.** Section B of Paper 1 uses a case study that you have studied in class. It covers current developments, emerging technologies and ethical issues in computer science. In the exam you get a clean copy of the case, plus new stimulus material.

**How many marks.** At standard level, section B is worth 12 marks, about three questions. At higher level it is worth 24 marks: four or five shorter questions and one longer question. Paper 1 is 1 hour 15 minutes for 50 marks at standard level and 2 hours for 80 marks at higher level. Higher-level students study four challenges from the case in detail; standard-level students study two.

**What examiners look for.** The book lists six abilities: explain how the systems in the case operate, show the computational thinking behind them, apply syllabus concepts, relate scenarios to one another, discuss impacts and ethics, and evaluate, formulate and justify strategies.

**Link concepts to the scenario.** Most application marks come from linking the case to a concept. This topic's eight terms cover the usual links: <Term id="p1-case-latency"/> for response time, <Term id="p1-case-scalability"/> for growth, <Term id="p1-case-encryption-in-transit"/> for protecting data on a network, <Term id="p1-case-data-sovereignty"/> for the law that applies to stored data, <Term id="p1-case-data-protection"/> for personal data rules, <Term id="p1-case-algorithmic-bias"/> for unfair algorithms, <Term id="p1-case-stakeholder"/> for the people affected, and <Term id="p1-case-trade-off"/> for choices between two good qualities.

**A method for each answer.**

<Steps>
<Step>Underline the command term and the scenario words: who, what, where and which data.</Step>
<Step>Name the syllabus concept behind the question, from theme A or theme B.</Step>
<Step>Write as many separate points as the question needs, each one about the scenario.</Step>
<Step>For a longer question, plan first: a benefit, a limitation, an impact and a conclusion.</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout kind="exam">Marks go to separate, checkable points. Four marks need four different ideas; one idea written twice earns one mark.</Callout>

<Callout kind="define">A scenario is a short description of a real or realistic system, with its users, data and goals. Section B gives you one long scenario.</Callout>

## Worked example

Practice scenario (generic, not the exam case): a bike-sharing app unlocks a bike with the rider's phone. Each trip goes to a server, is stored in a database and feeds route recommendations.

Practice question: Explain why trip data should be encrypted while it travels from the phone to the server. Aim for three separate points.

<Reveal title="Model answer">1. Trip data shows where and when a person rides, so it is personal data. 2. On a shared network, anyone who intercepts the messages could read the trips if they were not encrypted. 3. Encryption in transit scrambles the data, so only the app and the server can read it.</Reveal>

A weak answer such as "Encryption keeps data secure" is true but generic. It names no data, threat or route, so it earns at most one point.

## How it is examined

Section B questions are short or long, and the command term sets the kind of answer. What a full answer needs:

- **Describe**: a detailed account of what something is and how it works, with an example if it helps.
- **Outline**: a brief summary of the main points, without reasons.
- **Identify**: choose the correct items from the possibilities, with a short reason for each.
- **Explain**: a detailed account that stresses the why, with reasons or causes.
- **Distinguish**: clear differences between two concepts; listing facts about each is not enough.
- **Compare**: similarities between two or more items, referring to all of them.
- **Construct**: information as a labelled diagram or in a logical form.
- **Suggest**: a proposed solution, with the reason for the choice.
- **Justify**: valid reasons or evidence, linked to the scenario.
- **Discuss**: a balanced review of several arguments, ending with a supported view.
- **Evaluate** and **To what extent**: strengths against limitations, then a conclusion on how far a claim holds.

Typical stems look like "Describe the advantages of ...", "Outline a consequence of ..." or "To what extent ...". Count the parts a stem asks for. The book also links each term to an assessment objective (AO1 knowledge, AO2 application, AO3 analysis and evaluation). These are not mark values; each question shows its own marks.

<Callout kind="tip">There is no negative marking, so never leave a mark blank. If a question stops you, write your best partial answer, note it and return later.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Writing general theory with no link to the scenario. Application marks need the names, data and users from the question.</Callout>

- Mixing up <Term id="p1-case-latency"/> with bandwidth. Latency is the delay for one request; bandwidth is how much data moves each second.
- Saying that encryption makes data secure without naming the threat it stops, such as someone reading traffic on a shared network.
- Answering Discuss, Evaluate or To what extent with advantages only. These need both sides and a conclusion.
- Describing an algorithm when the question asks why it is unfair. Name the bias, its source in the data and the group it harms.
- Spending most of the time on the long question. Share your time according to the marks.

## Summary

- Section B: 12 marks at standard level and 24 at higher level; at higher level, four or five shorter questions and one longer question.
- Higher level studies four challenges in detail, standard level studies two.
- Answer the command term exactly, and make each point about the scenario.
- Eight concepts link most answers: latency, scalability, encryption in transit, data sovereignty, data protection, algorithmic bias, stakeholder and trade-off.
- For long answers, weigh benefits and limitations, then give a clear conclusion you can justify.
