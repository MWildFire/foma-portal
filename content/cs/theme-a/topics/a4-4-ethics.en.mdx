## Big idea

Machine learning and everyday computing change which decisions get made, who makes them and who bears the consequences. Ethical thinking asks what good and harm a decision brings and who answers for it. A strong answer ties each point to a real group of people and a concrete consequence, then reaches a judgement that weighs benefit against risk.

## Explanation

### Ethical implications of machine learning

An ethical implication <Term id="a4-4-ethical-implication"/> is a possible good or bad result of a decision, judged by its effect on people. Three issues come up again and again. The first is accountability <Term id="a4-4-accountability"/>: when an automated system makes a mistake, someone must be clearly responsible, whether that is the developer, the user or the organization that deployed it. The second is bias <Term id="a4-4-bias"/>. A model learns from its training data, so if that data reflects past prejudice or leaves some groups out, the model repeats the unfairness, for example in lending, hiring or justice. The third is privacy. Machine learning often uses personal data, so people should give informed consent to how their data is used, and data should be anonymized where possible.

The book adds further concerns: training AI uses large amounts of energy and resources, systems can be attacked, manipulated or misused, and AI can disrupt jobs and social interaction. Being open about how an algorithm works, which data it uses and how it reaches decisions builds the trust that accountability depends on.

### Online communication

Machine learning helps create and spread content, and it can be misused. Misinformation <Term id="a4-4-misinformation"/> is inaccurate content shared without intent to deceive. Disinformation <Term id="a4-4-disinformation"/> is false information created and spread on purpose to mislead people. A deepfake <Term id="a4-4-deepfake"/> is synthetic media that makes a real person seem to say or do something they never did, and criminals have used them to steal money. Algorithms can also amplify bias in recommendations and advertising, bots can automate harassment and hate speech, and anonymity protects vulnerable people while making harmful behaviour easier. The tools that detect harmful content can also watch users, so privacy must be weighed too.

### Integration into daily life

Pervasive AI <Term id="a4-4-pervasive-ai"/> works in the background of phones, homes, healthcare and industry. Its benefits are convenience, personalization and efficiency, but it can cost jobs, increase surveillance, erode privacy and repeat the biases in its data. Augmented reality (AR) overlays digital content on the real world and can collect constant data about what the user sees. Virtual reality (VR) immerses the user in a simulated world, which can lead to social isolation and, for some people, anxiety. Quantum computing could break today's encryption, putting sensitive data at risk, and control of it could end up with a few rich countries and firms.

Ethical guidelines need regular reassessment, because social norms change and unforeseen harms appear over time. Data protection rules such as the GDPR in the European Union were tightened as concern about privacy grew.

### Mitigating risk

Mitigating risk <Term id="a4-4-mitigating-risk"/> means finding the risks in a situation and reducing their likelihood and impact. Useful measures include proactive regulation, transparency and explainability of algorithms, and educating the public about the risks and benefits of new technology. Testing a system on a varied group of users before release is a concrete example.

## Worked example

A supermarket chain uses an AI system to set individual shelf prices. The price a shopper sees depends on their loyalty-card history. Answer in three steps.

<Steps>
<Step>Name the mechanism: the model uses purchase history from loyalty cards to set each shopper's price.</Step>
<Step>Name who is affected and how: shoppers who did not know their data was used, and shoppers in lower-income areas who may pay more for the same goods.</Step>
<Step>Name the consequence and a measure: loss of trust and possibly unfair prices. The chain should ask for informed consent, test prices for unfair gaps and name a person responsible for complaints.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Judgement">A strong answer adds balance: personalized discounts can reward loyal customers, but without consent, testing and a named owner, the harm outweighs the benefit.</Reveal>

## How it is examined

These command terms recur in exam questions on this topic. A full answer needs the following.

- Discuss: several aspects, such as accountability, bias and privacy, then a balanced judgement with a reason.
- Explain: the mechanism that creates an ethical issue and why it matters, linked with "because" or "so that".
- Distinguish: a clear difference between two items, such as misinformation and disinformation, point by point, with intent named.
- Describe: the consequences for a named group of people, in several linked points.
- Define: a precise meaning. Examples are not needed unless the question asks for them.
- Suggest: a sensible measure, such as an opt-in setting, with a reason it helps.

<Callout kind="exam">A Discuss answer needs at least two different aspects, a balanced view and a judgement. One well-developed aspect with no judgement is a weak answer.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Mixing up misinformation and disinformation. The difference is intent: a false claim shared by mistake is misinformation, and a false claim made to deceive is disinformation.</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake">Blaming the algorithm alone. Accountability falls on the people and organizations that design, deploy and use the system.</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake">Assuming a model is fair because it works with numbers. Bias usually comes from the data, so check what the training data reflects.</Callout>

## Summary

Ethical implications are the possible good and bad results of a decision. The core concerns are accountability, bias, privacy and consent, and transparency. Misinformation is unintentional, disinformation is deliberate, and deepfakes are convincing synthetic media. Pervasive AI, AR, VR and quantum computing widen the questions, and mitigating risk through regulation, transparency and testing is the response.

<Callout kind="tip">For a Discuss question, use the chain mechanism, then the people affected, then the consequence, and finish with a judgement.</Callout>
