## Big idea

An essay argues a position. It answers one question, states a clear thesis, supports it with arguments and examples, and answers the strongest objection. In French B the marks come from a clear structure and accurate linking words, not from long or rare vocabulary.

## Explanation

A formal essay (soutenu) starts with a short, clear title and has four parts:

- **Introduction:** the context, the question, your thesis and the plan of the essay.
- **Development (two or three parts):** each part has one idea, one example and a link to the next part.
- **Counter-argument:** one objection, stated fairly and followed by your reply.
- **Conclusion:** a summary and an outlook on the future.

Choose *je* or *nous* at the start and keep it to the end. Avoid spoken forms and slang.

Linking words show the logic of the argument:

- **d'une part… d'autre part:** two arguments side by side.
- **certes… mais:** you grant a point, then argue against it.
- **en revanche:** a contrast.
- **en effet:** an explanation of the point just made.
- **par conséquent:** a result that follows from the argument.
- **bien que + subjunctive:** a concession inside one sentence. For example, *Bien que les habitudes soient semblables, le sens reste personnel.* After *bien que* the verb is always in the subjunctive: *soient*, not *sont*.

## Worked example

Compare two ways of opening the same idea. The weak version:

*Je pense que l'identité est importante. La langue est importante. La famille est importante.*

The stronger version:

*D'une part, la langue relie une personne à un pays. Par exemple, un enfant qui parle deux langues à la maison apprend aussi à plaisanter et à partager les fêtes. En revanche, la langue ne suffit pas à tout expliquer.*

The weak version repeats one pattern and gives no logic. The stronger version names the argument (*d'une part*), gives a concrete example (*par exemple*) and prepares the next idea with a contrast (*en revanche*). Use the same pattern in every part of your essay: idea, example, link.

## Visual

| Part | What it does | Typical phrases |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | context, question, thesis, plan | *Dans un monde où…*, *Je soutiens que…*, *Je montrerai d'abord…* |
| Argument 1 | idea and example | *D'une part…*, *Par exemple…* |
| Argument 2 | second idea and example | *D'autre part…*, *En effet…* |
| Counter-argument | objection and reply | *Certes…, mais…*, *Bien que…* with the subjunctive |
| Conclusion | summary and outlook | *En somme…*, *Demain…* |

## How it is examined

The P1 writing task asks for 250 to 400 words in one of the offered text types, and the essay is one of them. Criterion A Language (12) looks at accurate and varied structures: linking words, the concession with *bien que* and the subjunctive, agreements and accents. Criterion B Message (12) looks at the content and the layout: a clear thesis, two arguments each with an example, a counter-argument with a reply, and a conclusion. Criterion C Conceptual understanding (6) rewards real insight into identity, for example the idea that ties to a place, a language or a community can also be chosen.

## Common mistakes

- Listing facts about identity without a thesis or a plan, so the reader cannot follow the argument.
- Writing *bien que les habitudes sont* instead of *soient*. The subjunctive is required after *bien que*.
- Mixing *je* and *nous* in one essay.
- Linking every sentence with *et* or *aussi* instead of using varied connectors.
- Stating a counter-argument but never replying to it, so the essay seems to argue against itself.
- Ending with a new argument instead of a summary and an outlook.

## Summary

An essay has an introduction with a thesis and a plan, two or three developed parts, a counter-argument with a reply, and a conclusion that sums up and looks ahead. Link the parts with connectors, use the subjunctive after *bien que*, and keep one register and one pronoun (*je* or *nous*) from start to finish.
