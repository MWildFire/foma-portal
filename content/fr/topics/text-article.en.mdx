## Big idea

An article informs and persuades a general reader. It has a headline that catches attention, a short introduction (the chapeau) that gives the subject, and a body built on facts, examples and the voices of the people involved. In the P1 article the writer describes a problem, explains its causes and proposes solutions, and supports every claim with evidence rather than personal feeling.

## Explanation

A French magazine article usually has these parts:

- the headline (le titre): short, with a hook such as a question or a surprising fact;
- the chapeau: a few sentences under the headline, often in bold or italics, that present the subject and make the reader want to continue;
- the body: paragraphs that each develop one idea, with examples;
- the quotation: the words of a person involved or of an expert, introduced with a speech verb and the speaker's name;
- the conclusion: a short paragraph that returns to the subject and gives an outlook.

The register is neutral to soutenu (formal, literary). Write in the third person. Use « on » for general statements (« on trouve des bouteilles partout ») and the passive for processes (« les déchets sont triés »). Link ideas with « d'abord », « ensuite », « enfin », « en revanche » and « si bien que ».

Quotations go in « » and name the speaker: « Le tri commence par l'information », explique Claire Morel. For a neutral attribution use « selon » with the name: « Selon Claire Morel, … ». Do not present the writer's own view as the expert's: « Je pense que… » expresses your opinion, not the expert's.

Figures need context: say what they measure, where and when, and where they come from. In practice you may invent illustrative figures, but you must say that they are illustrative.

## Worked example

Here is a sentence from a weak paragraph: « Il y a beaucoup de déchets, c'est grave, et je pense que tout le monde doit faire quelque chose. »

An improved version: Devant la gare, les emballages s'accumulent sur les trottoirs. Selon Claire Morel, responsable d'une association de quartier, « le tri commence par l'information ».

The steps:

1. replace the vague « beaucoup » with an observable example (« devant la gare »);
2. replace the personal opinion with a neutral source (« selon… »);
3. quote the expert in « » and name the expert;
4. keep the register neutral: no « c'est grave » and no direct appeal to the reader.

## How it is examined

The P1 article is marked with the same three criteria: A Language, B Message and C Conceptual understanding. Examiners look for a headline and a chapeau, the three content points in order, at least one concrete example of the problem, a clear explanation of the causes, a realistic solution, and a quotation with a neutral attribution. The article must have 250 to 400 words.

## Common mistakes

- Writing in the first person and in a diary tone, with personal feelings instead of facts.
- Attributing an expert's view with « Je pense que… », which turns the expert's view into the writer's.
- Using vague words such as « beaucoup » or « souvent » without an example.
- Forgetting the chapeau, or copying the first paragraph into it.
- Citing real statistics without a source. In practice, use illustrative figures and say so.
- Repeating « il faut » in every paragraph. Vary the structure with the passive and « on ».

## Summary

- An article has a headline, a chapeau, a body with examples and a conclusion.
- Write in the third person, in a neutral to soutenu register, using « on » and the passive.
- Attribute quotations with « selon » or a speech verb and the speaker's name. Never present your own opinion as the expert's.
- Figures need context and a source. Practice figures must be marked as illustrative.
