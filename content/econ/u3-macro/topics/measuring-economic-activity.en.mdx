## Big idea


Gross domestic product (GDP) measures the total value of final goods and services produced inside a country in one year. The same output can be measured through incomes, spending or production, and real GDP removes the effect of price changes, so growth can be compared over time.



## Explanation

Definitions required by the checklist:

<Term id="gdp-per-capita"/>

Output, income and spending form a loop, which the <Term id="circular-flow-of-income" inline="true" label="circular flow of income"/> describes. Households supply the factors of production (land, labour, capital and enterprise) to firms and receive income in return: rent, wages, interest and profit. They spend that income on goods and services, which becomes revenue for firms. Money leaves the loop through <Term id="withdrawals" inline="true" label="withdrawals (leakages)"/> (saving, taxes and imports) and enters it through <Term id="injections" inline="true" label="injections"/> (investment, government spending and exports). In the simplified planned-spending model, income is in equilibrium when planned injections equal planned withdrawals. Ex-post equality includes unplanned inventory investment and alone does not prove that planned spending was in equilibrium.

<Term id="gdp" inline="true" label="Gross domestic product (GDP)"/> counts final goods and services only, so a loaf of bread is counted once, not together with the flour inside it. It counts output produced within the country's borders, whoever owns the firm.

<Callout kind="define">GDP is the total market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period, usually one year.</Callout>

There are three ways to measure GDP, and they give the same answer, because every sale is spending by one party and income for another. The <Term id="income-approach" inline="true" label="income approach"/> sums employee compensation (wages and salaries before employee income-tax or social-contribution deductions, plus employers’ social contributions), gross operating surplus, gross mixed income, and taxes less subsidies on production and imports. If instead the supplied aggregate is net domestic factor income, add consumption of fixed capital (depreciation) and net production/import taxes to obtain market-price GDP. “Net” here excludes depreciation, not employee deductions from pay. Do not add depreciation or taxes again when already included. The <Term id="expenditure-approach" inline="true" label="expenditure approach"/> adds up spending on final output by households, firms, government and foreign buyers:

<Formula id="gdp-expenditure"/>

Imports are subtracted because they are spending on foreign output, and exports are added because foreigners buy domestic output. The <Term id="production-approach" inline="true" label="production approach"/> adds value added (output minus intermediate consumption). When sector values use basic prices, add net product taxes to reach market-price GDP. The three methods measure the same aggregate in principle; independently compiled estimates can have a statistical discrepancy.

<Term id="nominal-gdp" inline="true" label="Nominal GDP"/> is measured at current prices, so it rises when prices rise even if output does not change. <Term id="real-gdp" inline="true" label="real GDP"/> is measured at the prices of a base year. It is found by dividing nominal GDP by the <Term id="gdp-deflator" inline="true" label="GDP deflator"/>, the price index of domestic final output included in GDP with the base year equal to 100:

Economic growth is measured by the percentage change in real GDP, never in nominal GDP.

GDP per capita is GDP divided by population. <Term id="gross-national-income" inline="true" label="gross national income (GNI)"/> adds net primary income from abroad to GDP. <Term id="purchasing-power-parity" inline="true" label="purchasing power parity (PPP)"/> adjusts for differing national price levels: equal money amounts do not buy equal baskets in different countries. Use real GDP for output comparisons over time. Tragakes ch.8, pp.251–258.

## Worked example


Nominal GDP is AED 1260 bn and the GDP deflator is 105 (base year = 100). Find real GDP.

<Steps>
<Step>Real GDP = nominal GDP / deflator x 100 = 1260 / 105 x 100 = AED 1200 bn.</Step>
<Step>Check: 1260 / 1200 x 100 = 105, so the deflator is consistent with the real figure.</Step>
</Steps>

Next year real GDP is AED 1224 bn. Find the growth rate of real GDP.

<Steps>
<Step>Change in real GDP = 1224 - 1200 = AED 24 bn.</Step>
<Step>Growth rate = 24 / 1200 x 100 = 2.0%. Use real GDP, never nominal GDP, for growth.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes


- Adding imports to the expenditure formula instead of subtracting them, or counting intermediate goods as well as final goods.
- Calling a rise in nominal GDP "growth" when it may only be inflation.
- Getting the division wrong, or forgetting the x 100, when calculating the deflator or real GDP.
- Counting transfer payments such as pensions as government spending (G).

## Summary


- GDP is the total market value of final goods and services produced within a country in one year.
- The income, expenditure and production approaches give the same GDP; expenditure GDP is C + I + G + (X - M).
- Injections (investment, government spending, exports) add to the circular flow; withdrawals (saving, taxes, imports) take money out.
- Nominal GDP uses current prices; real GDP uses base-year prices: real GDP = nominal GDP / deflator x 100.
- The GDP deflator = nominal GDP / real GDP x 100, with the base year equal to 100.
