## Big idea

Markets distribute income and wealth unevenly, and some households live in poverty. Economists measure inequality with the Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient, and distinguish absolute poverty (a fixed living standard) from relative poverty (a comparison with typical income). Policies that reduce inequality have costs as well as benefits.

<Term id="income-inequality" inline="true" label="Income inequality"/> is the unequal distribution of income, a flow earned in a period from wages, profits, rent and interest. <Term id="wealth-inequality" inline="true" label="wealth inequality"/> is the unequal distribution of wealth, a stock of net assets (assets such as property, savings and shares minus debts). Wealth is usually more unequal than income, because it is passed on through inheritance and grows through saving.

<Term id="lorenz-curve" inline="true" label="Lorenz curve"/> plots the cumulative share of income against the cumulative share of the population, ranked from poorest to richest. The <Term id="line-of-equality" inline="true" label="line of equality"/> is the 45 degree line on which each share of the population receives the same share of income. The further the Lorenz curve sags below this line, the greater the inequality.

The <Term id="gini-coefficient" inline="true" label="Gini coefficient"/> summarises the curve in one number. Let A be the area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve, and B the area under the Lorenz curve. With both axes running from 0 to 100, A + B = 5000.

<Callout kind="define">The Gini coefficient is area A divided by the total area under the line of equality, A + B. For the school model of nonnegative incomes with a positive total, it lies between 0 (perfect equality) and 1 (ideal perfect concentration).</Callout>

$$
G = \frac{A}{A + B}
$$

<Formula id="gini"/>

At equality, A = 0 and G = 0. Perfect concentration at G = 1 is an ideal limiting case. For N equal-weighted households with one receiving everything, the uncorrected finite-sample Gini is (N−1)/N; a normalised statistic can rescale this maximum to 1.

<Term id="absolute-poverty" inline="true" label="Absolute poverty"/> means income is too low to buy basic needs: food, shelter, clothing, safe water and basic health care. It is measured against a fixed standard of living. <Term id="relative-poverty" inline="true" label="relative poverty"/> compares a household with the typical income in its society, for example less than half the median income. Relative poverty can rise even when everyone's income rises, if the poorest grow more slowly than the median.

Inequality has several causes. People differ in <Term id="human-capital" inline="true" label="human capital"/>, the skills and health that drive productivity and pay. Discrimination by gender, ethnicity or background limits access to jobs and promotion. Firms with market power can earn high profits and pay workers less, while unemployment and low-paid work keep households poor. Inheritance passes wealth between generations, and technological change may reward skilled workers most. Government policy matters too: the design of taxes and benefits can widen or narrow gaps.

Governments use several policies. A <Term id="progressive-tax" inline="true" label="progressive tax"/> takes a larger share of income from high earners. <Term id="transfer-payments" inline="true" label="transfer payments"/> such as benefits, pensions and child allowances redistribute income. Targeted support can raise low-income households’ resources; not every pension or allowance is means-tested or paid to the poorest. A <Term id="minimum-wage" inline="true" label="minimum wage"/> sets a legal floor for pay, and spending on education and health builds human capital over time. Each policy has costs: high tax rates can reduce incentives to work, save and invest; benefits withdrawn quickly as income rises discourage extra work; and a minimum wage above the market wage may raise unemployment. Governments must balance <Term id="equity" inline="true" label="equity"/> against efficiency.

Checklist — Tragakes ch.12, pp.359–382: poverty can be measured by a poverty line and the share below it, but prices, household needs, informal income and data quality complicate comparisons. Causes of inequality include unequal opportunity, resource ownership, human capital, discrimination, status/power, tax-and-benefit rules, globalisation/technology and market-based supply-side reforms. Each changes access to income or the reward to a factor. Severe inequality can restrict education and demand, lower living standards and weaken social stability; incentives and investment effects depend on its causes.

A direct tax is charged on income/wealth; an indirect tax on spending. For a tax-inclusive payment, first divide by one plus the tax rate to recover the pre-tax value. Average income tax rate is total tax divided by total income; marginal rate applies to the next income unit. Transfer payments redistribute purchasing power without buying current output. Evaluate transfers alongside education, health, housing, access to finance and anti-discrimination policies, considering targeting, incentives, cost and time.

## Worked example

<Steps>
<Step>The total area under the line of equality is A + B = 5000, half of the 100 by 100 square.</Step>
<Step>Gini before the policy: G = A / (A + B) = 1200 / 5000 = 0.24.</Step>
<Step>After a progressive tax and transfer policy, area A falls to 900, so G = 900 / 5000 = 0.18.</Step>
<Step>The Gini falls by 0.06: the total area between the Lorenz curve and equality has decreased. This alone does not prove that the new curve is closer at every population share; Lorenz curves may cross.</Step>
</Steps>

## Visual

The fixed Lorenz curve plots cumulative income share vertically against cumulative population share horizontally, ordered from poorest to richest. Reveal A (between equality and the curve) and B (under the curve), then calculate G = A / (A + B). The steps reveal areas; they do not move the curve. A different income distribution would give a different curve. Tragakes, ch. 12, pp. 354–358.

## How it is examined

Define questions ask for the Lorenz curve, the Gini coefficient or poverty. Calculate questions give areas A and B and ask for G, with the formula and the requested decimal places. Explain questions ask why inequality arises, with a causal link. Discuss and evaluate questions on policy need both benefits and costs, such as the trade-off between equity and efficiency. Paper 1 essays and Paper 2 data responses use these ideas; Paper 3 asks you to recommend a measure and justify it.

<Callout kind="exam">When you evaluate a redistribution policy, state its trade-off: it can reduce inequality but raise the government budget or weaken incentives.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- Treating area A as the area under the curve. A lies between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve; B lies under the curve.
- Writing the Gini as a number above 1 or as a percentage, without explaining the scale.
- Assuming that absolute and relative poverty always move together.
- Presenting a redistribution policy as costless. Always mention its effect on incentives or on the budget.

## Summary

- Income inequality concerns the distribution of income (a flow); wealth inequality concerns the distribution of assets (a stock).
- The Lorenz curve plots cumulative income share on the vertical axis and cumulative population share on the horizontal axis; the line of equality is the 45 degree line.
- Gini is zero at equality and approaches one at ideal concentration; for N equally weighted finite observations, its uncorrected maximum is (N−1)/N.
- Absolute poverty uses a fixed living standard; relative poverty compares a household with typical income.
- Causes include human capital, discrimination, market power, inheritance and the design of policy.
- Progressive taxes, targeted transfers and minimum wages can reduce inequality, but the net result and efficiency effects depend on their design and responses.
