## Big idea

The AD/AS model shows how the price level and real output are set together in the short run. An equilibrium above or below potential output creates an output gap, which drives the business cycle. Shifts of AD and SRAS explain changes in output and prices; LRAS shows the limit set by productive capacity.

<Term id="aggregate-demand"/> (AD) is the total planned spending on domestic output at each price level: AD = C + I + G + (X - M). Consumption (C) is household spending on goods and services; investment (I) is spending by firms on capital goods, buildings and stock; government spending (G) is spending on goods and services, so transfers such as pensions are excluded; and <Term id="net-exports"/> (X - M) are exports minus imports.

The AD curve slopes downwards. A lower price level raises the real value of wealth, so households consume more (the wealth effect). It also lowers the demand for money and interest rates, which raises investment and consumption (the interest rate effect). Lower domestic prices make exports cheaper and imports dearer, so net exports rise (the international trade effect). Changes in components that do not come from the price level shift AD: higher consumer confidence or tax cuts (C), lower interest rates or better business expectations (I), higher government spending (G), or higher foreign incomes and a depreciation of the currency (X - M).

<Term id="short-run-aggregate-supply"/> (SRAS) slopes upwards. In the short run, wages and some input prices are sticky, so a higher price level raises firms' profit per unit and output rises. A change in the price level moves the economy along SRAS. SRAS shifts when costs of production change, such as wages, energy, raw materials, imported inputs after an exchange rate change, or indirect taxes and subsidies, and when productivity changes. Lower costs or higher productivity shift it to the right; higher costs shift it to the left.

<Term id="long-run-aggregate-supply"/> (LRAS) is a vertical line at <Term id="potential-output"/> (potential output), the level of real output an economy can sustain when all resources are fully employed. It shifts with the factors of production, technology and institutions, but not with the price level, because in the long run wages and prices fully adjust. The Keynesian AS curve is horizontal when there is spare capacity, steepens as capacity runs out and is vertical at full employment. The neo-classical view holds that the economy returns to potential output in the long run, so a shift of AD changes only the price level.

<Term id="macroeconomic-equilibrium"/> is where AD meets SRAS, which gives the price level and real output. The <Term id="output-gap"/> measures the percentage difference between actual real GDP and potential output:

<Formula id="output-gap"/>

A positive gap is an <Term id="inflationary-gap"/>: demand pushes up wages and prices, SRAS shifts left, and output returns to potential at a higher price level. A negative gap is a <Term id="deflationary-gap"/>: unemployment is above its natural level, wages and prices fall over time, and SRAS shifts right.

The <Term id="business-cycle"/> describes fluctuations in real GDP around its trend: a boom with a positive output gap, a peak, a recession with a negative output gap, and a trough. A demand-side shock, such as a rise in consumer confidence, shifts AD to the right and raises both output and the price level in the short run (demand-pull inflation). A negative supply-side shock, such as a rise in oil prices, shifts SRAS to the left: the price level rises while output falls, which is stagflation.

## Worked example

Potential output is AED 400 bn and actual real GDP is AED 420 bn. Calculate the output gap and describe it.

<Steps>
<Step>Output gap = (actual - potential) / potential x 100 = (420 - 400) / 400 x 100 = +5.0%.</Step>
<Step>The gap is positive, so the economy has an inflationary gap: output is above potential and prices tend to rise.</Step>
<Step>Over time wages and costs rise, SRAS shifts left, and output falls back to AED 400 bn at a higher price level.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Treating a change in the price level as a shift of AD or SRAS; it moves the economy along the curves.
- Shifting LRAS when the price level changes; LRAS depends on factors of production and technology.
- Leaving net exports out of AD, or subtracting imports with the wrong sign.
- Reading a negative output gap as inflation; a deflationary gap puts downward pressure on prices and raises unemployment.

## Summary

- AD = C + I + G + (X - M): consumption, investment, government spending and net exports at each price level.
- AD slopes downwards because of the wealth, interest rate and international trade effects.
- SRAS slopes upwards and shifts with costs of production, indirect taxes and subsidies, and productivity.
- LRAS is vertical at potential output and shifts with factors of production, technology and institutions.
- Output gap = (actual - potential) / potential x 100: a positive gap is inflationary, a negative gap is deflationary.
- Demand-side shocks shift AD and supply-side shocks shift SRAS; the business cycle is the fluctuation of output around trend.
