## Big idea

Fiscal policy uses government spending and taxation to shift aggregate demand, output and employment. Its effect on national income can be larger than the first change in spending, but time lags, crowding out and the national debt limit how far it can be used.

## Explanation

<Term id="fiscal-policy"/> is the government's use of its budget. Tax revenue comes from taxes such as income tax, corporate tax and value added tax (VAT). Government spending covers goods, services, transfer payments and investment. The difference between the two is the budget balance:

$$
\text{Budget balance} = T - G
$$

<Formula id="budget-balance"/>

A <Term id="budget-surplus"/> is a positive balance, where tax revenue is greater than spending. A <Term id="budget-deficit"/> is a negative balance, where spending is greater than tax revenue. Each deficit adds to the <Term id="national-debt"/>, which is the total the government owes after past borrowing.

<Callout kind="define">Fiscal policy: changes in government spending (G) and taxation (T) that aim to influence aggregate demand, output, employment and the price level.</Callout>

<Term id="discretionary-fiscal-policy"/> is a deliberate decision to change G or T. An <Term id="expansionary-fiscal-policy"/> raises G, cuts T or both, which shifts <Term id="aggregate-demand"/> to the right. Output and employment rise, but if the economy is close to full capacity the price level rises too. Contractionary policy does the reverse and is used to fight inflation. Discretionary policy is slow: there is a recognition lag while the data arrive, a decision lag while the government agrees the measure, an implementation lag while projects are built, and an impact lag while households and firms respond.

<Term id="automatic-stabilizers"/> work without any new decision. Where income tax is progressive, tax revenue falls by a larger percentage than incomes in a recession, while spending on unemployment benefits rises as more people lose their jobs. Both effects cushion the fall in disposable income and demand, so the budget moves towards a deficit in a recession without any change in policy.

Government spending is an injection into the circular flow, so its effect is multiplied. The <Term id="multiplier"/> depends on the leakages of saving, tax and imports:

$$
k = \frac{1}{MPS + MPT + MPM}
$$

<Formula id="multiplier"/>

The change in national income is k times the change in spending. A tax cut has a smaller effect than an equal rise in spending, because part of the extra income is saved. Larger leakages reduce k, so an open economy with high imports gains less from a fiscal stimulus.

Fiscal policy has limits. <Term id="crowding-out"/> happens when government borrowing raises interest rates and reduces private investment; it is strongest when the economy is near full capacity. Debt also means higher interest payments in future budgets. Fiscal policy has a supply side too: income and corporate taxes change the incentives to work, save and invest, and spending on education, health and infrastructure raises productive capacity, shifting <Term id="long-run-aggregate-supply"/> to the right.

## Worked example

A government collects AED 500 million in tax and spends AED 460 million. Its marginal propensities are MPS = 0.1, MPT = 0.2 and MPM = 0.1, and it then raises spending by AED 20 million.

<Steps>
<Step>Budget balance = tax revenue - government spending = 500 - 460 = +40, so the budget is in surplus by AED 40 million.</Step>
<Step>Leakages: MPS + MPT + MPM = 0.1 + 0.2 + 0.1 = 0.4.</Step>
<Step>Multiplier: k = 1 / 0.4 = 2.5.</Step>
<Step>Change in national income = k x change in spending = 2.5 x AED 20 million = AED 50 million.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why is the tax multiplier smaller?">A tax cut gives households extra disposable income, but part of it is saved, so the first round of spending is smaller than an equal rise in government spending. The later rounds then work as usual.</Reveal>

## Common mistakes

- Reversing the budget balance (G - T instead of T - G), so a deficit looks like a surplus.
- Confusing the budget deficit, a flow in one year, with the national debt, the stock built up over time.
- Calling automatic stabilizers discretionary policy; they work without any new decision.
- Writing the multiplier as the sum of the leakages instead of one divided by that sum, or assuming that a tax cut has the same effect as an equal rise in spending.

## Summary

- Budget balance = tax revenue - government spending: a positive balance is a surplus, a negative one is a deficit.
- Discretionary fiscal policy is a deliberate change in G or T; expansionary policy shifts AD to the right.
- Automatic stabilizers, such as progressive taxes and unemployment benefits, reduce fluctuations without a new decision.
- The multiplier k = 1 / (MPS + MPT + MPM) turns a change in spending into a larger change in national income.
- Fiscal policy is limited by time lags, crowding out, the national debt and inflation when the economy is near full capacity.
- Taxes and spending on education and infrastructure also work on the supply side, shifting LRAS to the right.
