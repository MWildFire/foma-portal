## Big idea

Governments and central banks pursue several macroeconomic objectives at once: low inflation, low unemployment, economic growth, a stable balance of payments and an equitable distribution of income. Because these objectives often conflict, policy makers must choose which trade-offs to accept.

## Explanation

Definitions required by the checklist:

<Term id="economic-growth"/>

The five objectives are the standard yardsticks for judging an economy. Low, stable inflation keeps the value of money predictable. Low unemployment means that people who want to work can find jobs. Growth raises real GDP over time. External stability includes a sustainable current-account position and financing. A current-account deficit need not be funded only by debt: equity investment and changes in reserves can also finance external transactions. An equitable distribution of income means the gains from growth are shared fairly.

Inflation is measured with the <Term id="consumer-price-index" inline="true" label="consumer price index"/>, a weighted average of the prices of a basket of goods and services bought by a typical household. Each item is weighted by its share of household spending, and the index equals 100 in the base year.

<Callout kind="define">The consumer price index is a weighted index of the prices of a fixed basket of goods and services, expressed relative to a base year that equals 100.</Callout>

$$
\text{CPI}_t = \frac{\text{cost of basket in year } t}{\text{cost of basket in base year}} \times 100
$$

<Formula id="cpi"/>

The <Term id="inflation" inline="true" label="inflation"/> rate is the percentage change in the CPI from one year to the next:

$$
\pi = \frac{CPI_t - CPI_{t-1}}{CPI_{t-1}} \times 100
$$

<Formula id="inflation-rate"/>

A negative rate means falling prices, which is deflation. A fall in the inflation rate, from 6% to 3% for example, is disinflation: prices still rise, but more slowly. There are two main causes. <Term id="demand-pull-inflation" inline="true" label="demand-pull inflation"/> happens when aggregate demand grows faster than the economy can supply. <Term id="cost-push-inflation" inline="true" label="cost-push inflation"/> happens when production costs rise, for example from wages, energy or taxes, and firms pass them on. Inflation also imposes costs: menu costs of changing prices, shoe-leather costs of more frequent cash management and transactions, redistribution from holders of fixed nominal claims to debtors when inflation is unexpectedly higher and contracts do not adjust, uncertainty that deters investment, and lost competitiveness if prices rise faster than those of trading partners.

Unemployment is measured as a share of the labour force. <Term id="unemployment" inline="true" label="unemployment"/> covers people within the survey’s age coverage who are not employed, are available to start work and are actively seeking it in the specified reference periods. The statistical age coverage is not necessarily bounded by the pension age. The labour force is the employed plus the unemployed, so status as a student or pensioner does not itself exclude someone. A working student or pensioner is employed; one without a job who is seeking and available can be unemployed. Those neither employed nor meeting the unemployment criteria are outside the labour force, including many discouraged workers. The unemployment rate is:

$$
u = \frac{U}{L} \times 100
$$

<Formula id="unemployment-rate"/>

Distinguish four types on the checklist. <Term id="frictional-unemployment" inline="true" label="frictional unemployment"/> arises while people move between jobs. <Term id="structural-unemployment" inline="true" label="structural unemployment"/> arises when workers' skills or locations do not match the jobs on offer, for example after an industry declines. <Term id="cyclical-unemployment" inline="true" label="cyclical unemployment"/> arises when demand is too low during the downturn of the <Term id="business-cycle" inline="true" label="business cycle"/>. <Term id="seasonal-unemployment" inline="true" label="seasonal unemployment"/> varies with recurring seasonal demand for labour, such as tourism or harvesting.

The <Term id="phillips-curve" inline="true" label="Phillips curve"/> shows the link between inflation and unemployment. In the short-run model, with inflation expectations and supply conditions given, it slopes downwards: demand expansion raises output/employment and inflation. An adverse supply shock can instead raise inflation and unemployment together, shifting the curve; the inverse relationship is not an unconditional empirical law. In the long run it is vertical at the <Term id="natural-rate-of-unemployment" inline="true" label="natural rate of unemployment"/>, the rate that remains when the economy operates at its <Term id="potential-output" inline="true" label="potential output"/>. When workers and firms expect higher inflation, wages and prices adjust and the short-run curve shifts to the right (higher inflation at each unemployment rate), so there is no permanent trade-off.

The objectives often conflict. Faster growth from higher aggregate demand can cut unemployment but raise inflation. Strong growth can widen the current account deficit, because households buy more imports. Growth can also widen income gaps, and redistributive taxes may reduce incentives to work and invest. Policy makers must decide which objective to prioritise at a given time.

Checklist additions — Tragakes ch.10, pp.303–325: <Term id="deflation" inline="true" label="deflation"/> is a falling price level; <Term id="disinflation" inline="true" label="disinflation"/> is slower positive inflation. CPI can overstate changes in living costs when substitution and quality improvements are not captured, and a fixed basket may not represent every household. Falling demand can cause deflation: delayed spending lowers firms’ revenue and employment, while the real burden of debt rises. A supply-driven price fall can instead accompany rising output.

HL debt sustainability — Tragakes ch.11.2, printed pp.347–349: compare growth in nominal debt with growth in nominal GDP, using a consistent currency and coverage; a falling debt-to-GDP ratio can make servicing more manageable, but interest rates, maturity and revenue also matter. Debt service competes with other spending; weak credit ratings can raise borrowing costs. Future taxes, spending cuts, unequal ownership of government bonds and crowding out can affect inequality and growth. Borrowing to pay interest can create a debt trap. A deficit is a flow during a year; debt is the accumulated stock. See the fiscal-policy topic for the terms.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing unemployed people by the total population instead of the labour force.
- Treating a fall in the inflation rate as falling prices: prices are still rising, only more slowly.
- Dividing by the new CPI instead of the earlier one when calculating the inflation rate.
- Describing the long-run Phillips curve as a fixed trade-off. In the long run it is vertical at the natural rate.

## Summary

- The five objectives are low inflation, low unemployment, growth, a stable balance of payments and an equitable distribution of income; they often conflict.
- CPI = cost of the basket in year t / cost in the base year x 100; the inflation rate is the percentage change in the CPI.
- Unemployment rate = unemployed / labour force x 100, where the labour force is the employed plus the unemployed.
- Frictional, structural, seasonal and cyclical unemployment; the natural rate is the rate at potential output.
- The short-run Phillips curve shows an inverse relationship; the long-run curve is vertical at the natural rate.
- Expected inflation shifts the short-run curve, so there is no permanent trade-off.
