## Big idea

The central bank uses the money supply and interest rates to keep inflation low and stable, and to support growth and employment. A change in the policy interest rate passes through banks, spending and investment, and shifts aggregate demand. The effect comes with time lags and depends on confidence.

<Term id="monetary-policy"/> is the set of actions that a <Term id="central-bank"/> takes to change the money supply, interest rates and credit in order to meet its objectives, usually low and stable <Term id="inflation"/>. In many countries the central bank is independent of government, so that short-term political pressure does not drive interest rates. The main tools are the following: the <Term id="policy-interest-rate"/> is the benchmark that the central bank sets, and commercial banks' lending and deposit rates follow it. <Term id="open-market-operations"/> are purchases or sales of government bonds: buying adds money to banks' reserves and lowers interest rates, while selling withdraws money. Reserve requirements set the minimum share of deposits that commercial banks must hold; a higher requirement limits lending. <Term id="quantitative-easing"/> is large-scale buying of bonds and other assets, used when the policy rate is already close to zero.

A change in the policy rate works through the <Term id="transmission-mechanism"/> step by step:

1. The central bank changes the policy rate, and the money market moves to a new equilibrium interest rate.
2. Commercial banks change their lending and deposit rates, so loans and mortgages become cheaper or dearer.
3. Households and firms change their borrowing: investment, and spending on cars, housing and other durable goods, rise or fall.
4. Aggregate demand shifts: a cut moves <Term id="aggregate-demand"/> to the right, so output rises and unemployment falls in the short run.
5. If demand rises above potential output, firms raise prices and inflation rises.

Two other channels operate at the same time. A lower rate makes the currency less attractive, so it depreciates; exports become cheaper, imports dearer, and net exports rise. A lower rate also raises share and property prices, so households feel wealthier and spend more.

<Term id="expansionary-monetary-policy"/> lowers the policy rate, buys bonds or uses quantitative easing, so AD shifts right, output and employment rise and the price level tends to rise. <Term id="contractionary-monetary-policy"/> raises rates or sells bonds: AD shifts left and inflation falls, but output and employment fall in the short run, so the policy is a trade-off.

The monetarist model focuses on the money supply. Monetarists argue that inflation is mainly caused by money supply growth faster than output growth, as the quantity equation $MV = PY$ suggests when the velocity of money $V$ is stable. They therefore prefer a steady rule for money growth to frequent changes in rates. In a money market diagram the interest rate is set where money demand meets money supply; an increase in money supply shifts the supply curve to the right and lowers the rate.

The <Term id="inflation-targeting"/> framework has the central bank announce a target inflation rate and adjust its policy rate to reach it. Credibility matters: when people expect the target to be met, wages and prices grow more slowly. Monetary policy has long and variable time lags. In a <Term id="liquidity-trap"/>, near-zero rates do little. Low business confidence can stop firms investing even when borrowing is cheap, and high household debt means lower rates go to repaying loans rather than to spending. A country that fixes its exchange rate to a major currency must follow that currency's rate decisions, which limits its freedom.

HL — Tragakes ch.13, pp.391–394: a commercial bank keeps part of a deposit as reserves and lends the rest. When the loan is spent and redeposited, the banking system can make further loans: deposits and credit expand. Cash leakage, reserve rules and unwilling borrowers limit this process. Reducing the reserve requirement permits more lending; raising the central bank’s minimum lending rate makes bank funding dearer. Real interest is approximately nominal interest minus inflation: 6% nominal with 4% inflation gives 2% real.

Evaluation (p.397): policy can be adjusted in small steps, reversed and decided quickly, so decision/implementation lags are relatively short. Effects on spending still take time and are uncertain; near-zero rates and weak confidence can obstruct transmission. Distinguish the speed of the decision from the lag before output responds.

## Common mistakes

- Expecting a rate cut to lower prices: a cut raises demand, and if demand exceeds potential output, inflation rises.
- Giving a rate change as a percentage when the question asks for percentage points, or the reverse.
- Stopping the transmission chain at the interest rate, without linking it to investment, consumption and aggregate demand.
- Saying monetary policy works at once. Its time lags are long and variable.

## Summary

- Monetary policy is the central bank's action on the money supply, interest rates and credit to meet its objectives, mainly price stability.
- The tools are the policy interest rate, open market operations, reserve requirements and quantitative easing.
- The transmission mechanism runs from the policy rate to bank rates, borrowing and investment, aggregate demand, output and then prices.
- Expansionary policy shifts AD to the right; contractionary policy shifts AD to the left.
- The monetarist model links inflation to money supply growth and recommends a steady rule.
- Limits include time lags, the liquidity trap, low confidence, high debt and a fixed exchange rate.
