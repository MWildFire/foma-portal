## Big idea

The central bank uses the money supply and interest rates to keep inflation low and stable, and to support growth and employment. A change in the policy interest rate can pass through banks, spending and investment to aggregate demand. The effect comes with time lags and depends on confidence.

<Term id="monetary-policy" inline="true" label="Monetary policy"/> is the set of actions that a <Term id="central-bank" inline="true" label="central bank"/> takes to change the money supply, interest rates and credit in order to meet its objectives, usually low and stable <Term id="inflation" inline="true" label="inflation"/>. Central-bank independence can help insulate decisions from short-term political pressure. The <Term id="policy-interest-rate" inline="true" label="policy interest rate"/> is the central bank’s benchmark or operating-rate target; changes can influence commercial banks’ lending and deposit rates, with the response also depending on risk and funding conditions. <Term id="open-market-operations" inline="true" label="open market operations"/> are purchases or sales of government bonds in the school model. Buying adds bank reserves; in the simplified diagram, an increase in money supply lowers the equilibrium rate at a given money demand. Extra reserves do not mechanically turn into a fixed multiple of new loans or deposits. Where a binding reserve requirement applies, it sets a minimum reserve share relative to deposits; changing it can affect banks’ constraints and costs. <Term id="quantitative-easing" inline="true" label="quantitative easing"/> is large-scale buying of bonds and other assets, used when the policy rate is already close to zero.

A change in the policy rate works through the <Term id="transmission-mechanism" inline="true" label="transmission mechanism"/> step by step:

1. The central bank changes its operating-rate setting. Distinguish this implementation from the separate quantity-of-money experiment in the simplified diagram.
2. Commercial-bank lending and deposit rates can adjust, changing borrowing costs; pass-through need not be immediate or one-for-one.
3. Households and firms may change interest-sensitive borrowing, consumption and investment; confidence and balance sheets affect the response.
4. If the cut stimulates spending, <Term id="aggregate-demand" inline="true" label="aggregate demand (AD)"/> shifts right; with spare capacity, real output and employment can rise in the short run.
5. The price response depends on supply conditions: near capacity, stronger demand tends to create greater inflationary pressure; a horizontal Keynesian AS segment gives a different response from upward-sloping SRAS.

Two possible channels reinforce demand. Other things equal, a lower relative interest rate can reduce demand for the currency and cause depreciation under a floating rate. Export prices to foreign buyers and import prices then change, but the net-export response depends on trade elasticities, invoicing and time lags. Lower discount rates can also raise asset prices and support wealth-related spending; neither price response is guaranteed after every rate cut.

<Term id="expansionary-monetary-policy" inline="true" label="Expansionary monetary policy"/> uses tools such as lower rates or asset purchases to support AD. If spending responds and spare capacity exists, output and employment can rise; the price response depends on AS. <Term id="contractionary-monetary-policy" inline="true" label="contractionary monetary policy"/> aims to restrain demand through higher rates or asset sales. In the short-run AD/AS model with upward-sloping SRAS, lower demand reduces price pressure but also output relative to what it would otherwise be. This is a modelled trade-off, not a guaranteed size or timing of change.

The school money-market model holds money demand fixed and draws money supply vertically. A rightward MS shift lowers the equilibrium rate along downward-sloping MD; a leftward shift raises it. This quantity experiment is separate from the modern operating-rate framework described above. For effects on output and prices, use the monetarist/new classical or Keynesian AD/AS model specified by the question and explain the relevant supply shape.

The <Term id="inflation-targeting" inline="true" label="inflation targeting"/> framework has the central bank announce a target inflation rate and adjust its policy rate to reach it. A credible low, stable target can help anchor expectations used in wage and price decisions. Monetary policy has long and variable time lags. In the simplified <Term id="liquidity-trap" inline="true" label="liquidity trap"/> model, extra money is willingly held without a further fall in the rate or stimulus to spending; near-zero rates alone do not demonstrate this condition. Low business confidence can stop firms investing even when borrowing is cheap, and high household debt may lead households to use income freed by lower debt-service costs to repay principal rather than increase spending. With a credible fixed exchange rate and free capital movement, independent interest-rate policy is constrained. Capital controls, risk differences and interventions affect how closely domestic rates follow the anchor currency; a peg alone does not imply identical rates in every circumstance.

HL — Tragakes ch.13, pp.391–394 uses a simplified deposit-expansion model: a commercial bank keeps part of a deposit as reserves and lends the rest. When the loan is spent and redeposited, the banking system can make further loans: deposits and credit expand. Cash leakage, reserve rules and unwilling borrowers limit this process. This is a teaching model, not a universal sequence for modern banking: a bank can create a deposit when it grants a loan, subject to profitability, regulation and funding constraints (Bank of England, Money creation in the modern economy, 2014, pp.14–15). Within the simplified model, reducing a binding reserve requirement permits more lending; raising the central bank’s minimum lending rate makes bank funding dearer. Real interest is approximately nominal interest minus inflation: 6% nominal with 4% inflation gives 2% real.

Evaluation (p.397): policy can be adjusted in small steps, reversed and decided quickly, so decision/implementation lags are relatively short. Effects on spending still take time and are uncertain; near-zero rates and weak confidence can obstruct transmission. Distinguish the speed of the decision from the lag before output responds.

## Common mistakes

- Expecting a rate cut automatically to lower prices: it aims to support demand. Inflationary pressure depends on the spending response, supply conditions and expectations.
- Giving a rate change as a percentage when the question asks for percentage points, or the reverse.
- Stopping the transmission chain at the interest rate, without linking it to investment, consumption and aggregate demand.
- Saying monetary policy works at once. Its time lags are long and variable.

## Summary

- Monetary policy is the central bank's action on the money supply, interest rates and credit to meet its objectives, mainly price stability.
- The tools are the policy interest rate, open market operations, reserve requirements and quantitative easing.
- The transmission mechanism runs from the policy rate to bank rates, borrowing and investment, aggregate demand, output and then prices.
- Expansionary policy aims to shift AD right; contractionary policy aims to restrain AD. Transmission and capacity determine the actual effects.
- Keep the simplified money-market experiment separate from modern rate implementation; use the question’s specified AD/AS model.
- Limits include time lags, the liquidity trap, low confidence, high debt and a fixed exchange rate.
