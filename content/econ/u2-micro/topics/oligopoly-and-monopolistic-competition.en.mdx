## Big idea

Monopolistic competition and oligopoly sit between perfect competition and monopoly. In monopolistic competition, many firms differentiate their products and compete on brands, so each has some market power but earns only normal profit in the long run. In oligopoly, a few firms react to each other, so their choices become a strategic game.

## Explanation

<Term id="monopolistic-competition"/> has many firms, low barriers to entry and <Term id="product-differentiation"/>: each firm's product differs from its rivals' through brand, quality, location or service. Each firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve, so it has some control over price, but its demand is fairly elastic because close substitutes exist. In the short run, a firm can produce where $MR = MC$ and earn supernormal profit, as a monopolist does. That profit attracts new firms. Each entrant takes some customers, so the demand curve of every existing firm shifts left and becomes more elastic. Entry stops when demand just touches average total cost at the output where $MR = MC$, so the firm earns only normal profit in the long run.

Long-run equilibrium has two inefficiencies. Price is above marginal cost, so there is allocative inefficiency. Output is below the minimum point of average total cost, which is <Term id="excess-capacity"/>. Advertising and branding add to costs. Consumers gain variety, so the trade-off must be judged.

<Callout kind="define">Monopolistic competition: many firms, differentiated products, low barriers to entry, and only normal profit in the long run.</Callout>

<Term id="oligopoly"/> is a market with a few large firms and high barriers to entry. Its defining feature is <Term id="interdependence"/>: each firm's profit depends on its rivals' reactions, so each must predict them. Firms may avoid rivalry through <Term id="collusion"/>, either a formal cartel or a tacit agreement. In <Term id="price-leadership"/>, one dominant firm sets the price and the others follow, usually to avoid a price war. Collusion raises joint profit, but each member has an incentive to cheat by cutting its price or selling more, so agreements are unstable.

Game theory explains why. <Term id="game-theory"/> studies strategic choices in a payoff matrix that lists each player's payoff for every pair of choices. A <Term id="dominant-strategy"/> gives a higher payoff than any alternative whatever the rival does. When every firm has a dominant strategy, the outcome in which all choose it is a <Term id="nash-equilibrium"/>: no firm can gain by changing its choice alone. In a prisoners' dilemma, this outcome gives every firm less profit than collusion would, which is why collusion is hard to sustain.

Oligopolists also tend to keep prices stable. One explanation is the kinked demand curve: rivals follow a price cut but not a price rise, so demand is more elastic above the current price. The kink creates a gap in marginal revenue, so marginal cost can change without a change in price.

## Worked example

Example 1: A coffee shop with a differentiated product sells 300 cups a week at AED 12 per cup. It cuts the price to AED 10 and sells 450 cups a week.

<Steps>
<Step>Total revenue at AED 12: 12 x 300 = AED 3600 a week.</Step>
<Step>Total revenue at AED 10: 10 x 450 = AED 4500 a week, so revenue rises by AED 900.</Step>
<Step>Check the elasticity: the percentage change in quantity is (450 - 300) / 300 = +50%; the percentage change in price is (10 - 12) / 12 = -16.7%; so PED = 50 / -16.7 = -3.0. Demand is elastic, so the price cut raises revenue.</Step>
</Steps>

Example 2: In a duopoly, a firm sells 300 units at AED 12 with a total cost of AED 2400. Its payoff, the profit it would record in that cell of a payoff matrix, is found in two steps.

<Steps>
<Step>Total revenue = 12 x 300 = AED 3600.</Step>
<Step>Payoff (profit) = 3600 - 2400 = AED 1200.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Command terms set the answer: "Explain" and "Distinguish" compare market structures, and "Evaluate" judges efficiency in P1 essays. Revenue and payoff calculations come up in P2. For a payoff matrix, find each firm's dominant strategy first, then the Nash equilibrium, checking every best response before you name it. A monopolistic competition diagram must label the long-run tangency, normal profit, excess capacity and the price above marginal cost.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a market an oligopoly because it has many large firms. The defining features are few firms, high barriers and interdependence.
- Saying that monopolistic competition earns supernormal profit in the long run. Entry removes it.
- Calling the Nash equilibrium the best outcome for the firms. It is stable, but it can be worse for all of them than collusion.
- Thinking a dominant strategy is good for both players. It is best for each player individually, whatever the rival does.

## Summary

- Monopolistic competition: many firms, differentiated products, low barriers to entry, and normal profit in the long run.
- In the long run, price is above MC (allocative inefficiency) and output is below minimum ATC (excess capacity).
- Oligopoly: few large firms, high barriers and interdependence, so rivals' reactions matter.
- Collusion (a cartel or price leadership) can raise joint profit, but each firm has an incentive to cheat.
- A dominant strategy is best whatever the rival does; a Nash equilibrium is an outcome in which no firm gains by changing alone.
- In a prisoners' dilemma, the Nash equilibrium can leave every firm worse off than collusion.
