## Big idea

A monopolist sets its own price: it restricts output to the level where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, then charges the price the demand curve allows at that output. Barriers to entry let it keep supernormal profit, so it produces less at a higher price than perfect competition would. For a natural monopoly, one large firm is the cheapest supplier, so the question becomes how to regulate it.

## Explanation

A <Term id="monopoly"/> is a market with a single seller and no close substitutes. The firm's <Term id="market-power"/> comes from <Term id="barriers-to-entry"/>, such as patents, licences, high start-up costs, control of a key resource or <Term id="economies-of-scale"/> that make entry unprofitable for a small rival. Because it is the only seller, the firm faces the whole market demand curve, which slopes downwards.

A monopolist must cut the price on every unit to sell one more, so <Term id="marginal-revenue"/> is below the price. For a linear demand curve $P = a - bQ$, total revenue is $TR = aQ - bQ^2$, so marginal revenue falls twice as fast as demand:

$$
MR = a - 2bQ
$$

<Formula id="marginal-revenue"/>

The firm produces where $MR = MC$, because up to that point each extra unit adds more to revenue than to cost. It then reads the price from the demand curve above that output, so the price is above marginal cost. Profit is total revenue minus total cost:

$$
\pi = TR - TC
$$

<Formula id="profit"/>
If the price is above average total cost, the monopolist earns <Term id="supernormal-profit"/>, and barriers to entry let it keep this profit in the long run.

<Callout kind="define">A monopolist produces where MR = MC and sets the price from the demand curve at that output.</Callout>

Compared with <Term id="perfect-competition"/>, where price equals marginal cost and output is higher, the monopoly produces less and charges more. The triangle between the demand curve and the marginal cost curve, between the two outputs, is the <Term id="deadweight-loss"/>: welfare that nobody receives. A monopoly may also be productively inefficient, because a firm without rivals has less pressure to cut costs; this is <Term id="x-inefficiency"/>. A monopolist can practise <Term id="price-discrimination"/>, charging different buyers different prices. This captures more consumer surplus and can increase output in some markets.

A <Term id="natural-monopoly"/> is different. Its long-run average cost falls across the whole relevant range of output, so one firm can supply the market more cheaply than several firms could. Size is then a cost advantage, but the firm still has market power, so efficient pricing becomes a regulatory problem. <Term id="marginal-cost-pricing"/> sets the price equal to marginal cost, which is allocatively efficient, but the price is then below average cost, so the firm makes a loss and needs a subsidy. <Term id="average-cost-pricing"/> sets the price equal to average cost, so the firm earns normal profit, but the price is still above marginal cost, so some deadweight loss remains.

Governments respond with price regulation (price caps), competition policy (blocking mergers or breaking up firms), public ownership and policies that encourage entry. For a natural monopoly, breaking the firm up would raise costs, so regulation is usually preferred.

## Worked example

A monopolist faces the inverse demand curve $P = 60 - 2Q$ (price in AED, output in units) and has a constant marginal cost of AED 20. Its total cost of producing 10 units is AED 260.

<Steps>
<Step>Marginal revenue: $MR = 60 - 4Q$, because MR falls twice as fast as the demand curve.</Step>
<Step>Profit-maximising output: $MR = MC$ gives $60 - 4Q = 20$, so $Q = 10$ units.</Step>
<Step>Price from the demand curve: $P = 60 - 2 \times 10 = 40$, so the price is AED 40, which is above the marginal cost of AED 20.</Step>
<Step>Profit: total revenue = 40 x 10 = AED 400, so profit = 400 - 260 = AED 140. Average total cost is 260 / 10 = AED 26, below the price, so this is supernormal profit.</Step>
<Step>Deadweight loss: perfect competition sets $P = MC$, so $60 - 2Q = 20$ and $Q = 20$. The loss is $\tfrac{1}{2} \times (20 - 10) \times (40 - 20) = 100$, so the deadweight loss is AED 100.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Calculations apply $MR = MC$ to the demand curve and usually come up in P2 data questions; show MR, the output, the price and the profit, each with its unit. A P2 diagram needs labelled MR, MC, AR and ATC curves, the monopoly output and price, and the deadweight loss. P1 essays ask you to evaluate welfare effects or regulation, weighing economies of scale against market power. Use "natural monopoly" only when long-run average cost keeps falling.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the monopoly price off the MR curve, or from the point where MR cuts MC. The price comes from the demand curve above the chosen output.
- Stating that MR equals price for a monopolist. MR is below price because the price falls on all units sold.
- Calling any large firm a natural monopoly. The defining feature is falling long-run average cost over the relevant range.
- Assuming marginal cost pricing is the default. For a natural monopoly it gives a loss, so a subsidy is needed.

## Summary

- A monopolist is a single seller with no close substitutes, protected by barriers to entry.
- Profit is maximised where MR = MC; the price is read from the demand curve above that output.
- For linear demand P = a - bQ, MR = a - 2bQ, so MR is below the price.
- Price is above MC: a monopoly produces less and charges more than perfect competition, causing a deadweight loss.
- Supernormal profit can persist in the long run, and a monopoly may be X-inefficient.
- A natural monopoly has falling long-run average cost; marginal cost pricing gives a loss, while average cost pricing gives normal profit but leaves some deadweight loss.
