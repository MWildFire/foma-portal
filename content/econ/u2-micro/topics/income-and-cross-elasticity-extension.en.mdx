## Big idea

Income elasticity of demand (YED) shows how demand responds to a change in income, and cross-price elasticity of demand (XED) shows how demand for one good responds to a change in the price of another. The sign of each measure reveals the relationship: YED separates normal from inferior goods, and XED separates substitutes from complements. Firms and governments use both to forecast demand and policy effects.

## Explanation

<Term id="income-elasticity-of-demand"/> measures how far demand moves when income changes. It uses the same percentage changes as PED:

<Formula id="yed"/>

The sign of YED classifies the good. A positive YED means a <Term id="normal-good"/>: demand rises when income rises. A negative YED means an <Term id="inferior-good"/>: demand falls when income rises. Within normal goods, the size of YED separates a <Term id="necessity-good"/> (YED between 0 and 1) from a <Term id="luxury-good"/> (YED above 1).

<Term id="cross-price-elasticity-of-demand"/> measures how demand for good A responds to a change in the price of good B:

<Formula id="xed"/>

The price of the other good always goes in the denominator. A positive XED means the goods are <Term id="substitute-goods"/>: a rise in the price of one increases the demand for the other. A negative XED means they are <Term id="complementary-goods"/>: a rise in the price of one reduces the demand for the other.

<Callout kind="define">Income elasticity of demand: the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in income. Cross-price elasticity of demand: the percentage change in quantity demanded of good A divided by the percentage change in the price of good B.</Callout>

The size of XED shows how close two goods are: a large positive or negative value means buyers switch easily or use the goods together, so the goods are <Term id="close-substitutes"/> or <Term id="close-complements"/>. A value near zero means the goods are almost unrelated.

Firms use YED to forecast demand as incomes change: luxury goods may grow faster than income in a boom, and demand for inferior goods may rise in a recession. Firms use XED to respond to rivals: if their product has close substitutes, a rival's price cut may force them to cut prices too. For complements, such as printers and ink, a firm may cut the price of the main product to sell more of the complement. Governments use YED to forecast tax revenue from goods whose demand depends on income, and to judge who is most affected by a recession. They use XED to predict the side effects of a tax on one good. A tax on petrol will lower demand for cars if the two are complements, and may raise demand for public transport if public transport is a substitute for driving.

## Worked example

A household's monthly income rises from AED 3000 to AED 3600, and its purchases of salmon rise from 10 kg to 13 kg a month.

<Steps>
<Step>Percentage change in quantity = (13 - 10) / 10 x 100 = +30%.</Step>
<Step>Percentage change in income = (3600 - 3000) / 3000 x 100 = +20%.</Step>
<Step>YED = 30 / 20 = +1.50. The positive sign means salmon is a normal good; the value is above 1, so it is a luxury good for this household.</Step>
</Steps>

The price of coffee rises from AED 12.00 to AED 13.20 a cup (+10%), and weekly sales of tea rise from 200 cups to 230 cups.

<Steps>
<Step>Percentage change in the quantity of tea = (230 - 200) / 200 x 100 = +15%.</Step>
<Step>Percentage change in the price of coffee = (13.20 - 12.00) / 12.00 x 100 = +10%.</Step>
<Step>XED = 15 / 10 = +1.50. The positive sign means substitutes, and the size shows that they are close.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Calculation questions ask for YED or XED to two decimal places, with the sign; the answer is then interpreted. Distinguish and Explain questions need the sign and the size linked to a named good and to a decision: a firm's pricing, a government's tax forecast or the side effects of a policy. They appear in P1 essays, in P2 data sets with income or price tables, and in P3 policy papers on taxes.

## Common mistakes

- Putting the price of the good itself in the XED denominator instead of the price of the other good.
- Calling a good inferior because it is low quality; inferior describes the response to income.
- Treating a negative XED as a sign of weak goods; a negative XED means complements.
- Assuming that a normal good must be a luxury; check the size of YED as well.

## Summary

- YED is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in income: positive for normal goods, negative for inferior goods.
- A YED between 0 and 1 marks a necessity; a YED above 1 marks a luxury.
- XED is the percentage change in quantity demanded of good A divided by the percentage change in the price of good B.
- A positive XED means substitutes, a negative XED means complements, and a larger size means closer goods.
- Firms use YED to forecast demand and XED to plan responses to rivals.
- Governments use both to forecast tax revenue and to predict the side effects of taxes on other goods.
