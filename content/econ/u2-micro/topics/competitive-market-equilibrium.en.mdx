## Big idea

Price adjusts until quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Consumer and producer surplus measure the gains from trade.

## Explanation

Definitions required by the checklist:

<Term id="signalling-function"/>

<Term id="incentive-function"/>

<Term id="allocative-efficiency"/>

<Term id="market-equilibrium"/> is the price and quantity at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so there is no pressure for the price to change. With demand $Q_d = a - bP$ and supply $Q_s = c + dP$, setting the two equal gives the equilibrium price:

$$
P^{*} = \frac{a - c}{b + d}
$$

<Formula id="linear-equilibrium"/>

Substitute the equilibrium price into either equation to find the equilibrium quantity.

If the price is below equilibrium, quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied. This <Term id="excess-demand"/> pushes buyers to compete and sellers to raise the price. If the price is above equilibrium, there is <Term id="excess-supply"/>: sellers cannot sell all they produce, so they cut the price. This adjustment is the <Term id="price-mechanism"/>, which coordinates buyers and sellers without central direction.

Shifts move the equilibrium. An increase in demand (a shift right) raises both the equilibrium price and quantity; a decrease lowers both. An increase in supply lowers the price and raises the quantity; a decrease raises the price and lowers the quantity. When both curves move at once, one of the two effects is uncertain: if demand and supply both increase, quantity rises, but the price effect depends on the size of each shift.

Welfare is measured by surplus. The <Term id="consumer-surplus"/> is the gap between the maximum price buyers would pay and the price they actually pay, summed over the units bought: the triangle above the price and below demand. The <Term id="producer-surplus"/> is the gap between the price received and the minimum price producers would accept: the triangle below the price and above supply. The area of a triangle is:

$$
\text{Area} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}
$$

<Formula id="triangle-area"/>

## Worked example

Take demand $Q_d = 40 - 2P$ and supply $Q_s = 4 + 3P$, with Q in thousand units and P in AED per unit.

<Steps>
<Step>Set quantity demanded equal to quantity supplied: $40 - 2P = 4 + 3P$.</Step>
<Step>Rearrange: $36 = 5P$, so $P^{*} = 7.20$ AED.</Step>
<Step>Quantity: $Q^{*} = 40 - 2 \times 7.20 = 25.6$ thousand units. Check with supply: $4 + 3 \times 7.20 = 25.6$.</Step>
</Steps>

At AED 6, which is below equilibrium, quantity demanded is 28 and quantity supplied is 22. At AED 9, which is above equilibrium, quantity demanded is 22 and quantity supplied is 31.

<Steps>
<Step>At AED 6: $Q_d = 40 - 12 = 28$ and $Q_s = 4 + 18 = 22$, so excess demand = 28 - 22 = 6 thousand units.</Step>
<Step>At AED 9: $Q_d = 40 - 18 = 22$ and $Q_s = 4 + 27 = 31$, so excess supply = 31 - 22 = 9 thousand units.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Substitute equilibrium price back to find quantity.
- Below equilibrium there is excess demand, above it excess supply.
- A shift and a movement along a curve are different.

## Summary

- Equilibrium: quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
- Prices signal scarcity and create incentives.
- Shifts change equilibrium.
- Consumer surplus lies below demand and above price.
- Producer surplus lies above supply and below price.
