## Big idea

Markets work well when buyers and sellers know the same things. When one side knows more about quality, risk or effort, bad deals can drive good ones out, so the market supplies too little quality and too few trades.

## Explanation

<Term id="asymmetric-information"/> means that one party to a transaction has more or better information than the other. It is a common cause of market failure in insurance, used cars, banking and employment.

Two problems appear. The first happens before a deal. In <Term id="adverse-selection"/>, the less-informed party cannot tell quality or risk apart, so the market fills with poor products or high-risk buyers. In a used-car market, buyers who cannot tell a good car from a faulty one pay an average price; owners of good cars then withdraw, and the quantity traded falls. This pattern is the <Term id="market-for-lemons"/>. In insurance, if everyone pays the same premium, healthy customers are more likely to leave than high-risk customers, so the premium has to rise.

The second problem happens after a deal. <Term id="moral-hazard"/> means that one party changes its behaviour because it does not bear the full cost of its actions, and the other party cannot monitor or control this. It is a special case of the wider <Term id="principal-agent-problem"/>: an agent, such as a manager, contractor or doctor, acts on behalf of a principal, such as a shareholder, client or patient, but may pursue different goals.

<Callout kind="define">Asymmetric information: one party knows more than the other about quality, risk or behaviour. It causes adverse selection before a deal and moral hazard after it.</Callout>

The better-informed party can narrow the gap. <Term id="signalling"/> is an action, such as a warranty or a degree, that reveals its quality. The less-informed party can also act: <Term id="screening"/> collects information, such as a medical check before insurance is sold. Both reduce the gap, but both cost money, so they seldom remove the failure completely.

The efficiency cost is that some trades that would benefit both sides do not happen, and high-quality goods are under-supplied. Governments respond with <Term id="mandatory-disclosure"/>, minimum standards and regulation, compulsory insurance so that all risk types share one pool, guarantees and public provision. Each response has costs, such as enforcement and reduced choice, so the best policy depends on the market.

## Worked example

Suppose 800 used cars a month would be traded if buyers could see the quality of every car. Because buyers cannot tell good cars from faulty ones, they offer only an average price, good sellers leave, and only 200 cars a month are traded.

<Steps>
<Step>Quantity traded: 800 cars a month with symmetric information, and 200 cars a month with asymmetric information.</Step>
<Step>Ratio: 800 / 200 = 4, so the quantity traded is 4 times smaller.</Step>
<Step>Percentage change: (200 - 800) / 800 x 100 = -75%. The market loses three quarters of its trades. This is a clear market failure, even though buyers and sellers still exist.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Using "adverse selection" and "moral hazard" as synonyms. Adverse selection happens before the deal; moral hazard happens after it.
- Saying that asymmetric information always causes market failure. It does so when the gap is large and nothing, such as signals, screening or policy, closes it.
- Forgetting that buyers can hold private information too, for example an insurance buyer who knows his own health.
- Writing that the market "disappears". The correct claim is that both the quality and the quantity traded fall.

## Summary

- Asymmetric information: one party knows more about quality, risk or behaviour than the other.
- Adverse selection happens before a deal and can drive good products or low-risk buyers out of the market.
- Moral hazard happens after a deal: a party takes more risk because it does not bear the full cost.
- Signalling is done by the better-informed party; screening is done by the less-informed party.
- The market for lemons shows both the quality and the quantity traded falling.
- Governments use mandatory disclosure, regulation, compulsory insurance and guarantees; each has costs.
