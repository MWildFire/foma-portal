## Big idea

The standard model assumes that consumers maximize utility and firms maximize profit, using complete information and consistent choices. Behavioural economics shows that people face bounded rationality, predictable biases, limited self-control and other objectives, so real behaviour often departs from the model. This matters for policy, because a well-designed nudge can improve choices without banning any option.

## Explanation

Economic models assume rational, maximizing behaviour. <Term id="utility-maximization"/> means that a consumer chooses the affordable mix of goods that gives the highest total utility. <Term id="profit-maximization"/> means that a firm chooses the output and price that give the highest profit. The model also assumes that agents have complete information, can calculate the best option and act consistently.

<Callout kind="define">Rational maximizing behaviour: choosing the option that gives the highest utility or profit, given the information and the constraints the decision-maker faces.</Callout>

The model is useful because it gives clear predictions, such as the law of demand, and because those predictions can be tested. Its critics question the assumptions. The main criticism is that people face limits. <Term id="bounded-rationality"/> means that information is incomplete, time is short and the mind cannot process every option, so decisions are reasonable rather than perfect. Consumers often accept the first option that meets their needs, which is <Term id="satisficing"/>.

Behavioural economics also finds predictable mistakes. <Term id="anchoring-bias"/> means that the first price seen (the anchor) shapes the value people put on later prices. <Term id="loss-aversion"/> means that a loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases, so people hold on to things they should sell. <Term id="present-bias"/> means that immediate rewards get too much weight, so people spend today and save too little for later.

Some departures come from motives other than self-interest. <Term id="altruism"/> means that people care about others and may give up utility to help them, for example by donating. Firms may pursue <Term id="managerial-objectives"/>, such as sales growth or survival, instead of maximum profit, for example when managers are rewarded for growth. These goals explain why real firms do not always maximize profit.

These findings matter for policy. A <Term id="nudge"/> changes how a choice is presented, for example by making saving the default option that applies unless a person opts out. Because every option remains available, a nudge does not ban choices or change prices. Nudges can still be criticized: they may manipulate people, and a default may not suit everyone. The model remains a useful benchmark when behaviour departs from it, so the critique refines economic analysis rather than rejecting it.

## Worked example

Ayesha starts a job that offers a pension. She can save 5% of her salary each month, or nothing. The model predicts that she compares the future benefit of saving with the cost of spending today.

<Steps>
<Step>The model: a utility-maximizing worker saves when the future benefit outweighs the present cost, with full information and full self-control.</Step>
<Step>What happens: Ayesha does nothing, so the default option, which is no saving, applies for months.</Step>
<Step>The explanation: present bias gives extra weight to spending now, and the pension forms are complicated (bounded rationality), so she never opts in.</Step>
<Step>The policy: the employer makes 5% saving the default, and Ayesha can still opt out.</Step>
<Step>The evaluation: the nudge keeps her choice open and may raise saving, but it does not prove that 5% is the best choice for her, so the outcome has to be checked.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Explain and Evaluate questions on consumer and firm behaviour (P1 essays, P2 data sets and P3 policy papers) give marks for a named assumption of the model, a specific bias or limit, a link to the decision in the question, and a judgement on whether the criticism undermines the model or only qualifies it. P2 data may give survey results on saving or spending; a P3 question may ask you to recommend a nudge, which earns marks for a clear mechanism and a stated limitation.

<Callout kind="exam">A strong evaluation says what the model gets right as well as where it fails. Name the bias and the decision, not only the bias.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- Saying that bounded rationality means people are foolish or irrational; it means that good decisions are costly to make.
- Using one bias to reject the whole model; the model is still a useful benchmark.
- Treating satisficing as laziness; it is a sensible response when searching is costly.
- Calling a nudge a ban or a tax; a nudge keeps all options open and does not change prices.

## Summary

- The model assumes utility-maximizing consumers and profit-maximizing firms with full information.
- Bounded rationality (limited information, time and processing ability) leads to satisficing and rules of thumb.
- Anchoring, loss aversion and present bias cause predictable departures from the model.
- Altruism and managerial objectives are other reasons why behaviour departs from self-interest and profit.
- Nudges and default options change behaviour without banning choices, but they must be evaluated.
- The model is still a useful benchmark, so the critique qualifies economics rather than replacing it.
