## Big idea

Demand shows how much buyers want to buy at each price. A change in the good's own price moves buyers along one demand curve, while changes in income, tastes, expectations, population or the prices of related goods shift the whole curve.

## Explanation

Demand describes the buyers' side of the market. <Term id="supply"/> describes the sellers' side, and together they set the price.

<Callout kind="define">Demand: the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at each price, in a given period of time.</Callout>

The <Term id="law-of-demand"/> states that, ceteris paribus, a fall in price increases quantity demanded and a rise in price reduces it. Three reasons explain the downward slope. The substitution effect: a lower price makes the good cheaper than its alternatives, so buyers switch towards it. The income effect: a lower price raises purchasing power, so buyers can afford more. Diminishing marginal utility: each extra unit gives less additional satisfaction, so buyers pay less for each extra unit.

A <Term id="demand-curve"/> shows this relationship with price on the vertical axis and quantity demanded on the horizontal axis, and it slopes downwards. <Term id="market-demand"/> is the sum of the quantities demanded by all buyers at each price, found by adding the individual demand curves horizontally. If new buyers enter the market, market demand shifts to the right even though no price has changed.

The curve can move in two different ways. A change in the good's own price causes a <Term id="movement-along-demand-curve"/>, also called a change in quantity demanded: buyers move to another point on the same curve. Any other determinant causes a <Term id="shift-of-demand-curve"/>, also called a change in demand: the whole curve moves right (an increase in demand) or left (a decrease). The main determinants are:

- income: demand for a <Term id="normal-good"/> rises as income rises, while demand for an <Term id="inferior-good"/> falls;
- the prices of related goods: a rise in the price of a <Term id="substitute-goods"/> increases demand for the good, while a rise in the price of <Term id="complementary-goods"/> reduces it;
- tastes and fashion, advertising and health information;
- expectations: if buyers expect prices to rise next month, they buy more now;
- population: its size and its age structure.

## Worked example

Market demand is the sum of individual demands at one price. At AED 6 per unit, Ana buys 4 units and Ben buys 2 units.

<Steps>
<Step>Market demand at AED 6 = 4 + 2 = 6 units.</Step>
<Step>Ben's income rises and at AED 6 he now buys 3 units (the good is normal). Market demand at AED 6 becomes 4 + 3 = 7 units.</Step>
<Step>The price has not changed, so this is a change in demand: the market demand curve shifts right by 1 unit at AED 6.</Step>
</Steps>

Now take the market demand curve $P = 12 - 0.5Q$, with Q in units and P in AED. At AED 8 quantity demanded is 8 units, and at AED 6 it is 12 units.

<Steps>
<Step>A fall in price from AED 8 to AED 6 raises quantity demanded from 8 to 12 units. This is a movement along the curve.</Step>
<Step>Income rises and the curve becomes $P = 15 - 0.5Q$. At AED 8 quantity demanded is now $2 \times (15 - 8) = 14$ units, which is 6 units more than before.</Step>
<Step>The price is the same, so the whole curve has shifted right by 6 units at every price.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Demand appears in all three papers: in P1 essays, in P2 data sets about price changes and in P3 policy answers about measures that affect demand. Define questions reward precise wording: quantity, willing and able, price and time period. Distinguish questions need a movement and a shift, each with its cause. Explain questions need a chain: the change in a determinant, its effect on buyers at each price, and the shift of the curve, with a labelled diagram when the command asks for one. Calculate questions, such as a percentage change in demand, give a mark for each step and for the units.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a fall in price a fall in demand. A fall in price increases quantity demanded; demand itself does not change.
- Shifting the curve when the good's own price changes. That is a movement along the curve.
- Treating a rise in the price of a substitute as a movement along the demand curve of the good. The curve shifts right instead.
- Forgetting ceteris paribus: the law of demand describes the effect of price with everything else unchanged.

## Summary

- Demand is the quantity buyers are willing and able to buy at each price; the law of demand gives a downward-sloping curve.
- Market demand is the horizontal sum of individual demand curves.
- A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the demand curve (a change in quantity demanded).
- Income, the prices of substitutes and complements, tastes, expectations and population shift the whole curve (a change in demand).
- Demand for a normal good rises with income; demand for an inferior good falls.
- In every answer, say which curve moves and in which direction.
