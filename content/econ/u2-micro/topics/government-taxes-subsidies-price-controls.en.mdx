## Big idea

Governments use taxes, subsidies and price controls to change the prices and quantities that a free market produces. Each policy creates winners and losers, and in a simple model most of them also cause a deadweight loss.

## Explanation

<Term id="indirect-tax"/> is charged on spending and paid to government by sellers. A <Term id="specific-tax"/> is a fixed amount per unit; an <Term id="ad-valorem-tax"/> is a percentage of the price. Both raise the cost of supply and shift the supply curve upwards.

<Callout kind="define">Tax revenue is the tax per unit multiplied by the quantity sold after the tax.</Callout>

$$
T = t \times Q
$$

<Formula id="tax-revenue"/>

For a specific tax, the price paid by buyers rises, the price received by sellers falls, and the gap between them equals the tax. The division of the burden is <Term id="tax-incidence"/>, and it depends on elasticity. The share paid by consumers is $\frac{PES}{PES + |PED|}$, using positive values of <Term id="price-elasticity-of-supply"/> and <Term id="price-elasticity-of-demand"/>. When demand is inelastic, buyers pay most of the tax; when supply is inelastic, sellers pay most.

A tax also cuts the quantity traded below the efficient level, which in this model is the free-market equilibrium. The surplus that consumers and producers lose, and that nobody receives, is the <Term id="deadweight-loss"/>. On a diagram it is a triangle between the supply and demand curves, with an area equal to half the fall in quantity multiplied by the tax per unit.

$$
\text{DWL} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \Delta Q \times t
$$

<Formula id="deadweight-loss"/>

A <Term id="subsidy"/> is a payment per unit that lowers the cost of supply. It shifts the supply curve downwards by the amount of the subsidy, so the price paid by buyers falls and the quantity traded rises. The cost to government is the subsidy multiplied by the quantity bought.

Price controls are set by law. A <Term id="price-ceiling"/> set below the equilibrium price creates <Term id="excess-demand"/>, a shortage. A <Term id="price-floor"/> set above the equilibrium price creates <Term id="excess-supply"/>, a surplus. Excess demand is quantity demanded minus quantity supplied, so a negative value means a surplus.

Education and vaccination are examples of <Term id="merit-good"/>: the free market under-provides them, so governments subsidise them. Tobacco is an example of <Term id="demerit-good"/>: the free market over-supplies it, so governments tax it. Such taxes can be regressive, because low-income buyers spend a larger share of their income on them.

## Worked example

Demand is Qd = 400 - 20P and supply before any tax is Qs = 40 + 20P, with P in AED. The equilibrium is P = 9 and Q = 220.

A specific tax of AED 2 per unit:

<Steps>
<Step>Buyers pay $P_b$ and sellers receive $P_b - 2$, so $400 - 20P_b = 40 + 20(P_b - 2)$, which gives $P_b = 10$.</Step>
<Step>Quantity after the tax: $Q = 400 - 20 \times 10 = 200$. Sellers receive $10 - 2 = 8$ AED.</Step>
<Step>Tax revenue: $T = 2 \times 200 = 400$ AED. Deadweight loss: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times (220 - 200) \times 2 = 20$ AED.</Step>
<Step>Buyers' share: their price rises by AED 1 of the AED 2 tax, so buyers pay 0.50 (50%).</Step>
</Steps>

Price controls in the same market: a maximum price of AED 8 and a minimum price of AED 10.

<Steps>
<Step>At AED 8: $Q_d = 400 - 20 \times 8 = 240$ and $Q_s = 40 + 20 \times 8 = 200$, so excess demand is $240 - 200 = 40$: a shortage.</Step>
<Step>At AED 10: $Q_d = 200$ and $Q_s = 240$, so excess demand is $200 - 240 = -40$: a surplus of 40 units.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Drawing a tax as a shift of demand. A tax raises the cost of supply, so it shifts the supply curve.
- Assuming sellers pay the whole tax because they hand it to government. The burden depends on elasticities.
- Treating deadweight loss as tax revenue. Revenue goes to government; deadweight loss is surplus that nobody receives.
- Giving a minimum price a shortage. A minimum price above equilibrium creates a surplus, so excess demand is negative.

## Summary

- A specific tax is a fixed amount per unit; an ad valorem tax is a percentage of the price.
- Tax revenue is the tax per unit multiplied by the quantity sold after the tax.
- The side with the less elastic curve bears more of the tax; consumers bear the fraction $\frac{PES}{PES + |PED|}$ of it.
- A tax causes a deadweight loss of half the fall in quantity multiplied by the tax per unit.
- A subsidy shifts supply down; its cost to government is the subsidy multiplied by the quantity bought.
- A maximum price below equilibrium creates a shortage; a minimum price above equilibrium creates a surplus.
- Merit goods are subsidised and demerit goods taxed, but each policy has costs, side effects and trade-offs.
