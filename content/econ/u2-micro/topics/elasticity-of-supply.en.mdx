## Big idea

Price elasticity of supply (PES) measures how strongly producers change the quantity they offer for sale when the price changes. How quickly they can expand output decides whether supply is elastic or inelastic.

## Explanation

<Term id="price-elasticity-of-supply"/> compares the percentage change in quantity supplied with the percentage change in price. It applies the idea of <Term id="elasticity"/> to supply and uses the method of <Term id="percentage-change"/>.

$$
\text{PES} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_s}{\%\Delta P}
$$

<Formula id="pes"/>

<Callout kind="define">Price elasticity of supply: the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price. For a normal supply curve, which slopes upwards, it is positive.</Callout>

Because a higher price brings a higher quantity supplied, PES is positive. Its size sorts supply into three groups. <Term id="elastic-supply"/> means PES is greater than 1: quantity supplied changes by a larger percentage than price. <Term id="inelastic-supply"/> means PES is between 0 and 1. <Term id="unit-elastic-supply"/> means PES is exactly 1.

The main determinant is the time period. In the short run, firms cannot quickly add machines, buildings or skilled workers, so supply is inelastic. In the long run, they can expand capacity and enter the market, so supply becomes more elastic. Other determinants are the <Term id="factor-mobility"/>, which decides how easily resources switch between goods; <Term id="spare-capacity"/>, which allows output to rise without new investment; stock levels, because goods held in store can be sold when the price rises; and production time, because a vineyard or a forest takes years to mature and cannot be expanded quickly.

Two extreme cases mark the limits. <Term id="perfectly-elastic-supply"/> has a horizontal supply curve: producers will supply any quantity at one price, so PES is infinite. <Term id="perfectly-inelastic-supply"/> has a vertical supply curve: quantity supplied does not change whatever the price, so PES is zero. Most real supply curves lie between these two extremes.

Governments need PES together with price elasticity of demand to predict who pays a tax and how output changes after a subsidy. That link is developed in the next topic.

HL — Tragakes ch.3, p.109: crops take time to grow and mines take time to expand, so primary production often has low short-run PES. A demand increase then raises price strongly. Manufacturing can respond more quickly when firms have spare machinery, mobile inputs and inventories. If stocks cannot be stored or costs rise steeply as output expands, PES is lower. ## Worked example

A bakery raises the price of a loaf from AED 4.00 to AED 4.40. Its output rises from 500 loaves to 540 loaves a day.

<Steps>
<Step>Percentage change in quantity supplied: (540 - 500) / 500 x 100 = +8%.</Step>
<Step>Percentage change in price: (4.40 - 4.00) / 4.00 x 100 = +10%.</Step>
<Step>PES = 8 / 10 = 0.80. The value is positive and less than 1, so supply is inelastic over this range.</Step>
<Step>Interpretation: the bakery can add only a little output, perhaps because its ovens already run close to full capacity.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Giving PES a negative sign. Supply curves slope upwards, so the ratio is positive.
- Thinking supply is more elastic in the short run. Producers have least time to respond in the short run, so supply is less elastic than in the long run.
- Reading PES from the slope of the curve. The slope depends on units, while PES uses percentage changes.
- Treating perfectly inelastic supply as zero supply. The quantity is fixed, not zero.

## Summary

- PES is the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price; for a normal supply curve it is positive.
- PES above 1 is elastic, below 1 is inelastic, and exactly 1 is unit elastic.
- Perfectly elastic supply (PES infinite) is horizontal; perfectly inelastic supply (PES zero) is vertical.
- Supply is more elastic with spare capacity, mobile factors, stocks and short production times.
- Supply is more elastic in the long run, when producers can expand capacity and enter the market.
- PES, together with PED, decides how the burden of a tax is shared.
