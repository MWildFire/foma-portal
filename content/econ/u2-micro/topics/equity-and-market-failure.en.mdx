## Big idea

Efficient markets allocate resources where marginal benefit equals marginal cost, but they do not guarantee a fair distribution of income and wealth. Governments use taxes and transfers to pursue equity, and these policies can weaken incentives, which is why equity and efficiency may conflict.

## Explanation

<Term id="equity"/> is a judgement about fair outcomes, while efficiency is about using resources where marginal benefit equals marginal cost. An outcome can be efficient and still very unequal. Markets produce unequal outcomes because people differ in skills, inherited wealth, luck and access to education, and because firms with market power earn high returns. Discrimination adds to this when people with the same productivity are treated differently. This is the market's inability to achieve equity.

Two kinds of fairness are used. <Term id="horizontal-equity"/> means that people in similar circumstances are treated alike. <Term id="vertical-equity"/> means that people with a greater ability to pay contribute more, which is the basis for progressive taxation.

Taxes are classified by how the <Term id="average-tax-rate"/> changes with income. A <Term id="progressive-tax"/> takes a rising share of income as income rises; a <Term id="proportional-tax"/> takes the same share at every income; a <Term id="regressive-tax"/> takes a falling share as income rises. A flat sales tax is proportional in rate but can be regressive in effect, because low earners spend a larger share of their income on taxed goods.

<Callout kind="define">Equity: fairness in the distribution of income, wealth and opportunities. It is judged by outcomes, while efficiency is judged by how resources are used.</Callout>

Governments reduce inequality with taxes and with <Term id="transfer-payment"/>, such as pensions and unemployment benefit. But redistribution changes incentives: high marginal tax rates may reduce work, saving and investment. This is the <Term id="equity-efficiency-trade-off"/>: a policy that makes outcomes fairer may reduce output, so the gain in fairness must be weighed against the efficiency cost.

The trade-off is not always present. Spending on education and health can improve equity and productivity together. A <Term id="merit-good"/> such as education may be under-consumed when people cannot afford it, so subsidies or public provision can improve both fairness and efficiency.

## Worked example

Two people are compared under one tax system. Person A earns AED 50000 and pays AED 5000 in tax. Person B earns AED 150000 and pays AED 45000 in tax.

<Steps>
<Step>Average tax rate of A: 5000 / 50000 = 0.10, or 10%.</Step>
<Step>Average tax rate of B: 45000 / 150000 = 0.30, or 30%.</Step>
<Step>The average rate rises from 10% to 30% as income rises, so the system is progressive. If the rate on extra income is high, B may work fewer hours and output may fall slightly. That is the equity-efficiency trade-off.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Command terms set the answer. "Explain" asks why a policy has an efficiency cost; "Distinguish" asks you to separate progressive, proportional and regressive taxes by their average tax rates; "Evaluate" asks for a judgement on a redistribution policy that depends on values and evidence. The topic appears in P1 essays on market failure and in P2 data questions based on income tables. In an average tax rate calculation, marks go to the division (tax paid divided by income), to the result and to the comparison; the answer can be given as a decimal or a percentage.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a tax regressive only because its rate is the same for everyone. A proportional tax is regressive only if the share of income paid falls as income rises, as with a sales tax on necessities.
- Confusing the average tax rate with the marginal tax rate, which applies to the last AED of income.
- Saying that equity and efficiency always conflict. Some policies, such as education spending, can improve both.
- Presenting equity as something that can be measured. It is a judgement about what is fair.

## Summary

- Equity is a judgement about fair outcomes; efficiency is about using resources where marginal benefit equals marginal cost.
- Markets can produce unequal outcomes, so governments may intervene.
- Horizontal equity treats similar people alike; vertical equity asks people with a greater ability to pay to contribute more.
- A progressive tax takes a rising share of income, a proportional tax a constant share and a regressive tax a falling share.
- Redistribution can weaken incentives, so the equity-efficiency trade-off must be weighed.
- Some policies, such as spending on education and health, can improve equity and efficiency together.
