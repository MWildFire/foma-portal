## Big idea

An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on people outside a transaction and is missing from the market price. Because the market ignores it, too much or too little of a good is produced or consumed, and resources are misallocated.

## Explanation

Market prices reflect only the costs and benefits that the buyer and the seller pay for. An <Term id="externality"/> falls on third parties who are not part of the transaction; its size is the gap between the social and the private effect.

<Callout kind="define">Externality: a cost or benefit of a production or consumption decision that falls on third parties and is not reflected in the market price.</Callout>

An <Term id="external-cost"/> is a negative externality and an <Term id="external-benefit"/> is a positive one. The <Term id="marginal-private-cost"/> and the <Term id="marginal-private-benefit"/> belong to the seller and the buyer. The <Term id="marginal-social-cost"/> and the <Term id="marginal-social-benefit"/> include everyone affected, so the social curves equal the private curves plus the external effect:

$$MSC = MPC + EC, \qquad MSB = MPB + EB$$

Here EC and EB are the external cost and the external benefit per unit. For a negative externality, MSC is above MPC (production) or MSB is below MPB (consumption). For a positive externality, MSB is above MPB (consumption) or MSC is below MPC (production).

The market responds only to private curves, so the market quantity is where MPB equals MPC, while the socially optimal quantity is where MSB equals MSC. Negative externalities cause over-allocation: factory smoke leads to over-production and smoking to over-consumption. Positive externalities cause under-allocation: vaccination leads to under-consumption and training to under-production.

Between the optimum and the market quantity, some units cost society more than they are worth (or the reverse), so total welfare falls. The welfare loss is the triangle between the two social curves, from the optimum to the market quantity, with area $\frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$. Economists call it the <Term id="deadweight-loss"/>.

Governments can move the market towards the optimum. A <Term id="pigouvian-tax"/> equal to the external cost per unit raises the private cost to the social cost; a subsidy does the same for a positive externality. Regulation sets standards or bans, such as emission limits. <Term id="tradable-permits"/> set a cap that firms trade, so the cap is met at the lowest cost. <Term id="property-rights"/> concern legally recognised ownership, use and transfer; the textbook defines them in ch. 19.3, p. 585, in its discussion of development. Each policy has limits: the government must know the size of the external effect, and a tax can hurt poorer consumers when demand is inelastic. ## Worked example

A chemical plant produces in tonnes (Q), and its price is in AED per tonne (P). Its marginal private benefit (MPB) is the demand $Q_d = 40 - 2P$, and its marginal private cost (MPC) is the supply $Q_s = -8 + 2P$. Its smoke imposes an external cost of AED 6 per tonne at every output, so MSC is $Q_s = -20 + 2P$. There is no external benefit, so MSB equals MPB.

<Steps>
<Step>Market quantity, where MPB = MPC: $40 - 2P = -8 + 2P$, so $4P = 48$ and $P = 12$. Then $Q_m = 40 - 2 \times 12 = 16$ tonnes.</Step>
<Step>Socially optimal quantity, where MSB = MSC: $40 - 2P = -20 + 2P$, so $4P = 60$ and $P = 15$. Then $Q_{opt} = 40 - 2 \times 15 = 10$ tonnes. The market produces 6 tonnes too many.</Step>
<Step>Welfare loss: the base is $16 - 10 = 6$ tonnes and the height is the external cost of AED 6 per tonne. The area is $\frac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 6 = 18$ AED.</Step>
<Step>A Pigouvian tax of AED 6 per tonne moves MPC up to MSC, so output falls to 10 tonnes. Tax revenue is $6 \times 10 = 60$ AED.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Shifting the demand curve in a production externality. A negative production externality raises the social cost (MSC above MPC); demand stays at MPB.
- Placing the optimum where MSB equals MPB. The optimum is where MSB equals MSC.
- Measuring the welfare loss from zero. It is only the area between the social curves from the optimum to the market quantity.
- Saying a tax removes the externality. A Pigouvian tax makes the market quantity equal the optimum, but the external effect still exists at that quantity.

## Summary

- An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on third parties and is missing from the market price.
- Social curves equal private curves plus the external effect: MSC = MPC + EC and MSB = MPB + EB.
- Negative externalities put MSC above MPC or MSB below MPB, so the market over-allocates; positive externalities cause under-allocation.
- The market quantity is where the private curves meet; the optimum is where the social curves meet.
- The welfare loss is the triangle between the social curves: one half times the base times the height.
- Taxes, subsidies, regulation, tradable permits and property rights correct the market, within limits of information, enforcement and equity.
