## Big idea

Supply shows how much producers will offer for sale at each price. A change in the good's own price moves producers along one supply curve, while changes in costs, technology, taxes, subsidies, the prices of other goods, expectations and the number of sellers shift the whole curve.

## Explanation

Supply describes the sellers' side of the market. Producers are willing to offer more when the price they receive is higher, and the <Term id="law-of-supply"/> states this relationship.

<Callout kind="define">Supply: the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to sell at each price, in a given period of time.</Callout>

Two reasons explain the upward slope. A higher price makes production more profitable, which encourages existing firms to expand and new firms to enter. Also, the cost of each extra unit usually rises as output grows, because the cheapest resources are used first, so a higher price is needed to cover the higher marginal cost of extra output.

A <Term id="supply-curve"/> therefore slopes upwards, with price on the vertical axis and quantity supplied on the horizontal axis. <Term id="market-supply"/> is the sum of the quantities supplied by all firms at each price, found by adding the individual supply curves horizontally. When more firms enter a market, market supply shifts to the right.

A change in the good's own price causes a <Term id="movement-along-supply-curve"/>, also called a change in quantity supplied: producers move to another point on the same curve. Any other determinant causes a <Term id="shift-of-supply-curve"/>, also called a change in supply: the whole curve moves right (an increase in supply) or left (a decrease). The main determinants are:

- <Term id="cost-of-production"/>: higher costs of labour, raw materials, energy or capital reduce supply at each price, so the curve shifts left; lower costs shift it right;
- technology: an improvement that raises productivity lowers the cost per unit and shifts supply right;
- taxes and subsidies: an indirect tax on producers raises costs and shifts supply left; a subsidy lowers costs and shifts supply right;
- prices of other goods: <Term id="substitutes-in-production"/> compete for the same resources, so a rise in the price of one reduces the supply of the other; <Term id="joint-supply"/> means goods produced together, so a rise in beef output also increases the supply of leather;
- expectations: if producers expect a higher price later, they may hold stock today, so current supply falls;
- number of sellers: more firms in the market increase market supply.

HL — Tragakes ch.2, p.55: with at least one fixed input, adding units of a variable input eventually produces smaller additions to output. If labour is added to a fixed-size kitchen, workers eventually crowd each other. Rising marginal costs help explain upward-sloping supply. This is diminishing marginal returns, not diminishing marginal utility.

## Worked example

The market supply of loaves is 100 loaves a day at AED 4 and 140 loaves a day at AED 6.

<Steps>
<Step>The price rises from AED 4 to AED 6 and quantity supplied rises from 100 to 140 loaves. This is a movement along the supply curve.</Step>
<Step>Flour costs rise, and at AED 6 bakeries now supply only 110 loaves instead of 140. The price is unchanged, so the supply curve shifts left.</Step>
</Steps>

The market has 10 bakeries, each supplying 14 loaves a day at AED 6, which gives the 140 loaves in the table. Suppose four more bakeries open.

<Steps>
<Step>Market supply at AED 6 before the change = 10 x 14 = 140 loaves.</Step>
<Step>After four new bakeries enter, market supply at AED 6 = 14 x 14 = 196 loaves. The price is the same, so the supply curve shifts right.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Calling a rise in the price of the good an increase in supply. It is an increase in quantity supplied: a movement along the curve.
- Reversing the direction of a shift: a tax on producers shifts supply left, a subsidy shifts it right.
- Confusing substitutes in production (wheat and barley compete for land) with substitute goods in demand (tea and coffee).
- Assuming that higher expected prices always increase supply today; producers may hold stock and reduce current supply.

## Summary

- Supply is the quantity producers are willing and able to sell at each price; the law of supply gives an upward-sloping curve.
- Market supply is the horizontal sum of individual supply curves.
- A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the supply curve (a change in quantity supplied).
- Costs, technology, taxes and subsidies, the prices of related goods, expectations and the number of sellers shift the whole curve (a change in supply).
- A rise in costs or an indirect tax shifts supply left; a technology gain, a subsidy or new sellers shift it right.
- In every answer, name the determinant and the direction of the shift.
