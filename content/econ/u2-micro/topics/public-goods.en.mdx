## Big idea

Non-rivalry and non-excludability make a public good difficult to sell in a free market. The mock asks for this mechanism and provision policies, not vertical-summation calculations.

## Explanation

<Term id="public-good"/> has <Term id="non-rivalry"/>: another person can benefit without reducing what others receive. <Term id="non-excludability"/> means it is difficult to prevent non-payers from benefiting. Street lighting illustrates both, within its service area.

A <Term id="free-rider"/> benefits without contributing. If payment is voluntary and non-payers cannot be excluded, many people wait for others to pay; a private firm may not cover its costs. The result can be missing or insufficient provision. This follows from non-excludability, not simply from selfishness.

Government can finance the good through taxation and provide it directly. Alternatively it can contract a private company to produce it. Contracting out changes the producer, while public finance and access can remain. Compare costs, quality, accountability, information and the ability to monitor a contract. Tragakes ch.5, pp.182–186.

## Worked example

A town needs flood defences. Households behind the barrier are protected whether they pay voluntarily or not. A tax-financed project can solve the funding problem. Direct municipal construction gives control; contracting may bring expertise, but quality requirements and monitoring are essential.

## Common mistakes

- Public does not mean free to produce.
- Non-rivalry and non-excludability are different properties.
- A private contractor can produce a publicly funded good.
