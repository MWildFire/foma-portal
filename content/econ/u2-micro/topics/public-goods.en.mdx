## Big idea

A public good is non-rivalrous and non-excludable, so the market tends to under-provide it: people can enjoy it without paying, and private firms cannot cover their costs. The socially optimal quantity is found by adding the individual marginal benefit curves vertically and meeting the marginal cost.

## Explanation

A <Term id="public-good"/> has two features: <Term id="non-rivalry"/>, so one person's enjoyment does not reduce what is left for others (street lighting, national defence), and <Term id="non-excludability"/>, so people who do not pay cannot easily be kept from using it.

<Callout kind="define">Public good: a good that is non-rivalrous and non-excludable, so everyone can use it and one person's use does not reduce the amount for others.</Callout>

A buyer who enjoys the good and lets others pay is a <Term id="free-rider"/>. When many people do this, private firms cannot earn enough to cover their costs, so the market under-provides the good or does not provide it at all. This is the <Term id="free-rider-problem"/>, a market failure.

A private supplier can charge only those who pay, so it provides the quantity that paying buyers value at least at marginal cost, which is less than the socially optimal quantity. For a public good, the market demand is found by <Term id="vertical-summation"/> of the individual marginal benefit curves, because everyone consumes the same unit. The <Term id="optimal-public-good-quantity"/> is where the total marginal benefit equals the marginal cost:

$$MB_A(Q) + MB_B(Q) = MC$$

A <Term id="quasi-public-good"/> is partly non-rival or partly non-excludable, such as a toll road or a park. It can sometimes be provided privately with a charge, leaving only some free riding.

Governments can provide public goods and pay for them from taxes, which avoids free riding. <Term id="public-provision"/> still has real problems: the government does not know each household's willingness to pay, so it may over- or under-provide; collecting taxes and running the service is costly; and political pressure can distort the choice. Evaluate public provision by comparing the gain from solving free riding with these costs.

## Worked example

Two households value street lighting, which costs AED 6 per unit. Household A's marginal benefit is $MB_A = 10 - Q$ and household B's is $MB_B = 8 - Q$, with Q in units and MB in AED per unit.

<Steps>
<Step>Vertical sum: at each quantity, add the two marginal benefits: $MB_A + MB_B = 18 - 2Q$.</Step>
<Step>Socially optimal quantity: $18 - 2Q = 6$, so $2Q = 12$ and $Q = 6$ units. At 6 units, A values the light at AED 4 and B at AED 2; together they cover the AED 6 cost.</Step>
<Step>Market provision: only household A pays, so it buys until its own marginal benefit equals the cost: $10 - Q = 6$, so $Q = 4$ units. Household B free rides on A's purchase.</Step>
<Step>Under-provision: $4 / 6 = 0.67$, so the market provides about two-thirds of the socially optimal quantity.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Public goods appear in all three papers. In P1 essays, expect discuss or evaluate questions on whether government provision is the best answer to under-provision. In P2 data response, draw the vertical sum of two demand curves with the optimum, explain free riding, and calculate the market provision as a proportion of the optimum. In P3 policy papers, recommend how a public good should be funded and justify it with its costs and benefits. Marks come from accurate use of non-rivalry, non-excludability and free riding, and from a correct vertical sum.

## Common mistakes

- Adding individual demand curves horizontally. For a public good the curves are added vertically, at each quantity, because everyone consumes the same unit.
- Saying a public good must be provided by government. Government is one option with its own costs and information problems, and quasi-public goods can be provided privately.
- Confusing non-rivalry with being free. A public good can be costly to provide; the question is who pays.
- Blaming individuals alone for free riding. It comes from non-excludability, which makes payment voluntary.

## Summary

- A public good is non-rivalrous and non-excludable.
- Free riders enjoy the good without paying, so the market under-provides it.
- Market demand for a public good is the vertical sum of individual marginal benefit curves; for a private good it is the horizontal sum.
- The socially optimal quantity is where the total marginal benefit equals the marginal cost.
- Government provision, financed by taxes, avoids free riding but faces information and cost problems.
- Quasi-public goods, such as toll roads, can often be provided privately with a charge.
