## Big idea

Economists simplify reality on purpose, so that the main cause and effect can be seen and tested against evidence. They also separate statements that can be checked from statements that rest on values.

## Explanation

Economists explain the economy with an <Term id="economic-model"/>: a simplified picture of reality that keeps the key relationships and leaves out detail. Leaving out detail makes the main cause and effect easier to see. For example, a diagram of the bread market ignores differences in taste to show how price affects the quantity sold.

Every model rests on what economists call an <Term id="assumption"/>: a simplifying statement taken as given. For example, a model may assume that all buyers in a city have the same income. Assumptions can be unrealistic, but a model is useful when its assumptions are reasonable for the question being studied. To study one cause at a time, economists use <Term id="ceteris-paribus"/>, which holds all other relevant variables constant. This isolates the effect of a single change, for example a higher price on the quantity bought, with income and tastes unchanged.

Economists separate statements that can be tested from statements based on values. A <Term id="positive-statement"/> describes what is or what would happen, and evidence can show whether it is true or false. The opposite kind is a <Term id="normative-statement"/>, which says what should happen. It rests on a value judgement, so evidence alone cannot prove or disprove it. The claim that a fuel tax reduces petrol use is positive, while the claim that the tax is fair is normative.

<Callout kind="define">Positive statement: a testable claim about what is or would happen. Normative statement: a value judgement about what should happen.</Callout>

Models work at two levels. <Term id="microeconomics"/> studies the decisions of individual households and firms in specific markets, such as taxi fares in Dubai. <Term id="macroeconomics"/> studies the economy as a whole, using aggregates such as GDP, the inflation rate and the unemployment rate.

Each model names the decision makers it contains. An <Term id="economic-agent"/> is any individual or group that makes economic decisions: households, firms, the government and the rest of the world.

From its assumptions, a model derives a <Term id="prediction"/>, for example a 2% fall in petrol sales after a 10% price rise. Economists then check the prediction against data. If the data disagree, they revise the assumptions of the model or accept that it explains only part of the picture.

## Worked example

A government announces a tax of 1 AED per litre on petrol. Classify each statement below as positive or normative.

<Steps>
<Step>"The tax will raise 50 million AED of revenue in its first year." This is positive: revenue can be checked against tax records after the year.</Step>
<Step>"The tax will cut petrol sales by 5% in the first year." This is positive: it is a prediction that sales data can test.</Step>
<Step>"The tax is unfair to drivers, so the government should not introduce it." This is normative: it contains value judgements, "unfair" and "should not", which evidence cannot settle.</Step>

## How it is examined

Distinguish and explain questions are common in P1 and in the opening parts of P2. Distinguish questions need a clear contrast on each side and one example each. Explain questions need the reason linked to an assumption of the model or to ceteris paribus. In evaluation, a strong answer notes that the assumptions of a model may not hold in the case studied, and that a normative claim cannot be settled by data alone.

## Common mistakes

- Calling any statement with a number a positive statement; a positive statement must be testable, and a normative claim can also contain numbers.
- Rejecting a model only because its assumptions are simplified; judge it by whether its predictions fit the question being studied.
- Forgetting ceteris paribus when explaining one effect, so that unrelated changes are blamed for the result.
- Thinking microeconomics is only about small firms, or macroeconomics only about large numbers; the difference is whether the study looks at individual agents and markets or at the whole economy.

## Summary

- Economists use simplified models that rest on assumptions, so that the main cause and effect can be seen.
- Ceteris paribus holds other relevant factors constant in order to isolate the effect of one change.
- Positive statements can be tested against evidence; normative statements rest on value judgements.
- Microeconomics studies individual agents and markets; macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole.
- Economic agents are households, firms, the government and the rest of the world.
- Predictions derived from models are tested against data, and a failed test leads to revising the model.
