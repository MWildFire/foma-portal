## Big idea

Resources are limited, so every society must decide what to produce, how to produce it and for whom. Economics is the study of these decisions and of what each one costs.

## Explanation

<Callout kind="define">Economics is the study of how societies use limited resources to satisfy unlimited desires.</Callout>

The central problem is that the resources available cannot satisfy every desire. Economists call this condition <Term id="scarcity"/>, and it applies to every society, rich or poor.

Because of scarcity, every decision means choosing one option and giving up the others. Such a decision is called a <Term id="choice"/>, and it always has a cost. The cost of a choice is the value of the next best alternative given up. Economists call this cost <Term id="opportunity-cost"/>, and it is real even when no money changes hands.

The maximum combinations of two goods that an economy can produce, given its resources and technology, are plotted on a graph called a <Term id="production-possibility-curve"/>. Consumer goods go on the horizontal axis and capital goods on the vertical axis. Every point on the curve shows <Term id="productive-efficiency"/>: all resources are in use with no waste, so more of one good means less of the other.

A point inside the curve, such as U, shows unemployment or underproduction, because some resources are idle or badly used. A point beyond the curve cannot be reached with current resources and technology. The whole curve shifts outwards when resources increase, for example with a larger labour force or more capital, or when technology improves. This is economic growth in the model.

## Worked example

A student has 4 free hours on a weekday. The next best use of that time is a paid shift that earns 50 AED per hour. Studying for 4 hours therefore has an opportunity cost, even though the studying itself costs no money.

<Steps>
<Step>Identify the next best alternative: working the 4 hours for 50 AED per hour.</Step>
<Step>Value the alternative: 4 x 50 AED = 200 AED of earnings given up.</Step>
<Step>State the opportunity cost of studying: 200 AED, not zero, because the study itself costs no money.</Step>
</Steps>

An economy can produce these combinations in a year, in thousands of units: B (80 consumer goods, 60 capital goods), A (60 consumer goods, 80 capital goods) and C (0 consumer goods, 100 capital goods).

<Steps>
<Step>Move from B to A: capital goods rise by 20 (60 to 80) and consumer goods fall by 20 (80 to 60), so one extra capital good costs 1 consumer good.</Step>
<Step>Move from A to C: capital goods rise by 20 (80 to 100) and consumer goods fall by 60 (60 to 0), so one extra capital good now costs 3 consumer goods.</Step>
<Step>The cost of each extra capital good rises, which is why the PPC bows outwards.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Calling scarcity a high price or a temporary shortage; it is a permanent limit on resources relative to unlimited wants.
- Counting money as a factor of production; capital means produced goods such as machines, and money only pays for factors.
- Measuring opportunity cost only as money paid, or forgetting the next best alternative, such as the wages given up.
- Drawing the PPC as a straight line, or treating a point inside the curve as impossible; inside points are possible but inefficient.
