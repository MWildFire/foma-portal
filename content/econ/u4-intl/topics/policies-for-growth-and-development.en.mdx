## Big idea

Governments raise growth and development by opening trade, attracting investment, managing debt and investing in people and infrastructure. Each policy has benefits and costs, so the test is whether it relieves the constraint that actually binds in a given country.

## Explanation

<Term id="export-led-growth"/> was the path of several East Asian economies: they exported manufactured goods, used the foreign currency earned to import machinery, and moved into higher-value products. <Term id="trade-liberalisation"/>, meaning lower tariffs, quotas and export subsidies, supports this by lowering input costs and widening markets. The risks are that uncompetitive domestic firms are driven out and that the country stays specialised in low-value goods.

<Term id="foreign-direct-investment"/> brings capital, technology and access to export markets, and foreign firms may train workers and buy from local suppliers. The costs are profits sent abroad, competition that crowds out domestic firms, few links to the local economy, and pressure to lower environmental or labour standards. Governments offer tax breaks and <Term id="special-economic-zone"/> to attract investment; the gain depends on whether these firms build local links.

<Term id="foreign-aid"/> fills gaps in saving and pays for schools, clinics and emergency relief. Its advantages are quick finance for projects a poor country could not fund from its own revenue, and technical expertise. Its disadvantages include dependence, misuse and corruption, <Term id="tied-aid"/> that raises costs, and the risk that aid weakens the recipient's own tax effort. <Term id="debt-relief"/> works in a similar way: it frees revenue and reduces <Term id="debt-overhang"/>, but it does not fix the causes of borrowing. Both work best where institutions are sound.

<Term id="microfinance"/> addresses a market failure: banks do not lend to poor people without collateral because small loans are costly to make. Group lending and regular repayments reduce risk, and the Grameen Bank in Bangladesh is a well-known example. Advantages include empowerment and income for small businesses. Disadvantages include high interest rates, over-indebtedness and mixed evidence that microfinance alone lifts large numbers out of poverty.

Investment in education and health raises <Term id="human-capital"/>, which increases productivity and reduces child mortality. The benefits take years, and fees can exclude the poorest. <Term id="economic-infrastructure"/>, such as ports, power and roads, lowers the cost of trade and attracts investment, but projects are expensive, can be badly planned and may benefit mainly cities. Strong <Term id="economic-institutions"/> help all these investments succeed.

<Term id="industrial-policy"/> aims to build new sectors through selective subsidies, targeted credit, public research and export targets. East Asian governments used these tools to move firms up the value chain. Critics warn of government failure: officials may pick losers, rent-seeking may grow and corruption may increase. To evaluate any policy, ask how much it changes the binding constraint, how long it takes, who gains and who loses, what it costs and whether it can be financed, and whether the institutions to run it exist.

## Worked example

Take a landlocked country whose exporters pay high transport costs, and whose government can fund only one new policy. Evaluate the options in four steps.

<Steps>
<Step>Name the constraint: high transport costs raise the price of every export, so the binding barrier is infrastructure.</Step>
<Step>Test the options: a port or road upgrade targets that constraint directly, while microfinance and debt relief address other barriers.</Step>
<Step>Count the costs: a road financed by borrowing adds to external debt, takes years to build and needs maintenance. A special economic zone is cheaper to set up, but it gives little benefit if its firms import all their inputs.</Step>
<Step>Conclude: choose the infrastructure project if it can be financed sustainably and planned well, and state the conditions under which the judgement would change.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Explain questions (P2) ask for one advantage and one disadvantage, each linked to growth or development. Evaluate and discuss questions (P1) reward a judgement on which policy works, under which conditions, with the time lags and the interests of different groups. Recommend questions (P3) need one clear choice, a justification from the case data, the costs and risks, and a plan to reduce those risks.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a policy without naming the barrier it addresses.
- Calling FDI always good or always bad; the outcome depends on linkages, regulation and the terms of the investment.
- Ignoring time lags: education and infrastructure take years, while aid can deliver quicker relief.
- Treating debt relief as a complete cure; without better borrowing and tax systems, debt can build up again.

## Summary

- Export-led growth and trade liberalisation widen markets and raise efficiency, but weak domestic firms can suffer.
- FDI brings capital and technology, but the gains depend on local linkages and on the terms agreed with investors.
- Foreign aid finances development quickly, but it can create dependence, misuse and extra cost when it is tied.
- Microfinance widens access to credit, and debt relief frees revenue; both work best with good institutions.
- Investment in education, health and infrastructure builds long-run capacity, but the time lags are long.
- Industrial policy needs state capacity; judge each policy by its effect on the binding constraint, its cost and its feasibility.
