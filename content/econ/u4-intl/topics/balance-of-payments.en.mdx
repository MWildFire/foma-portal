## Big idea

The balance of payments records every economic transaction between a country and the rest of the world. Its accounts always balance in the accounting sense, so a deficit on one account must be matched by a surplus on another.

## Explanation

The <Term id="balance-of-payments"/> has three parts. The <Term id="current-account"/> records trade in goods and services, net primary income (earnings on investment and work abroad) and net secondary income (transfers such as aid and remittances). The <Term id="capital-account"/> is usually small: it records capital transfers and trade in some non-produced assets. The <Term id="financial-account"/> records changes in the ownership of financial assets, such as investment, loans, deposits and changes in official reserves.

<Formula id="current-account"/>

<Callout kind="define">Balance of payments: a record of all transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world over a period, split into the current, capital and financial accounts.</Callout>

The first part of the current account is the <Term id="trade-balance"/>: exports minus imports of goods and services. Income and transfers are then added.

Each transaction has two sides, so the accounts add up to zero. A current account deficit is therefore financed by a net inflow on the capital and financial accounts, for example foreign borrowing or the sale of assets to foreigners. The change in official reserves is part of the financial account, so the total of all three accounts, including reserves, is zero. A deficit is not an accounting error; it shows how the country is financed. Deficits usually arise when demand for imports exceeds demand for exports, because of low competitiveness, a strong currency, high incomes or dependence on imported energy and food. When domestic saving is lower than investment, the gap is financed from abroad, which shows up as a current account deficit.

The <Term id="terms-of-trade"/> compare export prices with import prices.

<Formula id="terms-of-trade"/>

A rise in the index means the same exports buy more imports. The index improves when export prices rise faster than import prices, for example after a rise in the price of a commodity the country sells. Percentage changes in the index are measured with <Formula id="pct-change"/>.

Policies to correct a deficit fall into three groups. An <Term id="expenditure-switching-policy"/> shifts demand towards domestic goods, for example through depreciation, tariffs or better non-price competitiveness. An <Term id="expenditure-reducing-policy"/> lowers total spending, and with it imports, for example through higher interest rates or cuts in government spending, but it can cost output and jobs. Supply-side policies, such as education and innovation, improve competitiveness over the long run.

The effect of a depreciation takes time. The <Term id="j-curve"/> shows that the balance may worsen first, because import prices rise at once while volumes adjust slowly, and then improve as buyers switch. The improvement needs demand for exports and imports to be elastic enough.

## Worked example

A country reports exports of AED 300 million, imports of AED 340 million, net primary income of AED 20 million and net secondary income of AED -5 million.

<Steps>
<Step>Trade balance: exports 300 - imports 340 = -40 (AED million).</Step>
<Step>Add net primary income: -40 + 20 = -20.</Step>
<Step>Add net secondary income: -20 + (-5) = -25. The current account is in deficit by AED 25 million.</Step>
</Steps>

In a second case, the export price index is 120 and the import price index is 110, with 100 as the base year.

<Steps>
<Step>Terms of trade = export price index / import price index x 100 = 120 / 110 x 100 = 109.1.</Step>
<Step>109.1 is above the base value of 100, so the same exports buy more imports than in the base year.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Adding gross exports and imports to the current account without net income and net transfers.
- Treating the trade balance as the whole current account; income and transfers are part of it.
- Saying the balance of payments is in deficit without naming the account or the subtotal.

## Summary

- The current account equals exports minus imports of goods and services, plus net primary income and net secondary income.
- The capital account holds transfers and non-produced assets; the financial account holds changes in ownership of assets and reserves.
- The accounts always balance, so a current account deficit is financed by net capital inflows or by reserves.
- Terms of trade = export price index / import price index x 100; a rise means more imports for the same exports.
- The J-curve: a depreciation may worsen the balance first, then improve it if demand is elastic enough.
- Policies to correct a deficit: expenditure switching, expenditure reducing and supply-side policies, each with trade-offs.
