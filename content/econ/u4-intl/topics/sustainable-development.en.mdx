## Big idea

Sustainable development meets present needs without reducing the options of future generations, and it balances economic, social and environmental goals. Pollution and resource depletion impose costs that markets do not price, so policy has to correct them.

## Explanation

The Brundtland Report (1987) popularised the idea that <Term id="sustainable-development"/> meets current needs without limiting the options of future generations.

<Callout kind="define">Sustainable development: development that satisfies current needs without limiting the options of future generations, balancing economic, social and environmental goals.</Callout>

Sustainable development has three dimensions that must be considered together. The economic dimension covers stable growth, efficient use of resources and jobs that last. The social dimension covers equity, access to health and education, and poverty reduction. The environmental dimension requires keeping <Term id="natural-capital"/> intact, controlling pollution, and protecting the climate and biodiversity. The three interact: growth that destroys the environment, or leaves people behind, is not sustainable.

The core problem is the <Term id="externality"/> from pollution and carbon emissions: producers and consumers do not pay for the damage, so the market produces too much polluting output and uses resources too fast. Future generations cannot take part in today's markets, which is why <Term id="intergenerational-equity"/> matters: today's decisions should not leave them with less natural capital or fewer opportunities.

Governments have four main tools. A <Term id="carbon-tax"/> sets a price per unit of emissions, so the quantity adjusts to the price. An <Term id="emissions-trading-scheme"/> sets the quantity instead: the cap is fixed, and firms trade <Term id="tradable-permits"/> to stay within it. A <Term id="renewable-energy-subsidy"/> corrects the under-provision caused by the external benefit of clean energy. <Term id="command-and-control-regulation"/> sets legal limits and penalties, which gives certainty about the result but may not find the cheapest way to cut emissions.

Each policy has limits. A national carbon price can cause <Term id="carbon-leakage"/>, where production and emissions move abroad. A carbon tax can also be regressive, because low-income households spend a larger share of their income on energy. Subsidies are paid by taxpayers, and tighter rules may slow short-run growth. The evaluation therefore depends on design, international coordination and how the revenue is used.

## Worked example

A government considers a carbon tax on coal-fired power. Apply the reasoning step by step.

<Steps>
<Step>Identify the externality: the emissions impose costs on people who do not buy the electricity, so the private cost is below the social cost.</Step>
<Step>Show the effect of the tax: the firm's cost rises, so it produces less coal power and invests in cleaner fuels.</Step>
<Step>Evaluate the distributional effect: electricity bills rise, so the government could return part of the revenue to low-income households.</Step>
<Step>Check the limits: if plants move abroad, emissions are only moved, which is carbon leakage.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Treating sustainable development as environmental protection only; the economic and social dimensions are part of the definition.
- Judging a national policy by the fall in national emissions alone; check carbon leakage and global effects.
- Ignoring costs: subsidies are paid by taxpayers, and carbon taxes can hit low-income households hardest.

## Summary

- Sustainable development meets present needs without limiting the options of future generations.
- It has economic, social and environmental dimensions, and policies must be judged against all three.
- Pollution and carbon emissions are negative externalities, so markets produce too much of them.
- A carbon tax fixes the price and an emissions trading scheme fixes the quantity; both create incentives to cut emissions.
- Renewable energy subsidies correct under-provision of clean energy, but they cost taxpayers money.
- Carbon leakage and the regressive effect of carbon prices are the main limits to check.
