## Big idea

Growth and development stall when a country cannot save and invest enough, build skills and health, keep institutions working and trade freely. These barriers reinforce each other, which is why a poor country can stay poor for generations.

## Explanation

Economic growth needs more capital, better skills and better technology, and each depends on people choosing to save, invest and learn. Where incomes are low, <Term id="vicious-circle-of-poverty"/> keeps them low: low income leads to low saving, low saving to low investment, low investment to little capital per worker and low productivity, and low productivity to low income again. Saving is the source of most investment, but poor households spend most of their income on food and shelter, and banks lend little when deposits are small. The country then depends on foreign finance, which may be scarce or expensive.

<Callout kind="define">Vicious circle of poverty: a self-reinforcing cycle in which low income leads to low saving, low investment and low skills, which keep income low.</Callout>

Low <Term id="human-capital"/> is a second barrier. Malnutrition, disease and little schooling reduce what workers can produce, so new machinery adds less. Low-income countries also lack the technology and skills to use it. <Term id="technology-transfer"/> can help, but only where investors and traders find a stable environment.

Growth also needs <Term id="economic-institutions"/> that protect <Term id="property-rights"/>, enforce contracts and keep public money honest. Where <Term id="corruption"/> is common, bribes add costs, honest firms lose contracts and investors stay away. Political instability adds risk to every investment decision, so even profitable projects may never start.

Many low-income countries depend on <Term id="primary-commodities"/>. Their prices swing sharply, so export earnings and government revenue are unstable, and long-term plans are hard to finance. Some economists argue that the prices of primary products have tended to fall relative to manufactured goods, which weakens the terms of trade. Trade barriers in rich countries add to the problem: tariffs that rise with the level of processing discourage processing in poor countries, and <Term id="protectionism"/> at home raises the cost of imported machinery.

Debt is a barrier when repayments take a large share of export earnings or tax revenue. <Term id="external-debt"/> must be repaid in foreign currency, so a depreciation of the domestic currency makes it more expensive. A very high level can create <Term id="debt-overhang"/>: investors expect most of any return to go to creditors, so they hold back. The same logic applies to <Term id="national-debt"/>, because interest payments crowd out spending on schools and clinics.

Inequality can also slow development. When the poor cannot borrow to educate their children, talent is wasted, and a high <Term id="gini-coefficient"/> can make reform harder to agree. <Term id="conflict-trap"/> is the most severe case: war destroys capital, drives out skilled people and weakens institutions, which makes the next conflict more likely.

## Worked example

Suppose a country has external debt of AED 10 billion at 6% interest and export earnings of AED 3 billion a year.

<Steps>
<Step>Annual interest = 10 billion x 0.06 = AED 0.6 billion, or AED 600 million a year.</Step>
<Step>Interest as a share of export earnings = 600 / 3000 = 20% (both in AED million), before any repayment of the loan itself.</Step>
<Step>A fifth of export earnings goes on interest alone. That money is not available for schools, roads or machinery, and lenders may charge more for new loans, which is how debt becomes a barrier.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Explain questions (P2) reward a clear chain of cause and effect: each link must lead to the next, and the answer must name the barrier. Discuss and evaluate questions (P1 and P3) reward weighing barriers against each other: which one binds in a given country, why, and what the evidence suggests. Use the data in the stimulus, such as the share of commodity exports or the level of debt, to support the argument.

## Common mistakes

- Listing barriers without saying how each one reduces investment, productivity or income.
- Treating all debt as harmful; the question is whether repayments limit investment and whether the borrowing financed productive projects.
- Assuming that commodity dependence alone causes poverty; weak institutions and little value added matter too.
- Treating the vicious circle as a single cause: it has several links, and it can start from any of them.

## Summary

- A vicious circle of poverty links low income, low saving, low investment, low human capital and low productivity.
- Weak institutions and corruption raise costs and deter domestic and foreign investment.
- Dependence on primary commodities brings unstable export earnings and little value added.
- Large external debt absorbs resources and, through debt overhang, discourages investment.
- Trade barriers, lack of technology and conflict limit markets, know-how and security, so growth stalls.
