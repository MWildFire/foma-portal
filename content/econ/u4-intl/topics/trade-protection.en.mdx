## Big idea

Trade protection uses tariffs, quotas, subsidies and other barriers to shield domestic producers from foreign competition. It raises the domestic price, moves income from consumers and foreign sellers to domestic producers and sometimes to government, and creates a loss for the economy as a whole.

## Explanation

<Term id="protectionism"/> is what governments do when they restrict imports or help domestic producers compete with foreign firms. The main types are tariffs, quotas, export subsidies, voluntary export restraints, administrative barriers and exchange controls. Each type changes price and quantity in its own way, but the test is always the same: who gains, who loses, and what happens to total welfare.

A <Term id="tariff"/> is a tax on imports: a specific tariff is a fixed amount per unit, and an ad valorem tariff is a percentage of the import value. A tariff raises the domestic price from the world price to the world price plus the tariff. Domestic firms supply more, domestic buyers demand less and imports fall. Government collects the tariff multiplied by the imports that remain. Consumers lose more than producers and government gain; the difference is a <Term id="deadweight-loss"/>, which has a production part (extra domestic output that costs more than the world price) and a consumption part (purchases that no longer happen).

A <Term id="quota"/> is a physical limit on imports. Because it caps supply from abroad, the domestic price rises until demand equals domestic supply plus the quota. The gap between the domestic price and the world price on each imported unit is a <Term id="quota-rent"/>. Unless government sells the import licences, the rent goes to licence holders or foreign suppliers. A quota that cuts imports by the same amount as a tariff creates the same deadweight loss, but government collects no tariff revenue.

An <Term id="export-subsidy"/> is a payment for each unit that producers sell abroad. The price exporters receive rises to the world price plus the subsidy. Because exporters can also sell at home for that price, the domestic price rises too. Domestic consumers pay more and buy less, producers gain, and government pays for every exported unit. Export subsidies are often linked to <Term id="dumping"/>.

<Callout kind="define">Dumping is selling exports in a foreign market at a price below their cost of production, or below the price charged in the exporter's home market.</Callout>

A <Term id="voluntary-export-restraint"/> is an agreement under which the exporting country limits its own exports, usually to avoid tighter measures. <Term id="administrative-barriers"/>, such as product standards, licences and slow customs checks, raise the cost of imports. <Term id="exchange-controls"/> limit how much foreign currency importers can buy.

## Worked example

The world price of a good is AED 20 per unit. At that price, domestic supply is 30000 units and domestic demand is 90000 units. A specific tariff of AED 5 per unit raises the domestic price to AED 25. At AED 25, domestic supply rises to 40000 units and domestic demand falls to 70000 units.

<Steps>
<Step>Imports before the tariff: 90000 - 30000 = 60000 units. Imports after the tariff: 70000 - 40000 = 30000 units, so imports fall by 30000 units.</Step>
<Step>Government revenue = AED 5 x 30000 = AED 150000.</Step>
<Step>Consumer surplus falls by AED 5 x 70000 + 0.5 x AED 5 x 20000 = AED 400000: the rectangle on the 70000 units still bought, plus the triangle on the 20000 units no longer bought. Producer surplus rises by AED 5 x 30000 + 0.5 x AED 5 x 10000 = AED 175000.</Step>
<Step>Deadweight loss = 0.5 x AED 5 x 30000 = AED 75000. Check: the change in welfare is 175000 + 150000 - 400000 = -75000 AED, the same loss. Its production part is 0.5 x 5 x 10000 = AED 25000 and its consumption part is 0.5 x 5 x 20000 = AED 50000.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Calling tariff revenue a deadweight loss. Revenue is a transfer from buyers to government; the deadweight loss is the two triangles that nobody receives.
- Saying that a quota gives government revenue. The rent goes to licence holders or foreign suppliers unless the licences are sold.
- Saying an export subsidy lowers the domestic price. It raises the price that exporters receive, and domestic buyers pay that price too.
- Calling any low price dumping. Dumping needs a price below cost or below the home price.

## Summary

- Trade protection includes tariffs, quotas, export subsidies, voluntary export restraints, administrative barriers and exchange controls.
- A tariff raises the domestic price by the tariff; government revenue is the tariff times the imports that remain.
- A quota raises the price too, but the rent goes to licence holders or foreign suppliers unless licences are sold.
- An export subsidy costs government the subsidy times the exports, and domestic consumers pay the higher price.
- Dumping is selling exports below cost or below the home price; it can justify an anti-dumping duty.
- Every form of protection creates a deadweight loss, because consumers lose more than producers and the recipients of revenue or rent gain.
