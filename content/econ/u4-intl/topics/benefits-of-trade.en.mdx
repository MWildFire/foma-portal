## Big idea

Trade lets each country concentrate on the goods it makes at the lowest opportunity cost, so world output and consumer choice rise. The gains are real but unevenly shared: some producers lose, and moving resources between industries is costly.

## Explanation

Countries differ in what they can produce cheaply. A production possibility curve shows the combinations of two goods that a country can make with its resources, and the slope of a straight curve is the opportunity cost of one good in terms of the other.

A producer has an <Term id="absolute-advantage"/> when it can make more of a good from the same resources, or the same output with fewer resources, than another producer. Absolute advantage alone does not decide trade. What matters is <Term id="comparative-advantage"/>: a producer has a comparative advantage in a good when it makes that good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer.

<Callout kind="define">Comparative advantage: a producer can make a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer, so it should specialise in that good and trade for the others.</Callout>

The opportunity cost of good X in terms of good Y is found with <Formula id="opportunity-cost"/>:

$$
\text{OC} = \frac{\text{units of good Y given up}}{\text{units of good X gained}}
$$

When each country concentrates on the good where it has the lower opportunity cost and trades for the rest (this is <Term id="specialisation"/>), the total world output of both goods can rise. This is the <Term id="gains-from-trade"/>. Further gains come from <Term id="economies-of-scale"/> in larger markets, from lower prices and more choice for consumers, and from more competition, which pushes firms to become more efficient.

The gains are not shared equally. Consumers gain from lower prices, and exporters gain larger markets, but <Term id="import-competing-industry"/> loses sales and may close. Workers in those industries bear <Term id="adjustment-costs"/>, such as unemployment and retraining. Specialisation can also create <Term id="trade-dependence"/>: a country that relies on a few imports or export markets is exposed to foreign shocks. <Term id="free-trade"/>, with no tariffs or quotas, is the benchmark in theory, but many governments protect some industries in practice.

## Worked example

Country A can make 90 units of wheat or 60 units of cloth a year. Country B can make 40 units of wheat or 80 units of cloth, with the same resources.

<Steps>
<Step>Opportunity costs for A: wheat = 60 / 90 = 0.67 cloth; cloth = 90 / 60 = 1.5 wheat.</Step>
<Step>Opportunity costs for B: wheat = 80 / 40 = 2 cloth; cloth = 40 / 80 = 0.5 wheat.</Step>
<Step>Comparative advantage: A gives up less cloth per unit of wheat (0.67 against 2), so A specialises in wheat; B gives up less wheat per unit of cloth (0.5 against 1.5), so B specialises in cloth.</Step>
<Step>Before trade, A makes 45 wheat and 30 cloth, and B makes 20 wheat and 40 cloth: the world makes 65 wheat and 70 cloth. After specialisation, A makes 90 wheat and B makes 80 cloth: world output is 90 wheat and 80 cloth, a gain of 25 wheat and 10 cloth.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="At what exchange rate do both countries gain?">A gets cloth for 1 unit of wheat instead of 1.5 units at home, and B gets wheat for 1 unit of cloth instead of 2 units at home. Any price between 0.67 and 2 units of cloth per unit of wheat benefits both countries.</Reveal>

## How it is examined

The command terms are Calculate, Distinguish, Explain and Evaluate. Calculations are marked for the formula, the division and the units: opportunity cost is the units of one good given up per unit of the other gained (P2). Explain and distinguish questions (P1 and P2) test absolute against comparative advantage: define each, then use opportunity costs, not output levels, to decide which country should specialise. Evaluation questions (P1 essays) need the gains (lower prices, choice, efficiency, growth) and the costs (adjustment, dependence, distribution), with a judgement about who gains.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the specialisation by the output level: a country with an absolute advantage in both goods can still have a higher opportunity cost in one of them.
- Inverting the ratio, so 60 / 90 is written where 90 / 60 is needed.
- Assuming that the gains from trade are shared equally: some workers and firms lose.
- Assuming complete specialisation in practice; countries keep some production of goods they also import.

## Summary

- Absolute advantage: a producer makes more of a good from the same resources.
- Comparative advantage: a producer makes a good at a lower opportunity cost, and this decides specialisation.
- Opportunity cost of good X in terms of good Y = units of Y given up / units of X gained.
- Specialisation and trade raise total world output and give consumers lower prices and more choice.
- Import-competing industries lose out, and adjustment costs and trade dependence limit the gains.
- Free trade is the benchmark in theory, while protection is common in practice.
