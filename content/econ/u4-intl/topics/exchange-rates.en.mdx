## Big idea

The exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another, so it links domestic prices, trade and inflation to the rest of the world. Under a floating rate the market sets it; under a fixed rate the central bank holds it at a target.

## Explanation

<Term id="exchange-rate"/> is the price of one currency expressed in units of another. This course writes the rate for the dirham as AED per USD, the same way as the simulation below. When that number rises, one US dollar costs more dirhams, so the dirham has depreciated. The rate quoted in the market is the nominal exchange rate.

<Callout kind="define">Appreciation: a rise in the value of a currency under a floating rate, so one unit buys more of another currency. Depreciation: a fall in its value under a floating rate.</Callout>

The <Term id="real-exchange-rate"/> adjusts the nominal rate for the difference in price levels at home and abroad. In words, the real rate equals the nominal rate multiplied by the foreign price level and divided by the domestic price level. If domestic prices rise faster than foreign prices, the real rate falls: domestic goods become relatively dearer and exporters lose competitiveness, even if the nominal rate has not moved.

The rate is set in the <Term id="foreign-exchange-market"/>. Foreign buyers need the domestic currency to pay for exports, tourism and investment in the country, so their demand creates demand for that currency. Domestic buyers need foreign currency to pay for imports, to travel abroad and to invest abroad, which creates the supply of the domestic currency. If demand for the currency rises faster than its supply, it appreciates; if supply rises faster than demand, it depreciates.

Under a floating rate, an <Term id="appreciation"/> and a <Term id="depreciation"/> are market changes, so they are measured as a percentage change in the rate, using <Formula id="pct-change"/>. An official change in a fixed rate is called devaluation (a cut) or revaluation (a rise), not depreciation or appreciation.

Changes in the rate matter for the economy. A depreciation makes exports cheaper for foreign buyers and imports dearer for domestic buyers, which can improve the current account and raise aggregate demand, but higher import prices can also add to inflation. <Term id="currency-speculation"/> can move the rate sharply in the short run, even when the underlying economy has not changed.

The systems differ in who sets the rate. A <Term id="floating-exchange-rate"/> is set by the market, with no official target. A <Term id="fixed-exchange-rate"/> is held at a target by the central bank, which buys and sells currency to keep it there. A <Term id="managed-exchange-rate"/> is mainly set by the market, but the central bank intervenes from time to time. The UAE dirham is pegged to the US dollar: the Central Bank of the UAE keeps the official rate at 3.6725 AED per USD.

## Worked example

<Formula id="convert"/>

The student holds USD 1000. The rate first stays at the official peg, then, in a hypothetical floating case, it moves to 3.80 AED per USD.

<Steps>
<Step>Convert at the peg: USD 1000 x 3.6725 = AED 3672.50.</Step>
<Step>Percentage change in the rate: (3.80 - 3.6725) / 3.6725 x 100 = +3.47%. The rate rises, so the dirham depreciates.</Step>
<Step>At 3.80 the same USD 1000 buys AED 3800.00, which is AED 127.50 more than at the peg.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why does a rising rate mean a weaker dirham?">Each dirham now buys fewer US dollars, because a US dollar costs more dirhams. The dirham is weaker against the dollar.</Reveal>

## Common mistakes

- Reversing the quote: a rise in AED per USD is a depreciation of the dirham, not an appreciation.
- Calling an official change in a fixed rate a depreciation; that is devaluation or revaluation.
- Forgetting that a depreciation raises import prices and can add to inflation, and that quantities respond only after time.

## Summary

- The exchange rate is the price of one currency in units of another; this course quotes it as AED per USD.
- Under a floating rate, a rise in AED per USD is a depreciation of the dirham, and a fall is an appreciation.
- The real exchange rate adjusts the nominal rate for relative price levels and measures competitiveness.
- Demand for a currency comes from foreign buyers of exports, tourism and investment; supply comes from domestic buyers of imports and investment abroad.
- Floating rates are set by the market, fixed rates are held by the central bank, and managed rates combine the two.
- A depreciation makes exports cheaper and imports dearer; the size of the effect depends on elasticities and time lags.
