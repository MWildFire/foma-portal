## Big idea

A 10-mark extended answer is judged on how directly it answers the command word and how well each point is backed by evidence: a named place, a figure with its year, and a judgement that follows from that evidence. A long general essay about cities often scores less than a shorter answer that uses one real case correctly and reaches a clear conclusion.

## Explanation

Start with the command word, because it sets the job of the whole answer. Discuss asks for a balanced review: the arguments on each side, then a conclusion. To what extent asks how far a claim is true, so the conclusion must give a degree, for example "largely true, but only for owners", and the conditions that change it. A simple yes or no misses the point of a to what extent question, which asks for a degree. The table in How it is examined lists the main command words and what each one asks for.

<Callout kind="define">Command word: the verb or phrase at the start of a question, such as discuss, evaluate or to what extent. It tells you what kind of answer the examiner expects.</Callout>

Spend two or three minutes on a plan before you write. Use four blocks: a short context paragraph that defines the key terms and names your place; two or three developed arguments, each with its own evidence; a counter-argument that tests the claim from the other side; and a conclusion that gives the judgement. Two arguments with solid evidence usually score better than four thin points with no place or figure.

<Callout kind="tip">Decide the judgement in your plan before you write. Then choose the arguments that test it, and check that the counter-argument really challenges it.</Callout>

Write each argument in four moves: the claim in the first sentence, the evidence (a named place, a year and a figure from a source you can name), the explanation of why this evidence supports the claim, and the link back to the question. A figure with no year is weak evidence, and a year with no place is weaker still. Sources can also disagree: material on the Mumbai floods of 2005 gives different figures for the rainfall and the death toll, so quote a number only with its source.

Use option G terms precisely, and define each one in a single clause the first time you use it. An urban heat island is a built-up area that is warmer than the countryside around it. Bid-rent theory explains that land goes to the use that can pay most for it, which is why central land is usually the most expensive. Gentrification is the arrival of richer residents in a lower-income area, which raises rents and can push out long-standing residents. A term used without a definition earns less credit than the same term explained in one line, and a wrong term, such as urban sprawl for urbanisation, breaks the link to the question.

A general answer, such as "cities are growing and this causes problems", stays at description however many paragraphs it has. The move up the levels comes from naming the place and the figure, then saying what they mean. Compare: "Mumbai, 26 July 2005: the Santacruz station recorded 944 mm of rain in 24 hours, and local trains stopped." That sentence gives the examiner something to credit, and the next sentence can explain why it matters for the question.

In the last two or three minutes, check four things: the first sentence of your conclusion answers the command word; there is a counter-argument; every paragraph names a place and a year; every term is defined.

## Worked example

Question: Evaluate the view that urban regeneration always benefits the people who lived in an area before it was regenerated. [10]

Plan it in two or three minutes. The four steps show what each block must contain, so there is no need to write the full answer here.

<Steps>
<Step>Block 1, context: define urban regeneration (renewing a run-down area with new housing, jobs and services) and gentrification (richer newcomers move in, rents rise and some long-standing residents may leave). Name the case you will test the view against: London Docklands, 1981–1998.</Step>
<Step>Block 2, first argument, the benefits: new homes, services and jobs can improve an area. Evidence: the London Docklands Development Corporation oversaw the regeneration of 22 km² of former docklands between 1981 and 1998.</Step>
<Step>Block 3, counter-argument, testing the word "always": Canary Wharf, a new office district begun in 1988, had its first buildings finished in 1991 and brought new workers into the area. Critics argued that many long-standing residents gained far less than the office workers and investors.</Step>
<Step>Block 4, judgement: partly true. The benefits depend on the design of the scheme, especially whether it includes affordable housing and involves the people who already live there.</Step>
</Steps>

Each block becomes one or two paragraphs in the exam. The plan already holds the place, the years and the judgement, so the writing only has to connect them.

## Visual

| Level (our training scale) | What the examiner sees |
|---|---|
| Level 1 (1–3 marks) | A description of the topic. Terms appear without definitions, and the command word is ignored. |
| Level 2 (4–6 marks) | Some points are explained with reasons, and there is some evidence. One side is covered thinly, and the judgement is missing or weak. |
| Level 3 (7–8 marks) | Two sides are argued with named evidence (a place, a year, a figure). The answer weighs them, but the judgement may be thin or not tied to conditions. |
| Level 4 (9–10 marks) | A supported judgement answers the command word, states the conditions under which it holds, and follows from the evidence. |

This is our simplified scale for training, not the official IB markbands. For the exact wording, use the official guide.

## How it is examined

The extended response is worth 10 marks and takes about 30 of the 45 minutes for the option. The examiner looks for four things: knowledge (correct terms and named places), application (the place is used to answer this question, not added as background), analysis (causes and effects are explained step by step) and evaluation (a judgement with its conditions). Length alone does not raise the level.

| Command word | What the answer must do |
|---|---|
| Explain | Give reasons: show why something happens, with a clear link from cause to effect. |
| Examine | Look at causes, processes or effects in detail, and show how they connect. |
| Discuss | Set out arguments for and against, each with evidence, then give a conclusion. |
| Evaluate | Weigh the strengths and limitations of an idea, and judge how well it works. |
| To what extent | Judge how far a claim is true, and state the degree and the conditions for it. |

Marks go to a clear answer to the command word, named places with years and figures, explained links between cause and effect, and a judgement that follows from the evidence.

## Common mistakes

- Description instead of argument. The answer lists what happens in a city but never says why it matters for the question.
- General words with no place or year, such as "many people in poor countries" or "pollution is getting worse". Name the city, the year and the figure.
- One side only. Discuss, evaluate and to what extent answers need a counter-argument, or they stay in the lower levels.
- A conclusion that is missing, placed too early, or does not answer the command word.

<Callout kind="mistake">A correct figure with no year or source is weak evidence. Write the year and the name of the source next to every number.</Callout>

## Summary

- Read the command word first: it sets what the answer must do.
- Plan four blocks in two or three minutes: context, two or three arguments, a counter-argument and a judgement.
- Back each argument with a named place, a year and a figure from a source you can name.
- Define each option G term once, in one clause, and use it correctly.
