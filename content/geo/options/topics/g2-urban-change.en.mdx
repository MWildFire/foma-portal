## Big idea

Cities keep changing: people, jobs and land uses move over time, so a city's map today can look very different from its map fifty years ago. Urban change follows a few repeatable processes, and each one can be named, explained and checked with figures.

## Explanation

<Callout kind="define">Urban change: the change in the population, land use and economy of a city over time, shown by movement, decline, renewal and growth.</Callout>

Many cities change in stages. First, people move outwards: <Term id="g-suburbanisation"/> adds housing and jobs at the edge of the built-up area. When that outward spread is low-density and depends on the car, it becomes <Term id="g-urban-sprawl"/>. Some outer areas grow large enough to hold their own offices and shops, and then they form an <Term id="g-edge-city"/>. Later, some people leave the urban area altogether; this net flow to smaller towns and the countryside is <Term id="g-counter-urbanisation"/>.

Decline often starts with the loss of manufacturing. <Term id="g-deindustrialisation"/> removes factory jobs, and when people leave the old industrial districts, the centre loses population, income and investment. The result is <Term id="g-inner-city-decline"/>, shown by a falling population, unemployment and empty buildings. Detroit is the clearest example: its population fell by 65.4% between 1950 and 2020.

<Case id="g-detroit-decline"/>

Cities respond with planned renewal, known as <Term id="g-urban-regeneration"/>. Developers can reuse a <Term id="g-brownfield-site"/>, such as a former dock or factory, instead of building on a <Term id="g-greenfield-site"/> on the urban fringe. London's Docklands show both the gains and the costs of this approach: Royal Docks closed to commercial traffic in 1981, and the London Docklands Development Corporation oversaw the regeneration of a 22 km² development area of land and water from 1981 to 1998.

<Case id="g-london-docklands"/>

Regeneration often brings wealthier residents into an area, a process called <Term id="g-gentrification"/>. Shops, cafés and property prices rise, and long-standing residents on low incomes may be unable to pay the new rents. The same scheme can therefore look like a success to the city council and a loss to the people it displaces.

Land uses compete for sites, and <Term id="g-bid-rent-theory"/> explains the pattern: each use bids the rent it can pay, and that bid falls with distance from the CBD. In a simple linear form the model is $R(d) = R_0 - kd$, where $R_0$ is the rent at the CBD, $d$ is the distance and $k$ is the rate at which rent falls. Each use has its own <Term id="g-bid-rent-curve"/>, and a zone boundary occurs where the highest bid changes from one use to another. A crossing below a third, higher curve does not create a boundary. When transport improves, distance costs less, the curves become flatter and <Term id="g-land-use-change"/> spreads outwards. In Medellín, Metrocable lines improved access to hillside neighbourhoods, which is the kind of transport change that can shift land values and uses.

<Case id="g-medellin-metrocable"/>

Some cities change because they become a global city, a centre of finance, business services and culture linked to other world cities. Migration connects these changes to people. A <Term id="g-push-factor"/> at home, such as unemployment or poor services, encourages people to leave, while a <Term id="g-pull-factor"/> in the destination, such as factory jobs or higher wages, draws them in. Shenzhen combines special-zone investment policy with rapid city population growth. The linked census comparison measures 2010–2020 growth; by itself it does not establish migration motives or the policy’s isolated causal effect.

## Worked example

Example 1: Detroit's population fell from 1,849,568 in 1950 to 639,111 in 2020. Work out the percentage change.

<Steps>
<Step>Change in population: 639,111 - 1,849,568 = -1,210,457 people.</Step>
<Step>Percentage change: -1,210,457 / 1,849,568 x 100 = -65.4%.</Step>
<Step>Interpretation: over the 70 years the city lost 65.4% of its 1950 population, nearly two-thirds.</Step>
</Steps>

Example 2 (hypothetical): a new tram line changes the office rent curve. Before the tram, rent fell by 20 units per km from 300 units at the CBD; after the tram, the fall is 10 units per km. Compare the rent at 5 km.

<Steps>
<Step>Before the tram: R(5) = 300 - 20 x 5 = 200 units at 5 km.</Step>
<Step>After the tram: R(5) = 300 - 10 x 5 = 250 units at 5 km.</Step>
<Step>Change: +50 units, or +25% (50 / 200). The flatter curve makes the 5 km site worth more to offices, increasing their competitiveness further out. Whether they win a site depends on the bids of other uses.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Describing a change without figures. "The population fell" earns less than "Detroit's population fell from 1,849,568 in 1950 to 639,111 in 2020".
- Mixing up suburbanisation and counter-urbanisation: the first moves people to the edge of the city, the second moves them beyond the city's commuter belt.
- Calling any improvement gentrification. The defining feature is the arrival of richer residents and the displacement of earlier ones.
- Reading a bid-rent curve as the rent that is actually paid. It shows what each use could afford to pay, and the winning use changes only at a crossing on the highest-bid envelope.

## Summary

- Cities change in stages: suburbanisation, urban sprawl, counter-urbanisation and the rise of edge cities each move people or jobs in a different direction.
- Inner-city decline often starts with deindustrialisation and job loss; Detroit's population fell by 65.4% between 1950 and 2020.
- Urban regeneration reuses brownfield land, but it can cause gentrification and the displacement of earlier residents.
- Bid-rent theory explains land-use zones: a use wins a site when it can pay the most, and cheaper transport flattens the curves and moves the zones outwards.
- Global-city links, migration factors and land-use change can contribute to urban change; their roles need evidence beyond a rising population total.
- In extended answers, give a clear judgement and support it with a named case and its figures.
