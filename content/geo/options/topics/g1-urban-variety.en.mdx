## Big idea

Urban environments vary widely in size, age, wealth and layout, from megacities of more than 10 million people to planned cities built from scratch. Simple models show common patterns of land use, but real cities in LEDCs often differ from them, so every model has to be tested against evidence.

## Explanation

<Term id="g-urbanisation"/> is the rise in the share of a population that lives in towns and cities. It happens through rural-urban migration and through natural increase within urban areas. More people living in cities is not the same as a rising share, so keep the two ideas apart.

<Callout kind="define">Megacity: an urban agglomeration with more than 10 million inhabitants.</Callout>

The UN counts a <Term id="g-megacity"/> as an urban agglomeration of more than 10 million people. In 2018 the Lagos urban agglomeration had about 13.5 million people, and Cairo, Kinshasa and Lagos were the only megacities in Africa that year.

Cities differ by level of development. In MEDCs many city populations are stable or ageing, while many LEDC cities grow fast and strain housing, water and transport. Cities also differ by how they grew. A <Term id="g-conurbation"/> forms when neighbouring towns grow until their built-up areas merge, while a <Term id="g-primate-city"/> dominates its country’s economy, government and culture. Settlements form an <Term id="g-urban-hierarchy"/> in which a few large centres provide specialist services to many small places. Planned cities, such as Brasilia, are designed as a whole, but most cities grow without a single master plan.

Burgess (1925) described a city as rings of land use around the <Term id="g-central-business-district"/> (CBD), with the <Term id="g-zone-in-transition"/> next to it. This is the <Term id="g-concentric-zone-model"/>. Hoyt (1939) showed that high-rent housing spreads outwards as <Term id="g-sector-model"/> along transport routes. Harris and Ullman (1945) proposed the <Term id="g-multiple-nuclei-model"/>, in which the city grows around several separate centres.

Griffin and Ford (1980) built the <Term id="g-latin-american-city-model"/> because earlier models did not fit many Latin American cities. An elite sector runs along a commercial axis, housing quality falls with distance from the centre, and <Term id="g-informal-settlement"/> occupy the edge. The edge where the city meets farmland is the <Term id="g-rural-urban-fringe"/>.

Four cases show these ideas in practice. Dubai grew from about 0.86 million people in 2000 to 3.86 million at the end of 2024. Lagos grows faster than formal housing can keep up with. Dharavi in Mumbai is a dense <Term id="g-informal-settlement"/> close to the centre of a huge city. Singapore shows a <Term id="g-planned-city"/> approach, in which public housing is home to most resident households. <Term id="g-gated-community"/> sit in the wealthier sectors and sell security and private services.

## How it is examined

This topic appears in 10 of the 39 questions in Paper 1 Option G, from May 2019 to November 2025 (in 9 of the 13 papers): 5 structured questions and 5 extended answers. Each of the five structured questions starts from a map or a diagram of a city, so practise reading grid references, distances and direction.

Map parts are 1-mark tasks: state or identify a feature, or estimate a distance from the scale. Explain parts need two developed reasons, each linked to the place. Extended questions ask why economic activity or residential areas sit where they do, using physical and human factors (Examine, To what extent).

<Callout kind="exam">Marks go to named features, to developed explanations (a point, its development and a link to the place) and to case examples with a date, a figure and a source.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up the models: Burgess draws rings, Hoyt draws wedge-shaped sectors along transport routes, and Harris and Ullman draw several nuclei.
- Presenting a model as a map of a real city. Models are idealised, and a real city such as Dharavi is distorted by transport routes and local geography.
- Giving a case without a date or a source. Write "Dubai: about 3.86 million at the end of 2024 (Dubai Statistics Center)", not "Dubai is very big".
- Calling every informal settlement a slum with no services, or saying it is always temporary. Many have stood for decades and vary a lot in quality.

## Summary

- Urbanisation is the rise in the share of people living in towns and cities; a megacity has more than 10 million inhabitants.
- The concentric zone, sector and multiple nuclei models describe land use in idealised ways (Burgess 1925, Hoyt 1939, Harris and Ullman 1945).
- The Latin American city model (Griffin and Ford, 1980) adds an elite sector along a commercial axis and informal settlements on the edge.
- Planned cities, informal settlements and gated communities can exist in one city, so their structures overlap.
- A primate city dominates its country, and the urban hierarchy links large and small settlements.
- Support every answer with a dated figure and a source from the cases.
