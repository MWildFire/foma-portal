## Big idea

A river basin is shared by farmers, cities, factories, power plants and ecosystems, and often by several countries. Water management therefore means choosing between three broad strategies: reduce demand, add new supply, or agree how to share a river. Each choice has costs in money, energy, ecosystems and people, and future pressures (population growth, city growth, food demand and climate change) will test every option. A strong answer weighs these trade-offs with dated evidence instead of naming one "best" solution.

## Explanation

<Term id="a-integrated-water-resources-management"/> is a planning approach that treats water, land and related resources as one system. It links all users, from farmers and cities to industry, energy producers and ecosystems, and it plans across administrative boundaries rather than inside one town or ministry. The aim is to raise economic and social welfare without damaging the ecosystems that depend on the water. The UN reports progress on this country by country. In 2025 the UAE Ministry of Energy and Infrastructure cited a UN report that gave the UAE 82% for integrated water resources management.

The natural unit for this planning is the <Term id="a-river-basin-management"/> area: the whole drainage basin, from its headwaters to its mouth. Water consumed upstream or diverted out of the basin reduces what remains downstream; some withdrawn water returns, so a basin plan must account for both consumption and return flows. That needs shared data, agreed rules and an institution that can enforce them. The 1995 Mekong Agreement, signed by Cambodia, Laos, Thailand and Vietnam, created the Mekong River Commission for this purpose; China and Myanmar take part as dialogue partners.

<Term id="a-demand-side-management"/> reduces the amount of water that users need or take. Useful tools include:

- <Term id="a-water-pricing"/> sets the charge for use. A constant volumetric tariff raises the total bill with use; an increasing-block tariff also raises the marginal price above specified thresholds. Affordability depends on household needs, income and the allowance.
- <Term id="a-drip-irrigation"/> delivers water slowly to plant roots. It can reduce field withdrawals and some evaporation or runoff, but reduced recoverable return flows and expanded cropping can offset basin-scale savings.
- Fixing real leaks can reduce one component of <Term id="a-non-revenue-water"/>. This indicator also includes apparent losses and authorised consumption that is not billed; those are not all physically wasted water.

<Term id="a-water-recycling"/> expands usable supply and can substitute for freshwater withdrawals; it does not necessarily reduce the final service demanded. It supplies suitably treated wastewater for irrigation, industry or, after advanced treatment, drinking. In 2025 the UAE reported that it reuses 73% of its treated wastewater to irrigate landscapes in its cities.

<Term id="a-supply-augmentation"/> adds water instead of saving it. A dam creates a <Term id="a-reservoir"/> that stores river water for irrigation, drinking water, hydroelectricity and flood control, so it brings reliability, but it also has costs. Sediment builds up in the reservoir and slowly removes its storage (<Term id="a-reservoir-sedimentation"/>), and the sediment it traps no longer renews the delta and floodplain downstream. Releases change the flow pattern, which can harm fish and wetlands unless enough water is kept in the river (an <Term id="a-environmental-flow"/>). People living in the flooded area need <Term id="a-resettlement"/>, which can destroy livelihoods and community ties.

<Callout kind="define">Desalination: removing salt from seawater or brackish water so that it can be used as fresh water.</Callout>

<Term id="a-desalination"/> turns seawater into fresh water and suits coastal arid states with little rain and no permanent rivers. It is not free water. Reverse osmosis forces seawater through membranes at high pressure, which requires energy; actual use depends on salinity, technology, recovery and the system boundary, and it leaves <Term id="a-brine"/> that must be diluted or spread out before it is discharged. By 2025 Singapore had five desalination plants; the fifth, Jurong Island, opened in 2022.

<Term id="a-inter-basin-transfer"/> moves water from a basin with a surplus to one with a shortage. China’s South-North Water Diversion Project diverted more than 76.7 billion cubic metres to northern China over its first ten years, but it reduces flow in the source basin and needs large amounts of money and energy. <Term id="a-managed-aquifer-recharge"/> stores surplus water underground, where evaporation losses are low, so that it can be pumped later. <Term id="a-rainwater-harvesting"/> collects runoff from roofs and yards. Its yield depends on rainfall, catchment area, storage and losses; installation and treatment costs must also be assessed.

A <Term id="a-transboundary-river"/> crosses or forms an international border, and each country on it is a <Term id="a-riparian-state"/>. The Indus, the Nile and the Mekong are all transboundary rivers, and their management depends on treaties. Cooperation is possible when a treaty sets rules and shares data, but treaties can come under strain. The Indus Waters Treaty, signed in Karachi on 19 September 1960 with the World Bank as a party, gives the three eastern rivers to India’s unrestricted use and the three western rivers to Pakistan’s unrestricted use, with India limited to some uses and to storage only as the annexes allow. On 23 April 2025 India announced that it was holding the treaty in abeyance; Pakistan maintains that the treaty remains in force. On the Nile, Ethiopia’s Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam was inaugurated on 9 September 2025 with an installed capacity of 5,150 MW, and its reservoir is designed to hold about 74 billion cubic metres. Egypt’s allocation under the 1959 Nile Waters Agreement is 55.5 km³ a year. The dispute is about how fast the reservoir fills and what reaches Sudan and Egypt downstream.

Future scenarios test how strategies hold up under different conditions. A business-as-usual scenario extends current use; an integrated scenario combines demand cuts, reuse and new supply. Demand is growing: PUB’s forecast (page updated 22 April 2026) expects Singapore’s total water demand to almost double by 2065, with the non-domestic sector accounting for about 60% of it. Food is the largest user, because agriculture takes about 69% of global water withdrawals (FAO AQUASTAT). Climate change makes rainfall and river flows more variable, so a plan built on past averages can fail in a drought or flood year.

The UAE is the local case. Dubai has no permanent rivers, so the country relies on desalination and reuse. In 2025 the Ministry reported that non-conventional sources (desalinated seawater and treated wastewater) provide 53% of the country’s water supply, The reported capacity of over 3 million cubic metres a day refers specifically to the country’s wastewater treatment plants. Its Water Security Strategy 2036 aims to cut total water demand by 21% and to reuse 95% of treated wastewater. The same ministry statement reports reuse of treated wastewater for urban landscape irrigation. Aquifer sustainability still requires comparing groundwater abstraction with recharge; neither that statement nor an undated irrigation statistic establishes that all current irrigation uses groundwater.

## Worked example

<Steps>
<Step>Rain collected from a roof is area times rainfall: $120\,\text{m}^2 \times 0.15\,\text{m} = 18\,\text{m}^3$. This is an illustrative roof in a place with 150 mm of rain a year.</Step>
<Step>Water that reaches the tank, with an assumed runoff coefficient of 0.8: $18 \times 0.8 = 14.4\,\text{m}^3$, which is $14{,}400$ litres a year.</Step>
<Step>Household demand: 4 people at 100 litres each a day is $4 \times 100 \times 365 = 146{,}000$ litres a year. The harvest covers $\frac{14{,}400}{146{,}000} \approx 9.9\%$, about one-tenth of the household’s use.</Step>
</Steps>

<Steps>
<Step>Illustrative dam: reservoir capacity $500$ million m³, with a sediment inflow of $10$ million m³ a year, all assumed to settle in the reservoir.</Step>
<Step>Time to fill with sediment: $\frac{500}{10} = 50$ years. After 20 years the sediment would be $20 \times 10 = 200$ million m³, which is $\frac{200}{500} = 40\%$ of the capacity.</Step>
<Step>The estimate assumes a constant inflow and full settling of sediment. Real inflows change and trapping efficiency varies, so state the assumption in an exam answer.</Step>
</Steps>

<Steps>
<Step>Illustrative plant: output of $100{,}000$ m³ a day at an assumed $3.5$ kWh per m³, an illustrative assumption for the plant processes counted in this example, not a measured national average.</Step>
<Step>Daily energy: $100{,}000 \times 3.5 = 350{,}000$ kWh, which is $350$ MWh a day.</Step>
<Step>Annual energy: $350 \times 365 = 127{,}750$ MWh, about $128$ GWh a year, for the processes covered by the assumed 3.5 kWh/m³. Additional processes require separate energy estimates; do not add them again if they were already included.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Read any figures in the stimulus before you write, and quote them with their units and years. Structured questions ask you to describe, explain or suggest. Extended questions ask you to examine, evaluate or judge to what extent.

- Describe: give the pattern or features clearly, using the data in the stimulus.
- Explain: give a detailed account including reasons or causes; develop the links needed by the specific question.
- Suggest: propose a possible solution, hypothesis or answer that fits the question and evidence.
- Examine, Evaluate, To what extent: compare strategies, weigh benefits against costs, and reach a conditional judgement, for example "desalination suits a rich coastal state with little rain, but not a poor inland one".

<Callout kind="exam">Marks go to a named strategy, a developed explanation of how it works, and a link to a place. Use accurate, relevant case evidence and explain how it supports the argument. Figures, dates and sources help precision; they are not a universal quota for every credited example.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- Treating desalination as free water. Name both its energy use and the disposal of its brine.
- Calling a dam simply good or bad. Name who gains (farmers, cities, the power grid) and who loses (displaced people, farmers and fishers downstream, the delta).
- Assuming a treaty ends conflict. A treaty can be suspended, and its rules depend on shared data and on enforcement.
- Mixing units. A reservoir stores a volume in cubic metres, while a river carries cubic metres per second. One cubic metre per second is about 31.5 million cubic metres a year.
- Giving a target as a result. "The UAE aims to reuse 95% of treated wastewater by 2036" is a target, not a measured figure.
- Recommending a transfer as the answer to a whole region’s shortage without naming its costs in the source basin: lost flow, energy and displacement.

## Summary

- Water is planned at basin scale, and a transboundary river needs agreement between all the countries that share it.
- Demand management, efficiency and real-leak control can reduce the required supply. Reuse expands usable supply; relative costs and actual savings require local comparison.
- Supply options (dams, desalination, transfers, recharge and rainwater harvesting) add water but bring energy, cost and environmental trade-offs.
- Dams store water, but they lose capacity to sediment, change downstream flows and can displace people.
- Desalination is energy-intensive and produces brine, so its value depends on energy prices and on where it is used.
- Treaties such as the Indus Waters Treaty (1960) can be strained or suspended, so judge them by their rules and by how they are enforced.
- Plans must be tested against demand growth and climate change, and every figure needs a date and a source.
