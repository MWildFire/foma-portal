## Big idea

A 10-mark extended answer in option A is judged on how directly it answers the command word and how well each point is backed by evidence: a named place, a figure with its year, and a judgement that follows from that evidence. A general answer about rivers and floods stays at description, however long it is. One real case, used correctly, scores better than a list of cases that are only named.

## Explanation

Paper 1 lasts 90 minutes and has two options, so each option gets about 45 minutes. Option A has two kinds of question. The structured question has several short parts, usually worth 1 to 3 marks each. The extended question offers a choice: answer either part (a) or part (b), and each part is worth 10 marks.

Choose the extended question in about two minutes. Read both parts and pick the one where you already know a place, a year and a figure that fit. Then read the command word, because it sets the job of the whole answer. Examine asks you to look at causes or effects in detail and show how they connect. Evaluate asks you to weigh the strengths and limitations of an idea and judge how well it works. To what extent asks how far a claim is true, so the conclusion must give a degree, for example "true for flash floods, but not for river floods", and the conditions that change it. A plain yes or no misses the point of a to what extent question. The table in How it is examined lists the command words and what each one asks for.

<Callout kind="define">Judgement: the answer to the command word, with the degree and the conditions under which it holds.</Callout>

Use option A terms precisely, and define each one in a single clause the first time you use it. A storm hydrograph is a graph of discharge against time at the basin outlet, showing how a river responds to one storm. Lag time is the time between the storm centre (or the centre of mass of the storm) and the peak of discharge, and peak discharge is the highest discharge on the hydrograph after one storm. Flood mitigation reduces the size or the chance of a flood, while flood adaptation changes how people live with the risk. A term used without a definition earns less credit, and a term used in the wrong sense, such as water scarcity for pollution, breaks the link to the question.

Plan before you write, in two or three minutes, with five steps:

<Steps>
<Step>Step 1, definition: define the key term in one clause, and name the place you will use.</Step>
<Step>Step 2, first point: make the claim, name the place, give a figure with its year and source, and link it back to the command word.</Step>
<Step>Step 3, second point: do the same, from the other side of the question or with a second place.</Step>
<Step>Step 4, test: give a limit or a counter-argument, with its own place and a dated figure, that shows where the claim fails.</Step>
<Step>Step 5, judgement: answer the command word with a degree and the conditions that change it.</Step>
</Steps>

Write each point in four moves: the claim in the first sentence, the evidence (a named place, a year and a figure from a source you can name), the explanation of why this evidence supports the claim, and the link back to the question. A figure with no year is weak evidence, and a year with no place is weaker still. Compare: "Boscastle, Cornwall: a flash flood in 2004, up to 200 mm of rain in about five hours (HR Wallingford, 2005). The water rose within hours, so the warning time was short." The first sentence gives the examiner evidence to credit, and the second explains why it matters for the question.

Keep a few dated cases ready, and check each figure before you use it. These come from the option A topics:

- **Boscastle, Cornwall: a flash flood in 2004**: up to 200 mm of rain in about five hours (HR Wallingford, 2005).
- **Room for the River: the Netherlands’ river flood strategy**: 34 locations, €2.3 billion, 2006–2019 (Rijkswaterstaat).
- **Pakistan floods 2022: a national disaster**: 33 million affected and more than 1,730 deaths (World Bank).
- **Toledo, Ohio: algal toxin in the tap water (2014)**: in August 2014 a do-not-drink advisory covered about 440,000 residents of four counties (2010 figure) from 2 to 4 August (CDC MMWR, 2016).

In the last two or three minutes, check four things: the first sentence of your judgement answers the command word; there is a limit or a counter-argument; every paragraph names a place and a year; every term is defined.

<Callout kind="tip">Decide the judgement in your plan before you write. Then choose the arguments that test it, and check that the test really challenges it.</Callout>

## Worked example

Question: Evaluate the view that flood defences on the main river are the best way to protect towns from flash floods. [10]

Plan it in two or three minutes. The five steps show what each part must contain, so there is no need to write the full answer here.

<Steps>
<Step>Step 1, definition: a flash flood rises within hours of very heavy rain, usually in small, steep or built-up catchments. Hard engineering uses structures such as levees and flood walls to control the flow of water. Name the two cases you will test the view against: Room for the River: the Netherlands’ river flood strategy, and Boscastle, Cornwall: a flash flood in 2004.</Step>
<Step>Step 2, first point (for the view): large schemes on a river can protect many people. Evidence: Room for the River: 34 locations, €2.3 billion, 2006–2019 (Rijkswaterstaat), with improved safety for around 4 million people. Link: this supports the view for river floods, which the programme was built to manage, but not for a flash flood.</Step>
<Step>Step 3, second point (against the view): a flash flood gives little time to act. Evidence: Boscastle, Cornwall: a flash flood in 2004, up to 200 mm of rain in about five hours (HR Wallingford, 2005). Link: when water rises within hours, flood warning and planning matter a great deal, and a defence on the main river does not replace them.</Step>
<Step>Step 4, test: hard engineering can be overtopped, can move the risk downstream and needs maintenance. The Dutch programme cost €2.3 billion and required changes to the rivers and their floodplains, so its benefit has a price. Check the figures: "around 4 million" is an approximate estimate, so write "around" and give the source.</Step>
<Step>Step 5, judgement: partly true. Defences on a large river can protect many people, but a flash flood leaves little warning time, so the view holds only for river floods, and only when warning and planning are in place.</Step>
</Steps>

Each step becomes one or two paragraphs in the exam. The plan already holds the place, the years and the judgement, so the writing only has to connect them.

## How it is examined

The extended response is worth 10 marks and takes about 30 of the 45 minutes for the option. The examiner looks for four things: knowledge (correct terms and named places), application (the place is used to answer this question, not added as background), analysis (causes and effects are explained step by step) and evaluation (a judgement with its conditions). Length alone does not raise the level.

| Command word | What the answer must do | Where it appears in option A |
|---|---|---|
| Examine | Look at causes, processes or effects in detail, and show how they connect | 10-mark extended questions, the most common |
| Evaluate | Weigh the strengths and limitations of an idea, and judge how well it works | 10-mark extended questions |
| To what extent | Judge how far a claim is true, and state the degree and the conditions for it | 10-mark extended questions |
| Explain | Give reasons, with a clear link from cause to effect | Structured parts |
| Outline | Give the main features or points briefly | Structured parts |
| Describe | Give the features or the pattern, without the reasons | Structured parts, less often |
| Suggest | Propose possible reasons or actions, with a short justification | Structured parts, less often |
| State, Identify | Give a fact or name it, with no explanation | Structured parts, usually 1 mark |

In the Paper 1 papers from 2019 to 2025, every 10-mark option A question starts with Examine, Evaluate or To what extent. Outline, Explain, State and Identify are the most common structured command words, with Describe and Suggest used less often. Discuss does not appear in option A.

Marks go to a clear answer to the command word, named places with years and figures, explained links between cause and effect, and a judgement that follows from the evidence.

## Common mistakes

- Describing without explaining. The answer says what happens on a river or in a basin, but not why it matters for the question.
- No named place. "Many countries have water shortages" earns little credit. Name the country, the river or basin, and the year.
- Misreading a hydrograph. Lag time runs from the storm centre (or the centre of mass of the storm) to the peak of discharge. Measuring it from the start of the rain gives the wrong answer.
- Confusing mitigation with adaptation. Raised floors and insurance do not reduce the size or the chance of a flood; they reduce the harm when it comes, so they are adaptation.
- A judgement that restates the question. "Desalination is the most effective response" is not a judgement until it gives a degree and says where it fails.
- A figure without a year or source. "Many residents received contaminated water" is weak. Write the year and the source next to it, as in Toledo, Ohio: algal toxin in the tap water (2014), where a do-not-drink advisory covered about 440,000 residents of four counties (2010 figure) from 2 to 4 August (CDC MMWR, 2016).

<Callout kind="mistake">A correct figure with no place, year or source earns little credit. Write all three next to every number.</Callout>

## Summary

- Read the command word first: it sets what the answer must do.
