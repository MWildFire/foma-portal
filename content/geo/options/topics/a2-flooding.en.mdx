## Big idea

Floods are natural events, but their harm depends on where people live and how land is used. Heavy or prolonged rain, snowmelt and saturated ground create the water. Urbanisation, deforestation, channelisation and building on floodplains often make it arrive faster and higher. Flood management aims to reduce the size and the chance of floods, to help people live with the risk, and to weigh the costs and benefits of every scheme.

## Explanation

A <Term id="a-flash-flood"/> rises within hours of very heavy rain, usually in small, steep or built-up catchments, so the warning time is short. A <Term id="a-river-flood"/> happens when a river overtops its banks and spreads over the floodplain, usually after long or heavy rain or snowmelt upstream. <Term id="a-floodplain"/> is the flat land beside a river that is covered with water when the river overflows.

<Callout kind="define">Flood risk: the chance of a flood together with the harm it would cause to people, property and the economy.</Callout>

Natural causes include intense or prolonged rain, rapid snowmelt and <Term id="a-saturated-soil"/>, where the ground can take no more water. Human causes increase the chance or the size of floods. <Term id="a-impermeable-surface"/>, such as tarmac, concrete and roofs, stops water soaking in, so rain goes straight into drains. Deforestation removes the canopy and roots that intercept and hold water. <Term id="a-channelisation"/> makes water move downstream faster. Building on the floodplain puts homes and businesses in the path of floods.

A <Term id="a-storm-hydrograph"/> shows how river discharge changes after a storm. The rising limb shows how quickly water arrives, the <Term id="a-peak-discharge"/> shows how big the flood is, and the falling limb shows how slowly the river returns to normal. The <Term id="a-lag-time"/> between the storm centre and the discharge peak shows how quickly the catchment responds.

<Term id="a-infiltration"/> is the process by which rain soaks into the ground. Rain that cannot soak in runs off as <Term id="a-surface-runoff"/>.

Urbanisation changes the hydrograph in three ways. First, more rain runs off, so the total volume of the flood increases. Second, the peak discharge is higher, because more water reaches the river at the same time. Third, the lag time is shorter, so the peak comes sooner and less time is left to warn people. Pavements, roofs and drains replace soil and vegetation, which would otherwise store water and slow it down.

<Term id="a-flood-warning"/> uses rainfall and river-level data to predict floods, so that people can move possessions, evacuate or prepare.

<Term id="a-hard-engineering"/> uses structures such as dams, flood walls and barriers to control the flow of water. The IB guide calls these structural measures and counts afforestation among them, although trees are not concrete. Among hard measures are a <Term id="a-levee"/> along the river and a <Term id="a-flood-relief-channel"/> around a town. Hard engineering controls the water directly, but it can be overtopped, it can move the risk downstream and it needs maintenance.

<Term id="a-soft-engineering"/> works with natural processes and with people’s behaviour. Examples are <Term id="a-floodplain-zoning"/>, <Term id="a-flood-storage"/> on natural or engineered land, building design such as raised floors, insurance and preparedness.

<Term id="a-flood-mitigation"/> covers two groups of measures. Structural measures, such as dams, levees, relief channels, channel modification and afforestation, reduce the size or the chance of flooding. Planning, such as insurance, flood preparation and warning technology, helps to limit the losses. <Term id="a-flood-adaptation"/> is separate: it changes how people and places live with the risk, so the harm is smaller when a flood happens. Option D of the guide counts insurance as adaptation, so read the question’s wording before you classify it. Most real schemes combine these approaches.

Costs, benefits and trade-offs: a scheme is judged by the damage it avoids and the people and land it protects, set against its costs. Costs include construction and maintenance, environmental damage such as the loss of habitat, social costs such as people being moved, and insurance that becomes too expensive for poorer households. A trade-off arises when protecting one area increases the risk elsewhere. The Dutch programme in the case below shows this balance: it cost €2.3 billion and improved safety for around 4 million people, but it required changes to the rivers and their floodplains.

## How it is examined

Structured questions give a hydrograph, a table of data or a map, and use command terms to say what to do. Describe means give an account of what the data show, using figures. Calculate means show the working and give the answer with its unit or as a percentage. Explain means give reasons or causes, each developed and linked to the place. Suggest means propose a measure and justify it. Extended questions use Evaluate (weigh strengths and weaknesses and reach a judgement) and To what extent (argue both sides and say how far the idea holds).

<Callout kind="exam">Marks go to correct use of the data with the working shown, to developed explanations (a point, its development and a link to the place) and to case examples with a date, a figure and a source.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- Reading the rainfall as discharge. On a hydrograph, rainfall is usually drawn as bars and discharge as a line, in cubic metres per second.
- Saying that urbanisation makes more rain. The main effect is on runoff: more water reaches the river, and faster.
- Saying that a dam or a levee stops all floods. It reduces the risk up to its design standard, and a larger flood can overtop or breach it.
- Presenting hard engineering as having no side effects. Channelisation can move the flood downstream, and barriers can damage habitats.
- Giving a case without a date or a figure. Write "Room for the River: 34 locations, €2.3 billion, 2006–2019 (Rijkswaterstaat)", not "the Dutch built big walls".

<Callout kind="mistake">When you calculate a percentage change, divide by the original value. In the worked example that is 18 m³/s, not 36 m³/s.</Callout>

- Confusing mitigation with adaptation. Raised floors do not reduce the size of a flood; they reduce the harm when it comes. Insurance is planning under mitigation in this option, but adaptation in Option D.

## Summary

- Two main flood types here: flash (within hours, in small or built-up catchments) and river (overtopping of the banks).
- Natural causes are intense or prolonged rain, snowmelt and saturated soil; human causes are urbanisation, deforestation, channelisation and building on floodplains.
- Urbanisation adds impermeable surfaces, so there is more runoff, a higher peak discharge and a shorter lag time.
- Prediction and warning give time to act; hard engineering (dams, levees, relief channels, barriers, afforestation) and soft engineering (zoning, storage, building design, insurance, preparedness) reduce risk in different ways.
- Mitigation covers structural measures, which reduce the size or the chance of flooding, and planning, which limits the losses; adaptation reduces the harm when floods happen.
- Every scheme has costs, benefits and trade-offs: Room for the River cost €2.3 billion (2006–2019) and improved safety for around 4 million people (Rijkswaterstaat).
- Support answers with dated figures: Boscastle, 16 August 2004, up to 200 mm in about five hours (HR Wallingford, 2005); Pakistan, 2022, 33 million affected and more than 1,730 deaths (World Bank).
