## Big idea

Water is renewable, but it is unevenly distributed in space and time, and people can use it faster than it recharges or pollute it faster than rivers and aquifers can clean themselves. Scarcity can be physical, when there is too little water for demand, or economic, when water is there but people cannot pay for it or reach it. Quality matters as much as quantity: a river with plenty of flow can still be unusable if sewage, nitrates or arsenic make it unsafe.

## Explanation

<Term id="a-water-scarcity"/> means that the supply of freshwater is not enough for the needs of people, farms and industry. It has two main forms, and exam questions often ask you to tell them apart.

**Physical water scarcity.** In <Term id="a-physical-water-scarcity"/> the natural supply of renewable freshwater is too small, or too variable, for demand. The usual measure is the <Term id="a-falkenmark-indicator"/>, which divides renewable freshwater by population. Below 1,700 m³ per person per year a country is under water stress, below 1,000 m³ it has water scarcity, and below 500 m³ it has absolute scarcity. These thresholds are a guide. Damkjær and Taylor (2017) point out that the 1,700 figure is widely quoted, but the 1989 paper usually cited for it does not make that claim, so use the thresholds as a classification and say so in your answer.

<Callout kind="define">Physical water scarcity: demand for water is greater than the natural supply of renewable freshwater, or the supply is too variable to rely on.</Callout>

**Economic water scarcity.** In <Term id="a-economic-water-scarcity"/> the water exists in rivers or aquifers, but people cannot get it, because there is no investment in pipes, treatment or institutions, or because they cannot pay. In 2022, 2.2 billion people did not have safely managed drinking-water services (WHO, 2023). Many of them live where rain is adequate but the systems are weak, so this scarcity is created by money and governance rather than by nature.

**Drought.** A <Term id="a-drought"/> is a period when water availability is well below normal. Hydrologists separate four types, which often follow one another:

- <Term id="a-meteorological-drought"/> is a rainfall deficit against the normal for that place and season.
- <Term id="a-hydrological-drought"/> is low water in rivers, lakes, reservoirs and aquifers. It lags behind the rain, because stores buffer the deficit for a time, and it can last after the rain returns.
- <Term id="a-agricultural-drought"/> is soil moisture too low for crops at a given stage of growth, which cuts yields.
- <Term id="a-socio-economic-drought"/> is the point at which supply cannot meet demand, and people, farms and industry face shortages. Here drought becomes a human problem, and the way supply is managed decides how severe it is.

**Water quality.** Pollution enters water from <Term id="a-point-source-pollution"/>, which comes from one identifiable outlet such as a sewage pipe or a factory discharge, and from <Term id="a-diffuse-pollution"/>, which comes from many spread-out sources such as fertiliser and manure washed off fields, pesticides, urban runoff and road salt. Point sources are easier to measure and regulate, but diffuse sources often carry most of the nutrient and pesticide load. Untreated <Term id="a-sewage"/> adds organic matter, nutrients and pathogens. WHO estimates that about 1 million people die each year from diarrhoea that comes from unsafe drinking-water, sanitation and hand hygiene (WHO, 2023), which is why sewage treatment is a first priority in many countries.

**Nutrients, eutrophication and algal blooms.** Fertilisers and manure add nitrogen and phosphorus. <Term id="a-nitrate-pollution"/> leaches through soil into groundwater, and high nitrate levels are dangerous for bottle-fed infants. WHO sets the guideline at 50 mg/L as nitrate ion and at 3 mg/L as nitrite ion (WHO, 2022). Phosphorus tends to attach to soil particles and reaches rivers and lakes when soil erodes. In a lake or slow river these nutrients drive <Term id="a-eutrophication"/>: algae and plants grow rapidly, and when they die and decay, bacteria use up the dissolved oxygen, so fish die. Some blooms of blue-green algae release toxins. An <Term id="a-algal-bloom"/> that releases <Term id="a-microcystin"/> can make tap water unsafe, and boiling does not remove the toxin.

**Salinisation.** On irrigated land, water evaporates and leaves salts in the soil. Where drainage is poor, <Term id="a-salinisation"/> builds up over years and crop yields fall, so irrigation can damage the land it is meant to support.

**Arsenic in groundwater.** <Term id="a-arsenic-contamination"/> is natural. In some river deltas, arsenic in the sediments is released into groundwater under the chemical conditions of the aquifer. WHO estimates that about 140 million people in at least 70 countries drink water above the 10 µg/L guideline (WHO, 2022). Long-term exposure causes skin lesions and cancers. The answer is safer sources and regular testing, such as deeper wells, rather than closing an industry.

**Quality indicators and management.** Water quality is judged with indicators. <Term id="a-biochemical-oxygen-demand"/> (BOD) shows how much oxygen microbes use to break down organic matter. <Term id="a-e-coli"/> is a faecal indicator: WHO drinking-water guidance says it must not be detectable in any 100 mL sample. Management responses fall into two groups. One reduces the input: sewage treatment, better fertiliser use, buffer strips and control of salt. The other reduces demand: <Term id="a-scarcity-demand-management"/>, such as pricing, restrictions and leak repair. Cape Town used both in 2018. The City reported water losses of 16% against a national average of 36%, and it was installing devices on the properties of high users (SAnews, 2018).

<Steps>
<Step>Renewable freshwater per person: a country has 3.6 km³ of renewable freshwater a year, which is $3.6 \times 10^{9}$ m³, and a population of 4 million (illustrative figures). Divide the water by the population: $\frac{3.6 \times 10^{9}}{4 \times 10^{6}} = 900$ m³ per person per year.</Step>
<Step>Classify with the Falkenmark indicator: 900 is above 500 and below 1,000, so the country has water scarcity, not absolute scarcity. It would be in water stress only if the value were between 1,000 and 1,700.</Step>
</Steps>

<Step>The WHO guideline is 50 mg/L as nitrate ion, NO$_3^-$. To express it as nitrogen, multiply by the ratio of the molar masses: $\frac{14.01}{62.00} = 0.226$.</Step>

## How it is examined

Questions on this option use the same command terms as the rest of Paper 1. For this topic, expect the following.

Calculations, such as water per person per year or the mean of several nitrate readings, carry method marks. Show the division or the sum, state the unit and check that the answer is in a sensible range. A correct method with a slip in the last figure still earns most of the marks.

Describe and explain questions on water quality ask for a process: name the source, give the pathway (leaching, runoff, evaporation) and state the effect on the water or on people. Distinguish questions ask for a contrast with a reason on each side, such as physical against economic scarcity, or point against diffuse pollution.

Extended questions (Examine, Evaluate, To what extent) are marked with a markband. Marks go to developed points, to evidence with a figure, a date and a source, and to a judgement that weighs the arguments.

<Callout kind="exam">Marks go to named thresholds and guideline values with their units, to developed explanations of a process and to case evidence with a date, a figure and a source.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- Treating physical and economic scarcity as the same thing. A country can have plenty of rain and no pipes, or little rain and good desalination; decide which one the question describes.
- Forgetting the units. Mixing up mg/L and µg/L is a 1,000-fold error, and nitrate as NO3 is not the same as nitrate as nitrogen.
- Calling the Falkenmark thresholds laws. They classify water per person; they do not measure how well a country uses its water.
- Describing eutrophication only as "too much algae". The chain runs from nutrient input to algal growth, then decay, then oxygen loss, and the death of fish comes from the oxygen loss.
- Giving a case without a date, a figure and a source. Write "Toledo, August 2014: about 500,000 people received contaminated water for three days (US District Court, 2018)", not "Toledo had a water problem".
- Mixing up the drought types. Meteorological drought is the rainfall deficit; hydrological drought is the low water in stores. The second follows the first and can last after the rain returns.

## Summary

- Physical water scarcity means too little renewable water for demand; economic water scarcity means water exists but people cannot get it.
- The Falkenmark indicator divides renewable freshwater by population: below 1,700 m³ is water stress, below 1,000 m³ is water scarcity and below 500 m³ is absolute scarcity.
- Drought moves from meteorological to hydrological, agricultural and socio-economic forms; its human effect depends on how supply and demand are managed.
- Point sources come from one outlet and diffuse sources from many; sewage, fertilisers, pesticides and industrial effluent are the main pollutants.
- Nutrients drive eutrophication and algal blooms; when the algae decay, the oxygen falls, and some blooms release toxins such as microcystin.
- Arsenic in groundwater is natural: the WHO guideline is 10 µg/L. BOD, nitrate and E. coli are the main indicators, each with its own interpretation or guideline.
- Support every answer with a figure, a date and a source, such as Cape Town 2018, Bangladesh 2001 or Toledo 2014.
