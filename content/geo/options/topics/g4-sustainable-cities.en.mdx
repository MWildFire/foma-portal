## Big idea

Urban sustainability means meeting today's needs for housing, jobs, mobility, water and waste without reducing what future generations can do. It requires balancing environmental, economic and social goals, and accepting trade-offs when they conflict. This topic links the main tools (compact form, green infrastructure, sustainable transport, eco and smart design, community participation) to real cases.

## Explanation

<Callout kind="define">Sustainable development: development that meets present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (Brundtland Report, 1987).</Callout>

Sustainable urban development rests on three linked aims: environmental protection, economic efficiency and social equity. A plan that cuts emissions but makes housing unaffordable, or creates jobs by destroying green space, is not sustainable in the full sense.

<Term id="g-sustainable-urban-development"/>

Urban form can influence travel. A compact, mixed-use district can bring destinations closer and support walking, cycling and frequent public transport. The result also depends on where jobs and homes match, service quality and affordability. Low-density expansion can lengthen networks and journeys, but density alone does not determine every household’s mode.

<Term id="g-compact-city"/>

Green infrastructure is the second tool. Street trees, parks, green roofs and wetlands cool the air, support wildlife and give residents places to exercise. Water and waste belong to the same system: sustainable drainage slows the runoff of rainwater from hard surfaces, and the waste hierarchy ranks options so that prevention and reuse come before disposal in landfill.

<Term id="g-green-infrastructure"/>

Transport combines several objectives. One useful measure is modal split, the share of trips made by car, bus, rail, cycling and walking, and improving alternatives can influence it. Total distance, occupancy, energy, emissions and access also matter. Curitiba's bus rapid transit shows how far a bus system can go: dedicated lanes, fast boarding at stations and frequent services, with about 600,000 daily passenger journeys on eight Expresso/BRT lines in the municipal 2014 account. That count is distinct from the wider integrated network. Emissions benefits depend on occupancy and whether car journeys are replaced, not ridership alone. Cycling is a second route to lower emissions, but it needs a dense, connected and safe network. Copenhagen is the example: in 2023, 45% of trips to work or study in the city were made by bike, and the municipality's cycle superhighways grew from 35 km in 2020 to 64 km in 2023.

### Volume is not modal share

In a hypothetical commuting survey, 90 bicycle and 110 other trips give 90/200=45%. If both halve, the share remains 45/100=45%; if only bicycle trips halve, it becomes 45/155≈29.0%. A lower total does not establish a lower share. Remote working could change the share only through a difference in which modes lose trips or a switch between modes. Those are hypotheses, not causes demonstrated by Copenhagen’s table. The municipal report also flags a changed trip definition in the 2024 mobility report; compare its series cautiously.

Street allocation is another option. Freiburg’s municipality describes Vauban as car-reduced, not wholly car-free, with a former military site developed into a district containing traffic-calmed residential streets, a main access road and pedestrian/cycle areas. In a specific earlier study, Nobis (2003, p. 703) reports about 400 households without a private car, more than 40% of households in the part of Vauban where parking on private plots is prohibited. This is a dated result for that area, not a current district-wide share. Sustainable cities also use new planning models. The linked Masdar case separates official 2024 ESG facts from AP’s dated 2023 population estimate. The gap between the reported plan and occupancy invites questions about uptake; it does not by itself establish costs or a causal explanation.

No plan is free of costs. Pedestrian streets can reduce car access for shops, cycle lanes can remove parking, and an eco-district can raise house prices. Smart-city sensors and data can support decisions or operational control, but information alone does not guarantee lower resource use or fair access. Planners must weigh these costs openly, and consulting residents early, which is community participation, can reveal local problems that would otherwise be missed.

## Worked example

Take a city with 10,000 trips a day, each averaging 6 km. Before the plan the modal split is car 60%, BRT bus 25%, rail 5%, cycling 2% and walking 8%. The plan moves it to car 35%, BRT 40%, rail 10%, cycling 8% and walking 7%. The calculation uses the same illustrative factors as the simulation: 210.3 g CO2e per km for a car with one person (DESNZ 2023 average car, unknown fuel: 166.64 g direct plus 43.66 g indirect per vehicle-km), 80 g per passenger-km for the BRT bus (a teaching assumption, not a DESNZ value), 44.43 g per passenger-km for rail (DESNZ 2023 national rail: 35.46 g direct plus 8.97 g indirect), and speeds of 20, 24, 30, 14 and 5 km/h.

<Steps>
<Step>Trips before the plan: car 60% of 10,000 = 6,000; BRT 2,500; rail 500. Car passenger-km: 6,000 × 6 km = 36,000, so car emissions = 36,000 × 210.3 g = 7.571 t.</Step>
<Step>Bus and rail before the plan: 2,500 × 6 km = 15,000 passenger-km × 80 g = 1.200 t; 500 × 6 km = 3,000 passenger-km × 44.43 g = 0.133 t. Total before the plan: 7.571 + 1.200 + 0.133 = 8.904 t a day.</Step>
<Step>After the plan: car 3,500 trips give 4.416 t; BRT 4,000 trips give 1.920 t; rail 1,000 trips give 0.267 t. Total after the plan: 6.603 t a day. The reduction is 2.301 t, about 26%.</Step>
<Step>Average travel time per trip: car 18 min, BRT 15, rail 12, cycling 25.7, walking 72. Before the plan: 0.60 × 18 + 0.25 × 15 + 0.05 × 12 + 0.02 × 25.7 + 0.08 × 72 = 21.4 minutes. After the plan: 0.35 × 18 + 0.40 × 15 + 0.10 × 12 + 0.08 × 25.7 + 0.07 × 72 = 20.6 minutes.</Step>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Calling a city sustainable because it has one green feature, such as a park. Sustainability is judged across transport, energy, water, waste and social outcomes.</Callout>

- Quoting a modal split or an emissions figure without saying whether it counts trips or journeys, and which year it refers to.
- Describing BRT as "a bus with a lane". Dedicated lanes, fast boarding and frequent services are what make it rapid.
- Treating a smart city as sustainable in itself: sensors and apps do not replace policy and investment.
- Forgetting the trade-off. A car-free zone may cut emissions but also reduce trade for shops; a strong answer names both sides.

## Summary

- Sustainable urban development considers environmental, economic and social goals, including possible co-benefits and trade-offs.
- Compact mixed-use development can support shorter journeys and public transport, conditional on access, destinations and services.
- Green infrastructure cools cities, slows runoff and supports health; sustainable drainage and the waste hierarchy belong to the same system.
- Assess modal share alongside distance, occupancy, emissions and access; BRT, cycle networks and street restrictions can influence outcomes.
- Eco-city evidence must distinguish building performance, company registrations, dated population estimates and citywide outcomes.
- Quote case figures with place, year and source: Curitiba (1974, 2014, 2020), Copenhagen (2023), Vauban (Nobis, 2003), Masdar City (IRENA 2015, AP 2023, ESG 2024).
