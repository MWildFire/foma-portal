## Big idea

Urban sustainability means meeting today's needs for housing, jobs, mobility, water and waste without reducing what future generations can do. It requires balancing environmental, economic and social goals, and accepting trade-offs when they conflict. This topic links the main tools (compact form, green infrastructure, sustainable transport, eco and smart design, community participation) to real cases.

## Explanation

<Callout kind="define">Sustainable development: development that meets present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (Brundtland Report, 1987).</Callout>

Sustainable urban development rests on three linked aims: environmental protection, economic efficiency and social equity. A plan that cuts emissions but makes housing unaffordable, or creates jobs by destroying green space, is not sustainable in the full sense.

<Term id="g-sustainable-urban-development"/>

The shape of the city comes first. A compact city puts homes, jobs and services close together, so trips are shorter, public transport has enough passengers to run frequently, and more people can walk or cycle. Low-density sprawl does the opposite: roads and pipes spread over more land and the car becomes almost unavoidable.

<Term id="g-compact-city"/>

Green infrastructure is the second tool. Street trees, parks, green roofs and wetlands cool the air, support wildlife and give residents places to exercise. Water and waste belong to the same system: sustainable drainage slows the runoff of rainwater from hard surfaces, and the waste hierarchy ranks options so that prevention and reuse come before disposal in landfill.

<Term id="g-green-infrastructure"/>

Transport is where the trade-offs are sharpest. The key measure is the modal split, the share of trips made by car, bus, rail, cycling and walking, and cities shift it by making the alternatives faster and safer. Curitiba's bus rapid transit shows how far a bus system can go: dedicated lanes, fast boarding at stations and frequent services, with about 54,000 passengers a day in its first year. Cycling is a second route to lower emissions, but it needs a dense, connected and safe network. Copenhagen is the example: in 2023, 45% of trips to work or study in the city were made by bike, and the municipality's cycle superhighways grew from 35 km in 2020 to 64 km in 2023.

Restricting cars across a whole district is the most radical option. Vauban in Freiburg removes through traffic and parking from residential streets: in its first development section, almost half of the households chose to live without a private car in the project of 1997–1999, although estimates from different studies vary, so every figure needs its year and source. Sustainable cities also use new planning models. Masdar City in Abu Dhabi, launched in 2006 for 45,000 to 50,000 residents, shows how hard that target is: by 2023 about 15,000 people lived and worked there, and about 5,000 of them were residents.

No plan is free of costs. Pedestrian streets can reduce car access for shops, cycle lanes can remove parking, and an eco-district can raise house prices. Smart-city technology, with sensors and data for traffic, energy and waste, helps only when it changes policy and investment. Planners must weigh these costs openly, and consulting residents early, which is community participation, can reveal local problems that would otherwise be missed.

## Worked example

Take a city with 10,000 trips a day, each averaging 6 km. Before the plan the modal split is car 60%, BRT bus 25%, rail 5%, cycling 2% and walking 8%. The plan moves it to car 35%, BRT 40%, rail 10%, cycling 8% and walking 7%. The calculation uses the same illustrative factors as the simulation: 137 g CO2e per passenger-km for a car, 80 g for the bus, 43 g for rail, and speeds of 20, 24, 30, 14 and 5 km/h.

<Steps>
<Step>Trips before the plan: car 60% of 10,000 = 6,000; BRT 2,500; rail 500. Car passenger-km: 6,000 × 6 km = 36,000, so car emissions = 36,000 × 137 g = 4.932 t.</Step>
<Step>Bus and rail before the plan: 2,500 × 6 km = 15,000 passenger-km × 80 g = 1.200 t; 500 × 6 km = 3,000 passenger-km × 43 g = 0.129 t. Total before the plan: 4.932 + 1.200 + 0.129 = 6.261 t a day.</Step>
<Step>After the plan: car 3,500 trips give 2.877 t; BRT 4,000 trips give 1.920 t; rail 1,000 trips give 0.258 t. Total after the plan: 5.055 t a day. The reduction is 1.206 t, about 19%.</Step>
<Step>Average travel time per trip: car 18 min, BRT 15, rail 12, cycling 25.7, walking 72. Before the plan: 0.60 × 18 + 0.25 × 15 + 0.05 × 12 + 0.02 × 25.7 + 0.08 × 72 = 21.4 minutes. After the plan: 0.35 × 18 + 0.40 × 15 + 0.10 × 12 + 0.08 × 25.7 + 0.07 × 72 = 20.6 minutes.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Questions on this option use IB command terms. Describe means give the main features without reasons. Explain means give reasons or causes and link them to the point, for example with "because" or "so that". Suggest means propose a reasonable answer that is not the only possible one, and justify it. Examine means look at causes, effects or arguments in depth. Discuss, Evaluate and To what extent mean weigh arguments for and against, then reach a supported judgement.

<Callout kind="exam">Structured parts earn marks for each correct point and for each development of it. Quote a case-study figure with its place and year, because a bare number is weak evidence. An extended answer needs a clear thesis, at least two case-study examples and a judgement at the end.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Calling a city sustainable because it has one green feature, such as a park. Sustainability is judged across transport, energy, water, waste and social outcomes.</Callout>

- Quoting a modal split or an emissions figure without saying whether it counts trips or journeys, and which year it refers to.
- Describing BRT as "a bus with a lane". Dedicated lanes, fast boarding and frequent services are what make it rapid.
- Treating a smart city as sustainable in itself: sensors and apps do not replace policy and investment.
- Forgetting the trade-off. A car-free zone may cut emissions but also reduce trade for shops; a strong answer names both sides.

## Summary

- Sustainable urban development balances environmental, economic and social goals, and always involves trade-offs.
- Compact cities shorten trips and make frequent public transport viable; sprawl does the opposite.
- Green infrastructure cools cities, slows runoff and supports health; sustainable drainage and the waste hierarchy belong to the same system.
- Modal split is the key measure of transport sustainability; BRT, cycle networks and car-free zones shift it.
- Eco-cities and smart cities need policy and participation; Masdar City shows the gap between plan and outcome.
- Quote case figures with place, year and source: Curitiba (1974, 2014, 2020), Copenhagen (2023), Vauban (1997–1999, 2003), Masdar City (2006, 2023).
