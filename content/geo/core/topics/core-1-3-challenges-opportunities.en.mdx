## Big idea

Ageing populations and youth bulges change how many people each worker has to support. They create challenges for health care, pensions and public budgets, and opportunities for a larger labour force, a silver economy and the gains from migration.

## Explanation

A country’s age structure decides how many people each worker has to support. <Term id="core-ageing-population"/> describes a population in which the share of people aged 65 and over is rising, usually because birth rates have fallen and people live longer. In 2024, 10.2% of the world’s 8.2 billion people were aged 65 and over (UN DESA, World Population Prospects 2024). Japan is one of the most aged countries: 29.8% of its people were 65 and over in mid-2024 (UN WPP 2024 estimate; the Statistics Bureau of Japan counts 29.3% on 1 October 2024).

The <Term id="core-working-age-population"/> (people aged 15 to 64) is the group usually counted as able to work and support others; everyone else is a dependant. The <Term id="core-total-dependency-ratio"/> counts all dependants for every 100 people of working age:

$$
\text{Total dependency ratio} = \frac{\text{population aged 0-14} + \text{population aged 65 and over}}{\text{population aged 15-64}} \times 100
$$

The total has two parts. The <Term id="core-child-dependency-ratio"/> counts children, and the <Term id="core-old-age-dependency-ratio"/> counts older people:

$$
\text{Child dependency ratio} = \frac{\text{population aged 0-14}}{\text{population aged 15-64}} \times 100
$$

$$
\text{Old-age dependency ratio} = \frac{\text{population aged 65 and over}}{\text{population aged 15-64}} \times 100
$$

<Callout kind="define">A dependency ratio of 60 means 60 dependants for every 100 people aged 15 to 64. The total ratio is always the child ratio plus the old-age ratio.</Callout>

When fertility falls, the child ratio falls. When large generations reach 65, the old-age ratio rises. In Japan the old-age ratio is projected to rise from 50.7 in 2024 to 73.1 in 2050, so the number of people aged 15 to 64 for each person aged 65 and over falls from about 2.0 to 1.4 (UN DESA, World Population Prospects 2024; values calculated from the population counts).

A different pattern is a <Term id="core-youth-bulge"/>: a large share of young people, created by an earlier period of high birth rates. When that bulge reaches working age, the working-age share can rise faster than the dependent share. This can create a <Term id="core-demographic-dividend"/>, a potential for faster growth that is realised only if young people gain education and jobs. The dividend is temporary.

Governments respond with population policy. The reference point is <Term id="core-replacement-level-fertility"/>, about 2.1 children per woman; below it, a population shrinks in the long run. A <Term id="core-pronatalist-policy"/> tries to raise births. Singapore’s Baby Bonus (2001) did not stop the fall in the total fertility rate, from 1.41 in 2001 to 0.97 in 2024 (<Case id="core-1-3-case-singapore"/>). Russia’s maternity capital (2007) coincided with a rise from 1.42 in 2007 to 1.78 in 2015, but a Higher School of Economics report found that much of the rise reflected earlier births rather than more children. Hungary’s family measures, including a tax exemption for mothers of four or more children from 2020, came after the rate had already risen from 1.25 in 2010; it reached 1.63 in 2021 and was 1.41 in 2024 (World Bank).

An <Term id="core-antinatalist-policy"/> tries to lower births. China’s one-child limit, from 1980, is the best-known case (<Case id="core-1-3-case-china"/>). Fertility was already falling before 1980, from 6.1 in 1970 to 2.74 in 1980 (World Bank), so the policy was one cause among several. Iran’s family planning programme, started in 1989, was followed by a fall in the total fertility rate from 5.17 in 1989 to 2.00 in 2000 (World Bank).

Migration is both a challenge and an opportunity. Brain drain, the emigration of skilled people, can leave the origin country short of nurses, engineers and teachers. A gain happens when returning migrants bring back skills and investment; this is <Term id="core-brain-gain"/>. Links with the <Term id="core-diaspora"/> can bring investment and trade too. Remittances, the money migrants send home, are another benefit: in the Philippines they were 8.5% of GDP in 2025 (<Case id="core-1-3-case-philippines"/>).

Ageing also brings an economic opportunity. The <Term id="core-silver-economy"/>, meaning spending on health, housing, leisure and technology for older people, creates jobs and firms, and older people who stay in work add to the labour force. Ageing also strains budgets: many public pensions are a <Term id="core-pay-as-you-go-pension"/>, so fewer workers for each pensioner mean higher contributions, lower benefits or a higher retirement age. Health spending rises too, especially for <Term id="core-long-term-care"/>, which many older people need for everyday tasks.

**Demographic dividend and gender.** A demographic dividend is a period when the working-age share of the population is high and the dependency burden falls. Viet Nam’s share of people aged 15 to 64 rose from 58.36% in 1995 to 67.73% in 2024, and its age dependency ratio fell from 71.35 to 47.65 dependents per 100 working-age people (World Bank, WDI). The dividend is not automatic: it needs jobs and education for the working-age population. Sex ratios and gender rules matter too. China’s sex ratio at birth was 1.103 male births per female birth in 2024 (World Bank, WDI). UNODC found that women and girls made up 61% of detected trafficking victims in 2022 (Global Report on Trafficking in Persons 2024). The Philippines’ Magna Carta of Women (Republic Act 9710, 2009) states that the State ensures the substantive equality of women and men. <Case id="core-1-3-case-vietnam"/>

## Worked example

Country A is hypothetical. In 2024 it has 20 million people aged 0 to 14, 60 million aged 15 to 64 and 15 million aged 65 and over.

<Steps>
<Step>Child ratio: 20 ÷ 60 × 100 = 33.3.</Step>
<Step>Old-age ratio: 15 ÷ 60 × 100 = 25.0.</Step>
<Step>Total ratio: (20 + 15) ÷ 60 × 100 = 58.3, which equals 33.3 + 25.0.</Step>
</Steps>

In 2050 (projected), Country A has 15 million aged 0 to 14, 50 million aged 15 to 64 and 30 million aged 65 and over.

<Steps>
<Step>Child ratio: 15 ÷ 50 × 100 = 30.0, lower than 33.3.</Step>
<Step>Old-age ratio: 30 ÷ 50 × 100 = 60.0, more than double the 25.0 of 2024.</Step>
<Step>Total ratio: (15 + 30) ÷ 50 × 100 = 90.0, up from 58.3.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="What does 90 mean?">Every 100 people of working age support 90 dependants: 30 children and 60 older people. The number of children fell, but the older group grew faster, so the total burden rose.</Reveal>

## How it is examined

Calculate questions give marks for each ratio, so show every division and give the answer to one decimal place. Outline and explain questions need a development: say what the change does to workers, pensions, services or growth, not only what the change is. Examine questions (6 marks) need both sides, one named example with figures and a judgement. Infographic questions reward values quoted with their years, plus a calculation or comparison that uses them. Evaluate questions need a judgement with a condition, for example: the policy works only if the incentive is large enough to change the cost of raising children.

## Common mistakes

- Dividing by the total population instead of by the population aged 15 to 64.
- Putting the boundary ages in the wrong group: ages 0 to 14 are children, 15 to 64 are working age, and 65 and over are older people.
- Saying that an ageing population means a shrinking population; the age structure can change while the total stays the same or grows.
- Judging a pronatalist policy from one year’s birth figure; compare the trend over several years.
- Writing that brain drain is always bad; remittances and brain gain can offset the loss, so evaluate the net effect.

## Summary

- An ageing population raises the old-age dependency ratio; a youth bulge raises the share of young people.
- Total dependency ratio = (population aged 0 to 14 + population aged 65 and over) ÷ population aged 15 to 64 × 100.
- Japan’s old-age dependency ratio is projected to rise from 50.7 in 2024 to 73.1 in 2050 (UN DESA, World Population Prospects 2024).
- A demographic dividend is temporary and needs education and jobs for young people.
- Pronatalist and antinatalist policies often have smaller or slower effects than governments expect (Singapore, China).
- Brain drain is a challenge; remittances and brain gain can be opportunities, and the net effect depends on return migration and on how the money is used.
- Ageing puts pressure on pensions and health budgets, while the silver economy creates opportunities.
