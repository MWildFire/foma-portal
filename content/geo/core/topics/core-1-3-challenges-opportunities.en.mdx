## Big idea

Ageing populations and youth bulges change the balance between age groups. The effect on actual workers depends on employment, productivity and how support is financed. They create challenges for health care, pensions and public budgets, and opportunities for a larger labour force, a silver economy and the gains from migration.

## Explanation

A country’s age structure helps indicate potential demand for services and support; it does not count actual workers or people financially dependent on them. <Term id="core-ageing-population"/> describes a population in which the share of people aged 65 and over is rising, usually because birth rates have fallen and people live longer. For mid-2024, the UN WPP 2024 medium projection puts the world’s population at 8.2 billion, with 10.2% aged 65 and over. Japan is one of the most aged countries: 29.8% of its people were 65 and over in mid-2024 (UN WPP 2024 medium projection; the Statistics Bureau of Japan counts 29.3% on 1 October 2024).

The <Term id="core-working-age-population"/> (people aged 15 to 64) is an age classification, not a count of employed people: some are studying or out of work, while some people aged 65 and over work. The <Term id="core-total-dependency-ratio"/> counts people outside ages 15–64 for every 100 people in that age group:

$$
\text{Total dependency ratio} = \frac{\text{population aged 0-14} + \text{population aged 65 and over}}{\text{population aged 15-64}} \times 100
$$

The total has two parts. The <Term id="core-child-dependency-ratio"/> counts children, and the <Term id="core-old-age-dependency-ratio"/> counts older people:

$$
\text{Child dependency ratio} = \frac{\text{population aged 0-14}}{\text{population aged 15-64}} \times 100
$$

$$
\text{Old-age dependency ratio} = \frac{\text{population aged 65 and over}}{\text{population aged 15-64}} \times 100
$$

<Callout kind="define">A dependency ratio of 60 means 60 people aged 0–14 or 65 and over for every 100 people aged 15–64. It is a demographic proxy, not 60 actual financial dependants per 100 workers. The total ratio is always the child ratio plus the old-age ratio.</Callout>

Lower fertility tends to reduce the child ratio over time, other things equal. Ratios also depend on migration, survival and the size of the working-age denominator. A large cohort reaching 65 can raise the old-age ratio without proving that the number of contributors has fallen. In Japan the old-age ratio is projected to rise from 50.7 in 2024 to 73.1 in 2050, so the number of people aged 15 to 64 for each person aged 65 and over falls from about 2.0 to 1.4 (UN DESA, World Population Prospects 2024; values calculated from the population counts).

A different pattern is a <Term id="core-youth-bulge"/>: an unusually large young-adult share, often reflecting earlier high fertility and improved survival. Migration also matters. As large younger cohorts enter working ages, the working-age share can rise faster than the dependent share. This can create a <Term id="core-demographic-dividend"/>, a potential for faster growth that is realised only if young people gain education and jobs. The favourable age-structure window is temporary; the benefit is not automatic.

Governments respond with population policy. The reference point is <Term id="core-replacement-level-fertility"/>, about 2.1 children per woman in low-mortality populations with no net migration. Sustained fertility below replacement eventually tends towards decline under those assumptions; population momentum can sustain growth for decades. A <Term id="core-pronatalist-policy"/> tries to raise births. Singapore’s Baby Bonus (2001) did not stop the fall in the total fertility rate, from 1.41 in 2001 to 0.97 in 2024 (<Case id="core-1-3-case-singapore"/>). Russia’s maternity capital (2007) coincided with a rise from 1.42 in 2007 to 1.78 in 2015, but the Higher School of Economics expert statement distinguishes competing interpretations, including earlier timing of births, and says the 2007–2015 period cannot reliably isolate the programme’s demographic effect from other changes. Hungary’s family measures, including a personal-income-tax exemption on specified qualifying income for eligible mothers of four or more children, introduced on 1 January 2020, came after the rate had already risen from 1.25 in 2010; it reached 1.63 in 2021 and was 1.41 in 2024 (World Bank).

An <Term id="core-antinatalist-policy"/> tries to lower births. China’s one-child limit, promoted from 1980, is one example (<Case id="core-1-3-case-china"/>). Fertility was already falling before 1980, from 6.1 in 1970 to 2.74 in 1980 (World Bank), so the whole decline cannot be attributed to the one-child restrictions. Iran’s family planning programme, revived in 1989 after an earlier programme in 1967–1979, was followed by a fall in the total fertility rate from 5.17 in 1989 to 2.00 in 2000 (World Bank).

Migration is both a challenge and an opportunity. Brain drain, the emigration of skilled people, can leave the origin country short of nurses, engineers and teachers. <Term id="core-brain-gain"/> can benefit a destination through skilled immigration and an origin through returning migrants’ new expertise or diaspora knowledge exchange. Links with the <Term id="core-diaspora"/> can bring investment and trade too. Remittances, the money migrants send home, are another benefit: in the Philippines they were 8.5% of GDP in 2025 (<Case id="core-1-3-case-philippines"/>).

Ageing also brings an economic opportunity. The <Term id="core-silver-economy"/>, meaning economic activity serving older consumers (the European Commission’s 2018 study uses ages 50 and over), can create demand for health, housing, leisure and technology and opportunities for firms and workers, and older people who stay in work add to the labour force. Ageing can create financing pressure: many public pensions are a <Term id="core-pay-as-you-go-pension"/>, where current contributions or taxes finance current pensions. If contributors fall relative to pensioners and earnings and other financing do not offset this, budgets face pressure. Possible responses include higher employment or productivity, tax funding, contribution or benefit changes and later retirement. Demand for health and care services can also rise, especially for <Term id="core-long-term-care"/>, which many older people need for everyday tasks.

**Demographic dividend and gender.** A rising working-age share and a falling age dependency ratio can open a demographic-dividend opportunity; they do not establish an economic gain. Viet Nam’s share of people aged 15 to 64 rose from 58.36% in 1995 to 67.73% in 2024, and its age dependency ratio fell from 71.35 to 47.65 people outside ages 15–64 per 100 people in that age group (World Bank, WDI). The dividend is not automatic: it needs jobs and education for the working-age population. Sex ratios and gender rules matter too. The <Term id="core-sex-ratio"/> at birth is the number of boys born per 100 girls; it is normally slightly above 100. UNODC reports 39% women and 22% girls among detected trafficking victims: together, 61% (Global Report on Trafficking in Persons 2024, Chapter 1, p. 39, Figure 24). The estimate uses 68,090 detected victims in 135 countries, with data for 2022 or the most recent available year; it does not measure all undetected trafficking. Policy works on two fronts: <Term id="core-gender-equality-policy"/>, such as the Philippines’ Magna Carta of Women (Republic Act 9710, 2009), which states that the State ensures the substantive equality of women and men; and <Term id="core-anti-trafficking-policy"/>, such as the Philippines’ Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act (Republic Act 9208, 2003), which sets up policies against trafficking and mechanisms to protect and support trafficked persons. <Case id="core-1-3-case-vietnam"/>

## Worked example

Country A is hypothetical. In 2024 it has 20 million people aged 0 to 14, 60 million aged 15 to 64 and 15 million aged 65 and over.

<Steps>
<Step>Child ratio: 20 ÷ 60 × 100 = 33.3.</Step>
<Step>Old-age ratio: 15 ÷ 60 × 100 = 25.0.</Step>
<Step>Total ratio: (20 + 15) ÷ 60 × 100 = 58.3, which equals 33.3 + 25.0.</Step>
</Steps>

In 2050 (projected), Country A has 15 million aged 0 to 14, 50 million aged 15 to 64 and 30 million aged 65 and over.

<Steps>
<Step>Child ratio: 15 ÷ 50 × 100 = 30.0, lower than 33.3.</Step>
<Step>Old-age ratio: 30 ÷ 50 × 100 = 60.0, more than double the 25.0 of 2024.</Step>
<Step>Total ratio: (15 + 30) ÷ 50 × 100 = 90.0, up from 58.3.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="What does 90 mean?">There are 90 people outside ages 15–64 per 100 in that age group: 30 children and 60 older people. This does not establish how many are employed or financially dependent. The numerator rose from 35 to 45 million and the denominator fell from 60 to 50 million; both changes raise the demographic ratio. No financial burden is measured.</Reveal>

## Common mistakes

- Dividing by the total population instead of by the population aged 15 to 64.
- Putting the boundary ages in the wrong group: ages 0 to 14 are children, 15 to 64 are working age, and 65 and over are older people.
- Saying that an ageing population means a shrinking population; the age structure can change while the total stays the same or grows.
- Judging a pronatalist policy from one year’s birth figure; compare several years and distinguish a trend from the policy’s causal effect, which needs a credible comparison without the policy.
- Writing that brain drain is always bad; remittances and brain gain can offset the loss, so evaluate the net effect.

## Summary

- A rising share aged 65 and over does not alone determine the old-age dependency ratio: check the 15–64 denominator. A youth bulge is a large young cohort.
- Total dependency ratio = (population aged 0 to 14 + population aged 65 and over) ÷ population aged 15 to 64 × 100.
- Japan’s old-age dependency ratio is projected to rise from 50.7 in 2024 to 73.1 in 2050 (UN DESA, World Population Prospects 2024).
- The favourable age-structure window is temporary; realising growth requires education and jobs.
- Fertility trends before and after a policy do not isolate its causal effect; other changes and a credible counterfactual matter.
- Brain drain is a challenge; remittances and brain gain can be opportunities, and the net effect depends on return migration and on how the money is used.
- Ageing can increase demand for care; pension financing depends on actual contributors, earnings and funding rules. The silver economy can create opportunities.
