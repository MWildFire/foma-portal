## Big idea

Responding to climate change works in two ways. Mitigation cuts the causes, which are the greenhouse gases that warm the planet. Adaptation reduces the impacts that are already happening or cannot be avoided. International agreements set the rules, but most action happens in countries, regions and cities, and every response has costs, winners and losers.

## Explanation

**Two responses.** <Term id="core-mitigation"/> is any human action that cuts greenhouse gas emissions or strengthens the natural sinks that absorb them. <Term id="core-adaptation"/> is the adjustment of human or natural systems to the actual or expected climate and its effects. Mitigation acts on the cause, so its benefit is global and slow to build up. Adaptation acts on the effects, so its benefit is local and often immediate.

<Callout kind="define">Mitigation tackles the causes of warming; adaptation tackles its impacts. Keep these two ideas separate in every answer.</Callout>

**Mitigation options.** Four groups matter most:

- <Term id="core-renewable-energy"/>, such as wind, solar and hydro, replaces coal and gas in electricity. Germany and the UAE show how fast a share can rise.
- <Term id="core-energy-efficiency"/> gives more useful output per unit of energy, for example through insulation and efficient vehicles.
- <Term id="core-carbon-pricing"/> and <Term id="core-emissions-trading"/> put a cost on emissions, so cuts happen where they are cheapest.
- <Term id="core-afforestation"/> stores carbon while the trees live. <Term id="core-carbon-capture-and-storage"/> captures CO₂ at large plants and stores it underground, at high cost.

**Adaptation options.**

- <Term id="core-flood-defence"/>, such as dykes and storm surge barriers, lowers the chance of flooding but needs maintenance and can fail.
- <Term id="core-early-warning-system"/> give people time to move or evacuate, but only if the warnings are understood and a response plan exists.
- Drought-resistant crops and water storage reduce harvest losses.
- <Term id="core-nature-based-solutions"/>, such as mangrove planting or wetland restoration, reduce flood and heat risk and also provide habitat.
- <Term id="core-managed-retreat"/> is the planned relocation of people and assets from areas of severe hazard. It is usually the last option.

**International framework.** The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), adopted on 9 May 1992, set the aim of preventing dangerous human interference with the climate system and sets out the principle of <Term id="core-common-but-differentiated-responsibilities"/>. The Kyoto Protocol (adopted 11 December 1997, in force 16 February 2005) set binding targets for developed countries: at least 5% below 1990 levels in 2008 to 2012. The Paris Agreement (adopted 12 December 2015, in force 4 November 2016) aims to hold warming well below 2 °C and to pursue 1.5 °C. Each country submits a <Term id="core-nationally-determined-contribution"/> and updates it every five years.

**National and local responses.** <Case id="core-2-3-case-dubai-solar-park"/> Dubai’s solar park shows a mitigation response, and <Case id="core-2-3-case-netherlands-delta-works"/> the Dutch Delta Works show engineered adaptation. A response works when it fits the hazard, is funded for the long term and is maintained.

**Trade-offs and equity.** Every response raises the question of who pays and who benefits. Renewables and efficiency need large upfront investment that poorer countries struggle to finance. A carbon price raises fuel and power costs, which hit poorer households hardest unless the revenue is returned to them. Flood defences protect the area behind them but can shift the risk elsewhere. Managed retreat removes people from danger but can break communities and livelihoods.

<Term id="core-loss-and-damage"/> is the harm that remains after mitigation and adaptation. At COP27 in November 2022 governments agreed to provide funding for it. <Case id="core-2-3-case-pakistan-floods"/>

**Geoengineering.** Geoengineering is a contested option. Solar radiation management (SRM) would reduce the sunlight reaching the surface, for example by injecting reflective particles into the stratosphere. It could cool the planet quickly, but it would not remove CO₂ and would have to be sustained. Carbon dioxide removal (CDR) takes CO₂ out of the air, for example through afforestation or direct air capture. It addresses the cause, but it is costly and its effects at scale are uncertain. Critics fear that either option could weaken the pressure to cut emissions.

## Worked example

**Example 1: Calculate (2 marks).** Using the world figures from Figure 1 and Ember’s yearly data, find the percentage increase in electricity generated from renewables between 2015 (5,519.5 TWh) and 2025 (10,701.6 TWh).

<Steps>
<Step>Increase: 10,701.6 − 5,519.5 = 5,182.1 TWh.</Step>
<Step>Percentage increase: 5,182.1 ÷ 5,519.5 × 100 = 93.9%.</Step>
<Step>Check: generation nearly doubled over the decade (about 1.94 times), so an increase of about 94% is sensible. Give the units and the years.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Percentage points or percentage change?">The share of renewables rose by 10.6 percentage points, but that is a different measure. Percentage points compare two percentages. A percentage increase compares the size of the quantity itself. Read the question to see which one it asks for.</Reveal>

**Example 2: Explain (4 marks).** Explain why renewable energy and energy efficiency are often combined in a mitigation strategy.

<Steps>
<Step>Point 1: renewables cut the emissions of each unit of electricity, because wind and solar release almost no CO₂ while they generate.</Step>
<Step>Point 2: efficiency reduces the amount of electricity needed, so a smaller renewable system can meet demand.</Step>
<Step>Development: together they lower both the emissions per unit and the total demand, which makes a national target cheaper to reach.</Step>
<Step>Qualifier: efficiency needs money up front, and renewable output varies with the weather, so the two work best with storage and demand management.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up the two responses: flood defences and early warning are adaptation, while renewables and afforestation are mitigation, although trees can also help adaptation.
- Describing adaptation as a way to stop warming. It reduces impacts; it does not change the temperature trend.
- Treating renewable energy as zero-carbon in every case, or forgetting that wind and solar output depends on the weather.
- Giving a percentage change when part (b) asks for percentage points.
- Describing the Paris Agreement as one common target for all countries. Each country sets its own target inside its NDC.

## Summary

- Mitigation cuts greenhouse gas emissions or removes CO₂ and acts on the cause; adaptation reduces impacts and acts on the effects.
- The main mitigation responses are renewables, energy efficiency, carbon pricing and emissions trading, afforestation and carbon capture and storage.
- Flood defences, early warning systems, nature-based solutions and managed retreat are the main adaptation responses.
- The UNFCCC (1992), the Kyoto Protocol (1997) and the Paris Agreement (2015) set the framework, and each country updates its NDC every five years.
- Common but differentiated responsibilities and loss and damage put the question of who pays and who benefits at the centre of negotiations.
- Support answers with named cases and figures: the Delta Works, Dubai’s solar park and Pakistan’s 2022 floods in this topic.
