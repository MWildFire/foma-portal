## Big idea

A 10-mark Section C essay on climate change is one argument, not a list of causes, effects or responses. You answer the question in a clear thesis, support it with developed analytical paragraphs using relevant, accurate evidence, weigh the other side, and finish with a direct answer. Marks follow the judgement and the evidence, not the amount of description.

## Explanation

A Section C essay is worth 10 marks. It tests four things: accurate knowledge of climate change (its causes, consequences and responses), application of that knowledge to named places, analysis of how causes and effects link, and a judgement that answers the question. Clear communication (a logical structure and precise terms) supports all four.

**The structure.** One possible practice structure, as in Unit 1, is:

- Introduction (3 to 4 sentences): define the key term briefly, then state your thesis. The thesis answers the question and says how far it is true.
- Three developed points: each makes a claim, explains the mechanism, supports it with relevant evidence and links back to the question; use accurate dates, units and figures where they help.
- Evaluation: weigh the other side, state the conditions under which your view holds, and say which factor matters most.
- Conclusion (1 to 2 sentences): answer the question directly. Do not add new evidence.

<Callout kind="define">Mitigation (mitigation) means reducing greenhouse gas emissions or strengthening their removal, so that climate change is smaller. Adaptation (adaptation) means adjusting natural or human systems to the actual or expected climate and its effects, so that harm is reduced. Vulnerability concerns susceptibility to harm, including sensitivity and limited capacity to cope and adapt. In the IPCC AR5/AR6 risk framework, distinguish it from exposure: risk arises through their interaction with the hazard. Resilience (resilience) is the ability to absorb a shock and recover from it.</Callout>

**What the command term changes.** The command term tells you what kind of answer is needed:

- Discuss: give a balanced review of the arguments for and against, supported by evidence, and reach a reasoned conclusion.
- Evaluate: weigh the strengths and limitations of a response or a factor, and judge which is more important or more reliable.
- To what extent: decide how far something is true or how much it matters. A partial answer is valid if you argue why it is partial.
- Examine: explain how and why something happens, showing the links and interactions between causes rather than just listing them.

**Evidence.** Unit 2 offers measured evidence: the carbon dioxide record, sea-ice extent, national emission totals, disaster losses and the cost of protection. Choose relevant named evidence and check its definitions, date and units. Give approximate figures with "about" or "around", check the year, and keep the rounding of your source. If you cannot recall a verified figure, use a qualitative example and say so.

**Balance.** The highest band rewards balanced analysis and evaluation appropriate to the question. Where the question asks for a judgement, argue both sides, compare the evidence and state which is stronger. A conclusion that says "both are important" without a reason earns little.

**Causes, feedbacks and natural factors.** Climate questions often ask whether human activity or natural factors explain warming. Name the mechanisms: the enhanced greenhouse effect from emissions of carbon dioxide and other gases; positive feedbacks that amplify warming, such as the ice–albedo feedback; and natural drivers such as solar output, volcanic eruptions and orbital cycles. Then judge their size and time scale using attribution evidence.

**Time and planning.** In these practice essays you have about 20 minutes. Spend about three minutes on the plan: the thesis, the three points with their places and figures, and the judgement. A short plan helps the structure; marks depend on the full response, not a separate planning allocation.

**Markbands.** A 10-mark essay is placed in one of five bands: 1–2, 3–4, 5–6, 7–8 or 9–10 marks. Each band describes knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation. The markschemes in this topic paraphrase those descriptors in general words; the exact wording is in the Geography guide.

## Worked example

Two students plan the same question with different theses. Both plans are valid, because the examiner rewards a clear judgement supported by named evidence. The difference is which response each student judges to matter more, and over what time scale.

**Question:** Evaluate the claim that mitigation should take priority over adaptation when responding to climate change. Use named places. (10 marks, 20 minutes)

**Plan A: mitigation first, adaptation where exposure is immediate**

<Steps>
<Step>Thesis: mitigation should lead the long-term response because it limits the scale of change, but adaptation must come first where exposure is immediate and the capacity to respond is low.</Step>
<Step>Point 1 (mitigation, the cause): the NOAA annual mean at Mauna Loa rose from 316.91 ppm in 1960 to 427.35 ppm in 2025 (NOAA GML). Adaptation has to absorb a larger change while concentrations keep rising.</Step>
<Step>Point 2 (mitigation, the high emitters): China's share of world fossil CO₂ emissions rose from 14.3% in 2000 to 31.8% in 2024 (Global Carbon Project, 2025). Cuts must reach the economies where emissions are still growing.</Step>
<Step>Point 3 (adaptation, now): the Netherlands built the Delta Works after the 1953 flood, which drowned 1,836 people (Stichting Watersnoodmuseum). Defences protect people in the short run, whatever other countries do.</Step>
<Step>Judgement: mitigation has priority over the long run, because the scale of later adaptation depends on today's emissions. Adaptation has priority where exposure is immediate. The time frame and the place decide which comes first.</Step>
<Step>Conclusion: mitigation first for the long-run trend; adaptation first for exposed places now.</Step>
</Steps>

**Plan B: adaptation first, because exposure is immediate**

<Steps>
<Step>Thesis: adaptation has priority for people who are already exposed, because mitigation works slowly and its benefits come later, while the losses happen now.</Step>
<Step>Point 1 (adaptation, now): Pakistan's 2022 floods affected 33 million people and caused combined damages and economic losses above USD 30 billion, close to 70% of them in Sindh province (World Bank, 2022). Protection and reconstruction cannot wait for global emission cuts.</Step>
<Step>Point 2 (the emissions gap): Pakistan emitted about 0.49% of the world total of annual CO₂ emissions in 2023, or 186.9 million tonnes out of 38,094 million tonnes (Our World in Data, using Global Carbon Project data). Adaptation is needed where emissions are small, which is a question of fairness as well as of risk.</Step>
<Step>Point 3 (counter-evidence): mitigation still matters. Concentrations rose from 316.91 ppm in 1960 to 427.35 ppm in 2025 (NOAA GML), so the hazard that adaptation must meet keeps growing without cuts.</Step>
<Step>Judgement: adaptation leads where exposure is immediate and capacity is low; mitigation leads for the long-run trend. The priority depends on time frame and place, so the essay should say which.</Step>
<Step>Conclusion: adaptation first for exposed places now; mitigation first for the long-run trend.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Which plan scores higher?">Either thesis can be defended. Develop and evaluate the argument using relevant, accurate evidence; a plan alone cannot establish a markband. Plan A is stronger on the long-run cause; Plan B is stronger on people who are already exposed. The better essay states the time frame in the judgement, because the time frame decides the priority.</Reveal>

## How it is examined

Use the school’s printed Section C instructions and timing. The five 10-mark bands in the actual May 2019 Paper 2 markscheme (printed pp.3–4) assess the whole response, not a fixed number of countries, statistics or paragraphs. A 7–8 response can remain unbalanced; the 9–10 band requires well-developed evaluation and a justified, balanced conclusion where appropriate. Our question-specific checklists are revision guidance, not fixed marks per bullet. See the [Unit 1 essay method](#/s/geo/t/core-essay-1) for a fuller paraphrase.

Answer the exact command, explain mechanisms, integrate relevant evidence and assess its limits. Use a named case when the question requests one. Do not invent a figure or assume every point requires a number.

## Common mistakes

- Treating weather as climate. A cold winter or one storm does not prove or disprove long-term warming; use decades of data and a named record, such as the Mauna Loa series.
- Making causal claims without evidence, such as "climate change caused this flood" or "Kyoto failed". Use event-specific attribution evidence before claiming that climate change increased this event’s likelihood or severity; otherwise explain the mechanism as a hypothesis and separate hazard, exposure and vulnerability.
- Using a generic case ("in many countries") with no name, figure or year. A named place with a verified figure, year and source is worth far more.
- Equating vulnerability with poverty. Sensitivity and the capacity to cope and adapt affect vulnerability; assess exposure separately. Income alone decides neither vulnerability nor total risk.
- Ending with a conclusion that repeats the introduction or adds a new case study. The conclusion gives the answer and nothing more.

## Summary

- A 10-mark essay is one argument: a thesis, developed analytical points, a judgement and a direct conclusion; three points are one workable practice structure, not a markband quota.
- Each developed point needs a relevant mechanism, appropriate evidence and a link to the question; numerical evidence is useful where relevant, with its date and units.
- Mitigation reduces greenhouse gas emissions or strengthens their removal; adaptation adjusts to a climate that is already changing. Judge them by time frame and exposure.
- International agreements are judged by measured emissions, not only by their targets and the number of Parties.
- Consequences are uneven between countries and within them: exposure, sensitivity and capacity to adapt decide the scale of loss.
- Natural factors can modulate warming, especially in the short term; attribution evidence shows that human influence is the main driver of recent warming.
- Spend about three minutes planning; planning helps structure the answer; marks assess the whole response.
