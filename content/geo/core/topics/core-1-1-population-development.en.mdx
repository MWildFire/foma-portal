## Big idea

Birth rates, death rates and population structure change together as a country develops. The demographic transition model, the population pyramid and the development indicators (HDI and GNI per capita) describe that change, and each one helps you say where a country is on it.

## Explanation

**Measuring change.** Population change is measured in rates per 1000 people. The <Term id="core-crude-birth-rate"/> is the number of live births in a year per 1000 people; the <Term id="core-crude-death-rate"/> is the number of deaths per 1000. Their difference is <Term id="core-natural-increase"/>: positive when births outnumber deaths, negative when deaths outnumber births. It excludes migration. The <Term id="core-total-fertility-rate"/> counts children per woman: the average number a woman would have over her lifetime at the birth rates of a given year. About 2.1 children per woman keeps a population stable where child mortality is low; below that, a population shrinks without migration.

$$\text{CBR} = \frac{\text{live births in a year}}{\text{population}} \times 1000$$

<Callout kind="define">Natural increase = crude birth rate minus crude death rate, per 1000 people.</Callout>

**Demographic transition.** The <Term id="core-demographic-transition-model"/> shows how birth and death rates change as a country develops, in five stages. Examples use UN WPP 2024 values for 2023 unless a year is given.

- **Stage 1, high stationary:** both rates are high, so growth is slow. WPP data start in 1950, so this stage is shown with an early example: Afghanistan in 1950, with a birth rate of 49.4 and a death rate of 37.4 per 1000, and natural increase of 12 per 1000 (UN WPP 2024). The classic textbook case, pre-industrial England, has no WPP figures.
- **Stage 2, early expanding:** the death rate falls quickly, partly because fewer infants die (see <Term id="core-infant-mortality-rate"/>), but the birth rate stays high, so natural increase peaks. Example: Niger, with a birth rate of 41.9 and a death rate of 8.9 per 1000, giving 33.0 per 1000.
- **Stage 3, late expanding:** the birth rate falls, so natural increase slows but stays positive. Example: Bangladesh, where the total fertility rate fell from 6.7 in 1975 to 2.2 in 2023.
- **Stage 4, low stationary:** both rates are low and natural increase is close to zero. Example: the United Kingdom, with a birth rate of 10.0 and a death rate of 9.5 per 1000.
- **Stage 5, declining:** the death rate is above the birth rate, so the population falls. Example: Japan, with a birth rate of 6.0 and a death rate of 12.3 per 1000, a natural decrease of about 6 per 1000.

Countries move through the stages at different speeds, and migration or policy can change the pattern.

The <Term id="core-population-pyramid"/> shows each sex’s share of every age group, and its shape records the past. A wide base means many children (stages 1 and 2). A bulge in the middle and a wider top appear as death rates fall and people live longer (stages 3 and 4). A narrow base with a wide top shows an ageing population (stage 5). In 2023 the median age was 15.2 years in Niger and 49.0 years in Japan (UN WPP 2024).

**Malthus and Boserup** offered two views of population and food. <Term id="core-malthus-theory"/> (1798) argued that population grows geometrically while food supply grows arithmetically, so growth is checked through <Term id="core-positive-preventive-checks"/>: famine, disease and war, or later marriage and fewer births. <Term id="core-boserup-theory"/> (1965) argued the reverse: population pressure pushes farmers to adopt new methods, so food output rises with population. Neither fits every case; technology, institutions and the pace of growth decide the outcome, and every area has a <Term id="core-carrying-capacity"/>.

**Development and population structure.** The <Term id="core-human-development-index"/> is a score from 0 to 1 built from three parts: <Term id="core-life-expectancy"/>, years of schooling and <Term id="core-gni-per-capita"/>. In 2023 Niger scored 0.419 and Japan 0.925 (UNDP, Human Development Report 2025). Higher income and schooling usually lower birth rates, and better health care ages the population. A high <Term id="core-dependency-ratio"/> (dependants per 100 working-age people) means spending on schools in a young country, and on pensions and health care in an ageing one.

**Distribution.** Population is unevenly spread. Physical factors attract people to fertile lowlands, reliable water, flat land and mild climates; human factors, such as jobs, transport and services, attract them to cities and industrial areas. Most people live in the core, a region of high density and economic activity, while the periphery has fewer people and fewer services. The contrast is large: in 2024 Bangladesh had 1,333 people per km² of land, while Mongolia had 2.3 (World Bank, EN.POP.DNST). The World Bank groups economies as low, middle or high income by gross national income per person, and comparing income groups helps explain why jobs and services, and so people, concentrate in some places.

## Worked example

**Example 1: natural increase in Niger, 2023** (crude birth rate 41.9 and crude death rate 8.9 per 1000, UN WPP 2024).

<Steps>
<Step>Natural increase = crude birth rate minus crude death rate.</Step>
<Step>41.9 − 8.9 = 33.0 per 1000 people.</Step>
<Step>As a percentage: 33.0 ÷ 10 = 3.3% a year, before migration.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why divide by 10?">A rate per 1000 is a fraction of a thousand. One per cent equals 10 per 1000, so dividing by 10 converts the rate into a percentage.</Reveal>

**Example 2: the stage of Japan, 2023** (birth rate 6.0 and death rate 12.3 per 1000, UN WPP 2024).

<Steps>
<Step>Compare the two rates: the death rate (12.3) is higher than the birth rate (6.0).</Step>
<Step>So natural change is negative, a natural decrease of about 6 per 1000: this is stage 5 (declining).</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Using the total fertility rate as if it were the birth rate: the TFR counts children per woman, while the birth rate counts births per 1000 people.
- Forgetting to multiply by 1000: 43,700 ÷ 1,850,000 is 0.024, which must be multiplied by 1000 to give 23.6 per 1000.
- Calling natural increase "population growth": total change also includes net migration.
- Calling a country stage 4 when its death rate is above its birth rate; that is stage 5.
- Writing about Malthus or Boserup without a named example or a judgement, which keeps the answer out of the top bands.

## Summary

- Natural increase is the crude birth rate minus the crude death rate, per 1000; migration is excluded.
- The total fertility rate counts children per woman; about 2.1 keeps a low-mortality population stable.
- The five DTM stages run from high stationary to declining; natural increase peaks in stage 2 (Niger in 2023: 33.0 per 1000) and is negative in stage 5 (Japan).
- The shape of a population pyramid shows the stage: a wide base means high birth rates, and a wide top means an ageing population.
- Malthus argued that population outruns food; Boserup argued that population growth drives agricultural change.
- HDI and GNI per capita measure development; higher development usually goes with lower birth rates and an older population.
- Always quote the figure, its year and its source.
