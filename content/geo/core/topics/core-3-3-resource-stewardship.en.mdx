## Big idea

Resource stewardship is the sustainable management of a resource, so that people today benefit from it without reducing what future generations can use. It works through limits such as quotas, prices, shared rules, international agreements, technology and individual choices. Each method involves trade-offs between economic growth, fairness and conservation.

## Explanation

**What stewardship means.** <Term id="core-resource-stewardship"/> applies to renewable resources such as fish, forests and fresh water: harvests should stay within the <Term id="core-sustainable-yield"/>, the amount that can be taken each year without reducing the stock. For non-renewable resources, the aim is to use less and recycle more.

**Quotas and catch limits.** A <Term id="core-total-allowable-catch"/> sets the maximum catch of a stock, and a <Term id="core-quota"/> divides it among fleets or vessels. Limits protect a stock only if they are set at or below the sustainable yield and enforced. <Case id="core-3-3-case-peru-anchoveta"/> Peru’s catches of 13.1 million tonnes in 1970 and 11.2 million in 1971 exceeded the recommended ceiling of 8 to 9 million tonnes a year; by 1973 the catch was 1.7 million tonnes (FAO, 1997).

**Certification.** <Term id="core-certification"/> lets buyers identify products from well-managed sources. The Marine Stewardship Council (MSC), launched as an independent body in 1997, certifies fisheries; the Forest Stewardship Council (FSC), founded in 1993, certifies forests. Certification relies on market demand rather than law, so its reach and its cost for small producers limit its impact.

**Demand management and water pricing.** <Term id="core-demand-management"/> reduces use through prices, restrictions and information. <Term id="core-water-pricing"/> signals scarcity: higher charges for larger volumes discourage non-essential use, such as garden watering. Cape Town set a target of 500 million litres a day, or 87 litres per person; after residents cut their use, the City moved Day Zero to 13 May 2018 (City of Cape Town, 2017). <Case id="core-3-3-case-cape-town"/>

**Recycling and the circular economy.** <Term id="core-circular-economy"/> keeps materials in use through design, reuse, repair and recycling. A <Term id="core-recycling-rate"/> measures how much waste is recycled, but reducing and reusing come first. Eurostat reports 2023 recycling rates of 68.7% in Germany, 57.9% in the Netherlands and 12.4% in Romania.

**Payments for ecosystem services.** <Term id="core-payments-ecosystem-services"/> pay landowners to maintain the benefits people obtain from nature, known as <Term id="core-ecosystem-service"/>, such as clean water or carbon storage. Costa Rica’s scheme is funded mainly by a 3.6% fuel levy, of which 3.5 percentage points go to the forestry fund FONAFIFO (El Financiero, 31 January 2022). In January 2022 the scheme could not take on new hectares because its budget had fallen. Payments depend on public budgets.

**Governance and international agreements.** Shared resources cross borders, so stewardship depends on agreements. The Convention on Biological Diversity was signed at the 1992 Rio Earth Summit. CITES regulates trade in endangered species. The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, adopted in 1982 and in force from 16 November 1994, gives coastal states sovereign rights in a 200-nautical-mile exclusive economic zone. The Montreal Protocol is a widely cited success: the ozone layer is projected to return to 1980 levels over the Arctic by about 2045 and over Antarctica by about 2066 (UNEP and WMO 2022 assessment, reported in January 2023). Treaties like these are a <Term id="core-multilateral-environmental-agreement"/>.

**Community-based management.** A <Term id="core-common-pool-resource"/> is hard to fence off, so each user may take more than their share, which can lead to the <Term id="core-tragedy-of-the-commons"/> (Garrett Hardin, 1968). Elinor Ostrom, who shared the 2009 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences, documented local groups that managed fisheries, forests and irrigation for long periods. Her book *Governing the Commons* (1990) links such success to clear membership, locally made rules, monitoring and graduated sanctions. These are conditions associated with success, not a guarantee.

**Technology: benefits and risks.** <Term id="core-desalination"/> provides fresh water but needs large amounts of energy and leaves brine to dispose of. Precision irrigation cuts water losses but needs investment and maintenance; renewable energy cuts fossil fuel use but needs land, storage and materials.

**Growth and conservation.** Stewardship has costs: limiting catches cuts jobs and export income. About a third of Peru’s export earnings came from fish products in 1970 (Wikipedia, 1972 Peruvian anchoveta crisis). The right balance depends on what a living stock is worth over time and how fast it can recover. Changes in demand depend on households and firms as well as on government rules, so individual choices matter: buying certified products, cutting food and packaging waste, and saving water.

## Worked example

**Example 1: Calculate (3 marks).** Peru’s anchoveta catch was 13.1 million tonnes in 1970 and 1.7 million tonnes in 1973 (FAO, 1997). Calculate the percentage fall.

<Steps>
<Step>Fall in tonnes: 13.1 − 1.7 = 11.4 million tonnes.</Step>
<Step>Percentage fall: 11.4 ÷ 13.1 × 100 = 87.0%, dividing by the 1970 value.</Step>
<Step>Check: 1.7 ÷ 13.1 × 100 = 13.0% remained, and 100 − 13.0 = 87.0%.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why not divide by 1.7?">Dividing 11.4 by 1.7 gives 670.6%, which is wrong. A percentage change is always measured from the starting value, so divide by 13.1.</Reveal>

**Example 2: Explain (4 marks).** Explain why a quota set above the sustainable yield can still fail to protect a fish stock.

<Steps>
<Step>Point: a quota above the sustainable yield removes more fish than the stock can replace, so the breeding stock shrinks.</Step>
<Step>Development: fewer adults mean fewer young fish survive to join the stock in later years.</Step>
<Step>Link: Peru’s catches of 13.1 and 11.2 million tonnes in 1970 and 1971 were above the 8 to 9 million tonne ceiling, and the 1972–73 El Niño then reduced recruitment.</Step>
<Step>Conclusion: a limit protects a stock only when it is set below the sustainable yield and enforced.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Treating a quota as protection in itself: a total set above the sustainable yield can still lead to collapse.
- Calling certification a legal requirement; MSC and FSC labels are voluntary.
- Presenting recycling as the whole answer; reducing and reusing waste come first.
- Describing common-pool management as always successful; Ostrom’s work identifies conditions associated with success, not a guarantee.
- Mixing up percentage change and percentage-point change in Section B.

## Summary

- Resource stewardship keeps use within the sustainable yield so that present and future generations can benefit.
- Quotas protect a stock only when the total is set at or below the sustainable yield and enforced, as Peru’s anchoveta collapse shows.
- Certification (MSC, FSC) and payments for ecosystem services work through markets and budgets; both can be weakened by voluntary take-up or falling funds.
- Water pricing and demand management cut use; Cape Town aimed to hold use to 87 litres per person a day in 2017–2018.
- Recycling is effective when it sits within the circular economy, after reducing and reusing waste.
- International agreements manage shared resources; the Montreal Protocol shows success, with recovery projected by about 2045 (Arctic) and 2066 (Antarctica).
- Community-based management can work when users share rules and monitoring, but it is not guaranteed.
- Every method involves trade-offs between growth, jobs, fairness and conservation.
