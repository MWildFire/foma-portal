## Big idea

Resource stewardship is the sustainable management of a resource, so that people today benefit from it without reducing what future generations can use. It works through limits such as quotas, prices, shared rules, international agreements, technology and individual choices. Each method involves trade-offs between economic growth, fairness and conservation.

## Explanation

**Population and resources: three views.** Malthus’s pessimistic view is that population can outgrow food supply, bringing shortages and checks on growth. Boserup’s optimistic view is that pressure on resources can encourage more intensive farming and technical change. A balanced view accepts both ecological limits and human capacity to adapt: investment, access, governance and the speed of change determine whether innovation meets demand. None is a guaranteed prediction. In an essay, compare their assumptions against evidence from named places (IB Geography guide, 2017, core 3.3, p. 46).

**What stewardship means.** <Term id="core-resource-stewardship"/> includes management of renewable resources such as fish, forests and fresh water: harvests should stay within the <Term id="core-sustainable-yield"/>, the amount that can be taken each year without reducing the stock. For non-renewable resources, the aim is to use less and recycle more.

**Quotas and catch limits.** A <Term id="core-total-allowable-catch"/> sets the maximum catch of a stock, and a <Term id="core-quota"/> divides it among fleets or vessels. Limits protect a stock only if they are set at or below the sustainable yield and enforced. <Case id="core-3-3-case-peru-anchoveta"/> Southeast Pacific catches of Peruvian anchoveta of 13.1 million tonnes in 1970 and 11.2 million in 1971 exceeded the recommended ceiling of 8 to 9 million tonnes a year; by 1973 the catch was 1.7 million tonnes (FAO, 1997).

**Certification.** <Term id="core-certification"/> lets buyers identify products from well-managed sources. The Marine Stewardship Council (MSC), launched as an independent body in 1997, certifies fisheries; the Forest Stewardship Council (FSC), founded in 1993, certifies forests. Certification relies on market demand rather than law, so its reach and its cost for small producers limit its impact.

**Demand management and water pricing.** <Term id="core-demand-management"/> reduces use through prices, restrictions and information. <Term id="core-water-pricing"/> signals scarcity: higher charges for larger volumes discourage non-essential use, such as garden watering. Cape Town set a target of 500 million litres a day, or 87 litres per person; after residents cut their use, the City moved its conditional Day Zero forecast to 13 May 2018 (City of Cape Town, 2017). <Case id="core-3-3-case-cape-town"/>

**Recycling and the circular economy.** <Term id="core-circular-economy"/> keeps materials in use through design, reuse, repair and recycling. A <Term id="core-recycling-rate"/> measures how much waste is recycled, but reducing and reusing come first. Eurostat reports 2023 recycling rates of 68.7% in Germany, 57.9% in the Netherlands and 12.4% in Romania.

**Payments for ecosystem services.** <Term id="core-payments-ecosystem-services"/> pay landowners to maintain the benefits people obtain from nature, known as <Term id="core-ecosystem-service"/>, such as clean water or carbon storage. Costa Rica’s Ley 8114, Article 5(c), assigns 3.5% of annual receipts from the single fuel tax to FONAFIFO for environmental-service payments (SINALEVI consolidated version of 21 September 2026). Payments depend on public budgets.

**Governance and international agreements.** Shared resources cross borders, so stewardship depends on agreements. The Convention on Biological Diversity was signed at the 1992 Rio Earth Summit. CITES regulates trade in endangered species. The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, adopted in 1982 and in force from 16 November 1994, gives coastal states sovereign rights in a 200-nautical-mile exclusive economic zone. The Montreal Protocol is a widely cited success: the ozone layer is projected to return to 1980 levels over the Arctic by about 2045 and over Antarctica by about 2066 (UNEP and WMO 2022 assessment, reported in January 2023). Treaties like these are a <Term id="core-multilateral-environmental-agreement"/>.

**Community-based management.** A <Term id="core-common-pool-resource"/> is hard to fence off, so each user may take more than their share, which can lead to the <Term id="core-tragedy-of-the-commons"/> (Garrett Hardin, 1968). Elinor Ostrom, who shared the 2009 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences, documented local groups that managed fisheries, forests and irrigation for long periods. Her book *Governing the Commons* (1990) links such success to clear membership, locally made rules, monitoring and graduated sanctions. These are conditions associated with success, not a guarantee.

**Technology: benefits and risks.** <Term id="core-desalination"/> provides fresh water but needs large amounts of energy and leaves brine to dispose of. Precision irrigation can reduce water applied per field, but basin-wide savings also depend on return flows, crop choice and expansion of irrigated area; efficiency alone is not proof that more water remains for other users (FAO, Canal irrigation systems, efficiency discussion). It also needs investment and maintenance. Renewable energy can displace fossil fuels, but needs land and materials, and variable sources can require storage or other balancing measures.

**Growth and conservation.** Stewardship has costs: binding catch limits can reduce short-term landings and income, while protecting the stock can support future livelihoods. Outcomes depend on prices, alternative work and implementation; a limit does not imply a necessary fall in long-term jobs or exports. The right balance depends on what a living stock is worth over time and how fast it can recover. Changes in demand depend on households and firms as well as on government rules, so individual choices matter: buying certified products, cutting food and packaging waste, and saving water.

**UN Sustainable Development Goals: judge progress, not just promises.** Adopted in 2015, the 17 SDGs provide shared goals, targets and indicators for 2030; they coordinate and monitor action rather than automatically enforce national delivery. SDG 6 concerns water and sanitation. Target 6.1 is universal equitable access to safe affordable drinking water; indicator 6.1.1 tracks the population using **safely managed drinking-water services**. In the UN’s 2025 reporting vintage, global coverage rose from 68% in 2015 to 74% in 2024: **6 percentage points**, or about 8.8% relative to the initial share. Nevertheless 2.1 billion people lacked the service in 2024. A world average conceals inequalities, and coverage is not a measure of sustainable abstraction or proof the 2030 target will be met. Stewardship therefore needs both service investment and source protection; compare 6.1.1 with water-stress indicator 6.4.2 rather than treating them as synonyms. [UN 2025 progress, Goal 6, targets 6.1/6.2](https://sdgs.un.org/goals/goal6).

## Worked example

**Example 1: Calculate (3 marks).** The Southeast Pacific catch of the species Peruvian anchoveta was 13.1 million tonnes in 1970 and 1.7 million tonnes in 1973 (FAO, 1997). Calculate the percentage fall.

<Steps>
<Step>Fall in tonnes: 13.1 − 1.7 = 11.4 million tonnes.</Step>
<Step>Percentage fall: 11.4 ÷ 13.1 × 100 = 87.0%, dividing by the 1970 value.</Step>
<Step>Check: 1.7 ÷ 13.1 × 100 = 13.0% remained, and 100 − 13.0 = 87.0%.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why not divide by 1.7?">Dividing 11.4 by 1.7 gives 670.6%, which is wrong. A percentage change is always measured from the starting value, so divide by 13.1.</Reveal>

**Example 2: Explain (4 marks).** Explain why a quota set above the sustainable yield can still fail to protect a fish stock.

<Steps>
<Step>Point: if a quota above sustainable yield is filled, the catch removes more fish than the stock can replace, so the breeding stock shrinks.</Step>
<Step>Development: a smaller breeding stock can weaken later recruitment; the relationship is not fixed, because survival and environmental conditions also matter.</Step>
<Step>Link: Southeast Pacific catches of Peruvian anchoveta of 13.1 and 11.2 million tonnes in 1970 and 1971 were above the 8 to 9 million tonne ceiling, and the 1972–73 El Niño then reduced recruitment.</Step>
<Step>Conclusion: a limit protects a stock only when it is set at or below a suitably updated sustainable yield and enforced; environmental shocks can still affect recruitment.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Treating a quota as protection in itself: a total set above the sustainable yield can still lead to collapse.
- Calling certification a legal requirement; MSC and FSC labels are voluntary.
- Presenting recycling as the whole answer; reducing and reusing waste come first.
- Describing common-pool management as always successful; Ostrom’s work identifies conditions associated with success, not a guarantee.
- Mixing up percentage change and percentage-point change in Section B.

## Summary

- For renewable stocks, stewardship aligns use with replenishment and ecosystem needs. Non-renewable stocks instead require attention to depletion, material efficiency, recycling and substitution.
- Quotas protect a stock only when the total is set at or below the sustainable yield and enforced, as Peru’s anchoveta collapse shows.
- Certification (MSC, FSC) and payments for ecosystem services work through markets and budgets; both can be weakened by voluntary take-up or falling funds.
- Water pricing and demand management can reduce use. Cape Town’s November 2017 target was 87 litres per person per day; by February 2018 it was 50, so do not apply one figure to the whole drought period.
- Recycling is effective when it sits within the circular economy, after reducing and reusing waste.
- International agreements manage shared resources; the Montreal Protocol shows success, with recovery projected by about 2045 (Arctic) and 2066 (Antarctica).
- Community-based management can work when users share rules and monitoring, but it is not guaranteed.
- Every method involves trade-offs between growth, jobs, fairness and conservation.
