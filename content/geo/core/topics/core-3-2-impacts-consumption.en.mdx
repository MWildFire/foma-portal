## Big idea

Using resources changes the water, land and energy systems that supply them, and the effects travel beyond the place of extraction. They can be environmental, socio-economic or geopolitical, and they decide how secure a population’s supply is. The <Term id="core-water-food-energy-nexus"/> links three resources, so a shock to one is felt by the others.

## Explanation

**Environmental impacts.** <Term id="core-resource-depletion"/> happens when a stock is used faster than it is replaced. Groundwater shows this clearly. When pumping exceeds recharge, the result is <Term id="core-groundwater-depletion"/>: water tables fall, wells dry up and, in coastal areas, salt water can move inland. UNESCO reports that groundwater holds 99% of liquid freshwater on Earth and supplies about half of domestic water withdrawals (UNESCO and UN-Water, 2022).

Converting forest to farmland, ranching, agricultural plantations such as oil palm, or mining is <Term id="core-deforestation"/>. Under the adopted FAO FRA 2015 terminology, forestry plantations can remain forest; clear-cutting followed by expected forest regeneration is not deforestation. The FAO’s Global Forest Resources Assessment 2025 reports 4.14 billion hectares of forest, 32% of the land area, and deforestation of 10.9 million hectares a year in 2015–2025, down from 17.6 million in 1990–2000. FAO still calls the current rate too high (FAO, 2025). Clearing also causes <Term id="core-habitat-loss"/>: IPBES (2019, Summary for Policymakers, p. 24, background paragraph 6) reports that about 1 million of an estimated 8 million animal and plant species are threatened with extinction, many within decades. Fertiliser runoff causes <Term id="core-eutrophication"/> in rivers and lakes, and oil leaks contaminate soil and groundwater.

**Socio-economic impacts.** Changes in supply reach households through prices and access. <Term id="core-price-volatility"/> hurts people who spend much of their income on food. The FAO Food Price Index averaged 98.1 in 2020 and 144.5 in 2022 (2014–2016 = 100), and in the revised series its highest monthly value since 1990 was 160.2 in March 2022 (FAO, data released 2 October 2026). Water is also a source of tension between users. Where demand is high relative to supply, an area is under <Term id="core-water-stress"/>. In the Colorado River Basin, agriculture uses 70% of the water, while 35 to 40 million people rely on it for some or all of their municipal needs (US Bureau of Reclamation). <Case id="core-3-2-case-colorado-river"/> When a project takes away land or water, people may be forced to move, which is <Term id="core-development-induced-displacement"/>.

**Geopolitical impacts.** A country that imports energy has <Term id="core-energy-dependence"/>. Russian LNG and pipeline gas supplied 45% of EU gas imports in 2021 and 12% in 2025 (European Commission, REPowerEU, 4 years on). A sudden cut in supply, from war, sanctions or drought, is a <Term id="core-supply-shock"/>. In April 2022, FAO said that Russia and Ukraine together had supplied about 30% of world wheat exports and about 20% of maize exports over the previous three years (FAO, 8 April 2022). Exporters can also apply an <Term id="core-export-restriction"/> to protect domestic supply. India, which exported almost 40% of world rice on average in 2021/22 and 2022/23, banned exports of white non-basmati rice on 20 July 2023 (Texas A&M AFPC, 2023).

**The water–food–energy nexus.** Agriculture took 71.0% of world freshwater withdrawals in 2022, industry 15.7% and domestic use 13.3% (World Bank, based on FAO AQUASTAT). Hydropower gave 15.2% of world electricity in 2025 (World Bank, WDI indicator EG.ELC.HYRO.ZS, October 2026 release). Three links matter in exam answers. Irrigation needs energy for pumping. Hydropower depends on river flow. Energy and fertiliser costs feed into food prices, and maize used for biofuel adds to demand (FAO, 2 October 2026). A drought can therefore cut crop yields, raise food prices and reduce hydropower at the same time.

**Local impacts of extraction.** The people nearest a mine, an oil field or a diversion often carry the costs. In Ogoniland, Nigeria, UNEP found benzene in well water at levels over 900 times the WHO guideline in one community (UNEP, 2012). <Term id="core-water-diversion"/> moves costs downstream: the Aral Sea’s river inflow fell from 42 km³ in 1960 to 4.5 km³ in 2010 (Springer, 2012). <Case id="core-3-2-case-ogoniland"/> <Case id="core-3-2-case-aral-sea"/>

**Resource security and insecurity.** Resource security is reliable access to enough food, water and energy at an affordable price. Its opposite, <Term id="core-resource-insecurity"/>, is the risk of shortage, price shock or disruption. Security is about access, not only the amount in the ground.

**Contrasting nexus cases: India and South Africa.** This comparison uses IPCC AR6 WGIII 2022 §17.3.3.2, WGII 2022 chapter 4 irrigation evidence, and the dated IEA India 2021/South Africa 2019 outlooks. Their scenarios are not current measurements or forecasts.

| Security link | India | South Africa |
|---|---|---|
| Water and climate | IPCC describes extensive groundwater irrigation and over-extraction. Hotter conditions can increase evaporative demand, and rainfall shortfalls can increase irrigation needs while aquifer decline limits the buffer; this is not uniform across India. | The 2015–2017 drought/Cape Town crisis illustrates exposure of existing supplies to climate variability. IPCC WGII2022 §9.6.2 projects reduced water availability in southern Africa, with regional uncertainty. Groundwater potential does not mean every community has wells, treatment or distribution. |
| Food and access | Pumped irrigation can protect harvests from rainfall shortfalls but links farm costs and food supply to electricity and sustainable abstraction. Poor farmers may lack credit or reliable power. | Access to irrigation affects farmers’ ability to adapt. A drought can constrain crops and livelihoods; infrastructure, allocation and affordability mediate access, not rainfall alone. |
| Energy and trade-offs | The IEA2021 outlook describes a coal-dependent grid supported by hydro/gas flexibility. Pumping needs electricity; water-cooled thermal plants and hydropower face water-related constraints. | The IEA2019 outlook describes coal dependence and a relatively developed grid, alongside unequal access. Cooling competes for water; wind/PV diversification can reduce operating-water demand, while jobs, grids and finance constrain transition. |

The contrast is between a major groundwater-irrigation system under depletion pressure and a drought-exposed, unevenly serviced system; neither country is uniformly secure. Drought is not automatically proof of anthropogenic attribution. Resource security requires reliable affordable access, not simply a national stock. [IPCC nexus section](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-17/), [IPCC water chapter](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg2/chapter/chapter-4/), [IEA India 2021](https://www.iea.org/reports/india-energy-outlook-2021), [IEA South Africa 2019](https://www.iea.org/articles/south-africa-energy-outlook).

**Consumer waste crosses borders.** EEA’s 2023 briefing, using 2019 trade data, reports that 46% by weight of EU used-textile exports went to Africa. This is a route for discarded consumer goods; it is **not** evidence that46% was dumped or even legally classified as waste. Items may be reused, processed into fibres/rags (**recycling/downcycling**), re-exported or disposed of through landfill/burning. Reuse keeps a garment in use; recycling processes its material; disposal does neither. Sorting quality, markets and local treatment capacity determine outcomes. The EEA explicitly reports uncertainty about final destinations and reuse rates. [EEA, commodity-code explanation and receiving-country/fate sections](https://www.eea.europa.eu/en/analysis/publications/eu-exports-of-used-textiles).

## Worked example

**Example 1: how much deforestation has slowed.** FAO’s Global Forest Resources Assessment 2025 reports 17.6 million hectares a year lost in 1990–2000 and 10.9 million in 2015–2025. Calculate the percentage fall.

<Steps>
<Step>Fall in the annual rate: $17.6 - 10.9 = 6.7$ million hectares a year.</Step>
<Step>Percentage fall: $\frac{6.7}{17.6} \times 100 = 38.1\%$.</Step>
<Step>The rate fell by 38.1%, but FAO still describes 10.9 million hectares a year as too high. Give the units and the years in the answer.</Step>
</Steps>

**Example 2: explaining a link in the nexus.** Explain how a drought can affect both food and electricity.

<Steps>
<Step>Start with the cause: a drought reduces river flow and soil moisture.</Step>
<Step>Agriculture takes 71.0% of world freshwater withdrawals, so less water for irrigation lowers crop yields.</Step>
<Step>Lower harvests raise food prices, and the energy needed to pump, transport and fertilise food adds to costs.</Step>
<Step>Hydropower falls where river flow drops, so electricity supply is reduced as well.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Withdrawal or consumption?"><Term id="core-water-withdrawal"/> is the water taken from a source. Consumption is the part that is not returned, for example because it evaporates. Read the question to see which one it asks for.</Reveal>

## Common mistakes

- Treating groundwater as unlimited because it is hidden; depletion happens whenever abstraction is higher than recharge.
- Mixing withdrawal with consumption: a power plant can withdraw a large volume for cooling and return most of it.
- Calling a high price volatile; volatility is about the size and frequency of changes.
- Listing water, food and energy side by side without naming the link between them.
- Quoting the 2020 to 2022 change of 46.4 index points as 46.4%; the percentage increase is 47.3%.

## Summary

- Resource depletion happens when a stock is used faster than it is replaced; groundwater depletion lowers water tables and can cause land subsidence.
- Deforestation slowed to 10.9 million hectares a year in 2015–2025 from 17.6 million in 1990–2000 (FAO, Global Forest Resources Assessment 2025), but the rate is still too high.
- Socio-economic impacts include price volatility (the FAO index averaged 144.5 in 2022, against 98.1 in 2020), water stress and displacement.
- Geopolitical impacts include energy dependence (Russian gas: 45% of EU gas imports in 2021, 12% in 2025), supply shocks and export restrictions.
- In the water–food–energy nexus, agriculture took 71.0% of world freshwater withdrawals in 2022, and hydropower gave 15.2% of world electricity in 2025.
- Extraction and diversion costs fall on communities: benzene over 900 times the WHO guideline in Ogoniland, and the Aral Sea’s river inflow down from 42 km³ in 1960 to 4.5 km³ in 2010 (Springer, 2012).
- Resource security is reliable access at an affordable price; resource insecurity is the risk of shortage, price shock or disruption.
