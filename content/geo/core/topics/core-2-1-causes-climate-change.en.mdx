## Big idea

Earth stays warm because the <Term id="core-greenhouse-effect"/> traps part of the heat the planet radiates to space. Since the industrial era, human emissions have strengthened that trap, which is the <Term id="core-enhanced-greenhouse-effect"/>, and the climate system has warmed. Natural drivers also change climate, but they act on different timescales, so they cannot explain the recent warming on their own.

## Explanation

Earth receives short-wave energy from the Sun and radiates long-wave (infrared) energy back to space. Greenhouse gases absorb part of that outgoing radiation and send some of it back down. This natural <Term id="core-greenhouse-effect"/> keeps the surface warm enough for life. The main <Term id="core-greenhouse-gas"/>es are carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide and water vapour. A change in a driver upsets the energy balance; its size is measured as <Term id="core-radiative-forcing"/> in watts per square metre (W m⁻²).

**Natural causes.** The <Term id="core-milankovitch-cycles"/> change how sunlight is spread over latitudes and seasons: eccentricity cycles over about 90,000 to 100,000 years, and the axial tilt over about 40,000 years (NASA Earth Observatory). <Term id="core-solar-output"/> varies by about 0.1% over the 11-year solar cycle (NASA). <Term id="core-volcanic-aerosols"/> cool the surface for a few years: after Mount Pinatubo erupted in 1991, global temperatures fell by about 0.5 °C from 1991 to 1993 (USGS).

These drivers cannot explain recent warming. Orbital changes act far too slowly for a rise of 1.09 °C between 1850–1900 and 2011–2020 (IPCC AR6). AR6 estimates that natural drivers changed global surface temperature by only −0.1 °C to +0.1 °C between 1850–1900 and 2010–2019, while well-mixed greenhouse gases contributed a warming of 1.0 °C to 2.0 °C (AR6, A.1.3). Volcanic cooling is also temporary.

**Human causes.** The <Term id="core-enhanced-greenhouse-effect"/> comes from greenhouse gases that people add. IPCC AR6 states that the rise in well-mixed greenhouse gas concentrations since about 1750 is unequivocally caused by human activities. Carbon dioxide comes mainly from burning fossil fuels, cement production and deforestation; methane from agriculture, waste and fossil-fuel production; nitrous oxide from fertilisers and manure. In 2019, AR6 gives averages of 1,866 ppb for methane and 332 ppb for nitrous oxide. Human-caused radiative forcing was 2.72 W m⁻² in 2019 relative to 1750 (AR6, A.4.1). Water vapour is also a greenhouse gas, but people do not add it directly: it increases as the air warms, so it acts as a feedback.

**The carbon cycle.** The <Term id="core-carbon-cycle"/> moves carbon between <Term id="core-carbon-store"/>s, such as the atmosphere, oceans, land, rocks and fossil fuels. Each transfer is a <Term id="core-carbon-flux"/>. In 2024, people emitted 11.6 GtC and the atmosphere gained 7.9 GtC; the rest was taken up by the ocean and land (Global Carbon Budget 2025). A <Term id="core-carbon-sink"/> absorbs more carbon than it releases, but it can weaken. <Case id="core-2-1-case-china"/>

**Feedbacks.** A <Term id="core-positive-feedback"/> amplifies a change: in the Arctic, melting ice lowers the <Term id="core-albedo"/>, so darker surfaces absorb more sunlight and melt more ice. Thawing permafrost can release methane, and warmer air holds more water vapour, which adds to warming. A <Term id="core-negative-feedback"/> reduces a change: a warmer Earth radiates more energy to space, and faster plant growth takes up more CO₂. Feedbacks change the size of the response, not the original driver. <Case id="core-2-1-case-arctic-sea-ice"/>

**Evidence.** <Term id="core-ice-core"/>s hold bubbles of past air. In the NOAA Antarctic composite, CO₂ between 1,000 and 800,000 years ago ranged from 173.71 ppm (about 667,000 years ago) to 298.60 ppm (about 335,000 years ago), and it was 277.60 ppm around 1750. The Mauna Loa record shows the modern rise: 316.91 ppm in 1960 and 427.35 ppm in 2025 (NOAA GML). Isotopes give an independent check: fossil-fuel carbon is depleted in carbon-13, and the falling 13C/12C ratio of atmospheric CO₂ fits a fossil origin (Keeling et al. 2017). <Case id="core-2-1-case-mauna-loa"/>

## Worked example

**Example 1: how much human CO₂ stays in the air (2024).** The Global Carbon Project gives total anthropogenic emissions of 11.6 GtC and an atmospheric gain of 7.9 GtC.

<Steps>
<Step>Share retained = atmospheric gain ÷ total emissions × 100.</Step>
<Step>7.9 ÷ 11.6 × 100 = 68.1%.</Step>
<Step>About two-thirds of human carbon stayed in the air in 2024; the ocean and land took up the rest, within the uncertainty of the budget.</Step>
</Steps>

**Example 2: explaining a positive feedback.** Explain why melting Arctic sea ice can cause further warming.

<Steps>
<Step>Start with the change: warming melts some sea ice.</Step>
<Step>Less ice means a lower albedo, because dark open water reflects less sunlight than ice does.</Step>
<Step>More sunlight is absorbed, so the surface warms and more ice melts.</Step>
<Step>The loop feeds back into itself, so it is a positive feedback that amplifies the original warming.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Section A questions carry 2 to 6 marks each. Section B (10 marks) is based on an infographic and has parts (a) to (c). The command terms reward different things:

- **Outline** and **State** need one correct, specific point per mark, such as a named natural cause with a brief description.
- **Explain** needs a cause, the process that links it to the effect, and the consequence. A definition alone earns no Explain marks.
- **Calculate** rewards each step: the working, the answer and its unit, to the precision the question asks for.
- **Using the infographic** and **Describe** reward the trend with values, years and units; a description without figures stays in the lower bands.
- **Evaluate** needs a strength and a limitation of the evidence, linked to the question, and a judgement.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the natural greenhouse effect as the problem; the issue is the enhanced greenhouse effect from human emissions.
- Using Milankovitch cycles to explain warming since 1850; they act over tens of thousands of years.
- Mixing CO₂ concentration (ppm) with emissions (GtC or GtCO₂ per year): a rise of 110.44 ppm is not an emission figure.
- Naming a feedback without its chain: say what changes, what that does, and whether the original change is amplified or reduced.
- Treating Mauna Loa as the global average: it is one station, and the global average for 2024 is 422.8 ppm (Global Carbon Project), against 424.61 ppm at Mauna Loa.

## Summary

- The natural greenhouse effect keeps the surface warm; the enhanced effect comes from human emissions of CO₂, methane and nitrous oxide.
- Orbital cycles, solar output and volcanic aerosols are natural drivers, but they cannot explain warming of 1.09 °C between 1850–1900 and 2011–2020 (AR6).
- Human-caused radiative forcing was 2.72 W m⁻² in 2019 relative to 1750 (AR6).
- The carbon cycle moves carbon between stores along flows; in 2024 the atmosphere gained 7.9 GtC of the 11.6 GtC emitted by people.
- A positive feedback, such as ice–albedo, amplifies change; a negative feedback, such as the extra radiation from a warmer Earth, reduces it.
- Evidence: ice cores (277.60 ppm around 1750), Mauna Loa (316.91 ppm in 1960; 427.35 ppm in 2025) and the falling 13C/12C ratio of atmospheric CO₂.
