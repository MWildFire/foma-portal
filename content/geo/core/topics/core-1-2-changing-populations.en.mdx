## Big idea

Changing populations and places is about where people live and why they move. Since 1950 the share of the world’s people living in cities has more than doubled, while many rural areas have lost people and international migration has grown. Every move changes both the place people leave and the place they reach, so each needs its own planning.

## Explanation

**Urbanisation and urban growth.** <Term id="core-urbanisation"/> is the rise in the share of a country’s population living in urban areas. <Term id="core-urban-growth"/> is the rise in the number of people living in urban areas. The two can move apart: an urban share can rise while the total population falls.

<Callout kind="define">Degree of Urbanization (UN DESA): cities have at least 50,000 people and 1,500 per km²; towns and semi-dense areas have at least 5,000 people and 300 per km².</Callout>

**Patterns by level of development.** In 2025 cities held 45% of the world’s 8.2 billion people, up from 20% in 1950; towns held 36% and rural areas 19%. Development shapes the pace:

- High-income countries urbanised early. Their biggest gains came between 1950 and 1975; growth has slowed since.
- Low-income countries started from a small base: cities held 8% of their people in 1950 and 29% in 2000. The UN projects 40% by 2050, so most of the change is still ahead.
- Middle-income countries lie between these patterns, with growth driven by factory and service jobs and by rural–urban migration.

**Megacities.** A <Term id="core-megacity"/> has 10 million or more inhabitants. There were 8 megacities in 1975 and 33 in 2025, and 19 of the 33 are in Asia (UN DESA, 2025). They offer large labour markets, but also strain housing, transport, water and sanitation, and rapid growth often produces an <Term id="core-informal-settlement"/> on the city edge. <Case id="core-1-2-case-lagos"/>

**Rural–urban migration.** <Term id="core-rural-urban-migration"/> is the main internal flow in many low- and middle-income countries. Each <Term id="core-push-factor"/> (such as low farm incomes, drought or conflict) makes people want to leave the countryside. Each <Term id="core-pull-factor"/> (such as factory jobs, higher wages or schools) draws them to towns. An <Term id="core-intervening-obstacle"/>, such as distance, the cost of travel or family ties, slows or stops the move. Because moving costs money, migration is selective: those with savings, education or city contacts move first.

<Case id="core-1-2-case-shenzhen"/> Shenzhen shows the scale: its residents rose from 10.4 million in 2010 to 17.6 million in 2020, and 71% of them were floating population, living away from their registered place. China’s hukou (household registration) system ties some public services to the place of registration, so migrants can face obstacles.

**Counter-urbanisation and rural depopulation.** <Term id="core-counter-urbanisation"/> is the movement from large cities to smaller towns and rural areas, most common in high-income countries with good transport and remote working. <Term id="core-rural-depopulation"/> is the loss of rural population through out-migration, sometimes with natural decrease. Both can happen in one country. In Europe, Northern America, Australia and New Zealand the rural share fell from 36% in 1950 to 26% in 2025.

**International migration.** <Term id="core-international-migration"/> is movement across national borders to live abroad. <Term id="core-voluntary-migration"/> is chosen, usually for work, study or family. <Term id="core-forced-migration"/> happens when conflict, persecution, disasters or environmental change leave no real choice. About 304 million people lived outside their country of birth in 2024 (UN DESA). In the UAE, migrants made up 74% of the population. <Case id="core-1-2-case-dubai"/>

**Impacts on the place of origin.**

- <Term id="core-remittances"/> can raise household incomes and pay for schooling and housing.
- <Term id="core-brain-drain"/> reduces the supply of doctors, engineers, teachers and other skilled workers.
- Labour loss means less farm and business output and faster ageing; remittances offset some of this but do not replace workers.

**Impacts on the place of destination.**

- Labour supply: migrants fill jobs in construction, care, services and farming, supporting growth.
- Pressure on housing and services: fast in-migration can overload housing, transport, schools and health care.
- Integration: outcomes depend on language, work rights and access to citizenship. Poor integration brings segregation and tension.

## Worked example

**Example 1: Calculate (2 marks).** Lagos had 7,909,000 people in 2000 and 12,792,000 in 2025 (UN DESA, World Urbanization Prospects 2025, Annex Table A4). Find the percentage increase.

<Steps>
<Step>Increase in people: 12,792,000 − 7,909,000 = 4,883,000.</Step>
<Step>Percentage increase: 4,883,000 ÷ 7,909,000 × 100 = 61.7%.</Step>
<Step>Check: the UN gives an average annual rate of change of 1.92% for 2000–2025. Over 25 years that gives roughly 61–62%, so the answer is consistent.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why not use the rounded figures?">Using 12.8 and 7.9 million gives 62.0%. Use the exact values when a question gives them, and always state the units and the number of decimal places.</Reveal>

**Example 2: Explain (4 marks).** Explain how push factors, pull factors and an intervening obstacle affect a rural young person’s decision to move to a city.

<Steps>
<Step>Push factor, linked: low farm income in a drought year makes staying hard.</Step>
<Step>Pull factor, linked: factory and service jobs in the city offer higher wages.</Step>
<Step>Obstacle, linked: the cost of the journey and of a first home may stop the poorest families from moving.</Step>
<Step>Conclusion: the decision depends on the balance of push, pull and obstacles, so migration is selective and the young and better-educated often move first.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Treating urbanisation (a share of people) and urban growth (a number of people) as the same thing.
- Listing push and pull factors without linking them to a decision, or forgetting the obstacles that explain why not everyone moves.
- Mixing national "urban" figures with Degree of Urbanization figures; they use different definitions and give different totals.
- Writing only about the place of origin or only about the destination when the question asks about both.
- Confusing counter-urbanisation (people leaving cities) with rural depopulation (people leaving rural areas).

## Summary

- Urbanisation is a rising share of people in towns and cities; urban growth is a rising number of people.
- Cities held 45% of the world’s people in 2025, up from 20% in 1950; megacities rose from 8 in 1975 to 33 in 2025.
- Rural–urban migration depends on push factors, pull factors and intervening obstacles, so it is selective.
- Counter-urbanisation and rural depopulation are different movements, and both can happen in the same country.
- International migration brings remittances and labour, but also brain drain, labour loss and pressure on housing and services.
