## Big idea

Changing populations and places is about where people live and why they move. Since 1950 the share of the world’s people living in cities has more than doubled, while many rural areas have lost people and international migration has grown. Every move changes both the place people leave and the place they reach, so each needs its own planning.

## Explanation

The demographic transition belongs to core 1.2 in the IB Geography guide (2017, p. 43). Revisit the [five-stage explanation and simulation](#/s/geo/t/core-1-1-population-development) and connect falling birth and death rates to natural increase.


**Urbanisation and urban growth.** <Term id="core-urbanisation"/> is the rise in the share of a country’s population living in urban areas. <Term id="core-urban-growth"/> is the rise in the number of people living in urban areas. The two can move apart: an urban share can rise while the total population falls.

<Callout kind="define">Degree of Urbanization (UN DESA): cities have at least 50,000 people and 1,500 per km²; towns and semi-dense areas are clusters outside cities with at least 5,000 people and 300 per km². This harmonised classification uses population size, density and contiguity on a 1-km² grid; it is not a rule based simply on municipal boundaries.</Callout>

**Patterns by level of development.** In 2025 cities held 45% of the world’s 8.2 billion people, up from 20% in 1950; towns held 36% and rural areas about 20% (19.58%, UN WUP 2025, F02). Development shapes the pace:

- High-income countries urbanised early. Among the successive 25-year intervals shown here, their Cities share rose most in 1950–1975: 29.1% to 37.6%; it reached 46.3% in 2025. These are share changes, not growth rates of city populations.
- Low-income countries started from a small base: cities held 8% of their people in 1950 and 29% in 2000. The 2025 share is 34.7%, with about 40% projected for 2050. This is continued growth, not evidence that most of the 1950–2050 increase is still ahead.
- In 2025 the Cities shares were 42.9% in lower-middle-income and 48.7% in upper-middle-income countries, compared with 46.3% in high-income countries. Income groups are therefore not a simple urbanisation ladder. Jobs and migration can contribute to growth, but these shares alone do not identify causes; natural increase and reclassification also matter.

**Megacities.** A <Term id="core-megacity"/> has 10 million or more inhabitants. There were 8 megacities in 1975 and 33 in 2025, and 19 of the 33 are in Asia (UN DESA, 2025). They offer large labour markets. Where housing and services fail to keep pace, growth can create pressure on transport, water and sanitation, and <Term id="core-informal-settlement"/> areas may expand. Such settlements are not confined to city edges. <Case id="core-1-2-case-lagos"/>

**Rural–urban migration.** <Term id="core-rural-urban-migration"/> is the main internal flow in many low- and middle-income countries. Each <Term id="core-push-factor"/> (such as low farm incomes, drought or conflict) makes people want to leave the countryside. Each <Term id="core-pull-factor"/> (such as factory jobs, higher wages or schools) draws them to towns. An <Term id="core-intervening-obstacle"/>, such as distance, the cost of travel or family ties, slows or stops the move. Moving costs and access to information can make migration selective: savings, education or city contacts can help. This does not establish that these groups always move first; forced movements can follow different constraints.

<Case id="core-1-2-case-shenzhen"/> Shenzhen shows a large population living away from registration, not a direct measure of rural origins or job motives: its residents rose from 10.4 million in 2010 to 17.6 million in 2020, and 71% of them were floating population, living away from their registered place. China’s hukou (household registration) system ties some public services to the place of registration, so migrants can face obstacles.

**Counter-urbanisation and rural depopulation.** <Term id="core-counter-urbanisation"/> is movement from large urban centres towards smaller settlements and rural areas. Transport and the ability to work remotely can make such moves feasible; they are not defining requirements. <Term id="core-rural-depopulation"/> is a fall in rural population through net out-migration, natural decrease or both. Reclassification can also change the recorded rural total. Both can happen in one country. In Europe, Northern America, Australia and New Zealand the rural share fell from 36% in 1950 to 26% in 2025. A falling share alone does not prove a fall in the number of rural residents or identify the migration flow.

**International migration.** <Term id="core-international-migration"/> is movement across national borders to live abroad. <Term id="core-voluntary-migration"/> is chosen, usually for work, study or family. <Term id="core-forced-migration"/> happens when conflict, persecution, disasters or environmental change leave no real choice. UN DESA estimated about 304 million international migrants in 2024, using country of birth where available and citizenship as a proxy otherwise. In the UAE, migrants made up 74% of the population. <Case id="core-1-2-case-dubai"/>

**Impacts on the place of origin.**

- <Term id="core-remittances"/> can raise household incomes and pay for schooling and housing.
- <Term id="core-brain-drain"/> reduces the supply of doctors, engineers, teachers and other skilled workers.
- Out-migration can reduce farm or business labour and increase the older share if younger people leave. The effect on output depends on unemployment, replacement workers and investment; remittances can partly offset income losses.

**Impacts on the place of destination.**

- Labour supply: migrants can fill jobs in construction, care, services and farming; the contribution to output depends on employment and use of their skills.
- Pressure on housing and services: fast in-migration can overload housing, transport, schools and health care.
- Integration: outcomes depend on language, work rights and access to citizenship. Barriers to integration can contribute to segregation and tension; these are not inevitable outcomes of migration.

**Forced movement.** Some movements are forced rather than chosen. In Somalia, PSMN’s monitored January–December 2025 displacement movements recorded drought for 52% and conflict/insecurity for 35% (UNHCR Annual Report 2025). These are reported drivers of monitored movements, not shares of all unique displaced people or a forecast of future causes. <Case id="core-1-2-case-somalia"/>

## Worked example

**Example 1: Calculate (2 marks).** Lagos had 7,909,000 people in 2000 and 12,792,000 in 2025 (UN DESA, World Urbanization Prospects 2025, Annex Table A4). Find the percentage increase.

<Steps>
<Step>Increase in people: 12,792,000 − 7,909,000 = 4,883,000.</Step>
<Step>Percentage increase: 4,883,000 ÷ 7,909,000 × 100 = 61.7%.</Step>
<Step>Check: the UN gives an average annual rate of change of 1.92% for 2000–2025. Over 25 years that gives roughly 61–62%, so the answer is consistent.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why not use the rounded figures?">Using 12.8 and 7.9 million gives 62.0%. Use the exact values when a question gives them, and always state the units and the number of decimal places.</Reveal>

**Example 2: Explain (4 marks).** Explain how push factors, pull factors and an intervening obstacle affect a rural young person’s decision to move to a city.

<Steps>
<Step>Push factor, linked: low farm income in a drought year makes staying hard.</Step>
<Step>Pull factor, linked: factory and service jobs in the city offer higher wages.</Step>
<Step>Obstacle, linked: the cost of the journey and of a first home may stop the poorest families from moving.</Step>
<Step>Conclusion: the decision depends on push, pull and obstacles, so migration can be selective. This hypothetical example does not establish which age or education group moves first in a real population.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Treating urbanisation (a share of people) and urban growth (a number of people) as the same thing.
- Listing push and pull factors without linking them to a decision, or forgetting the obstacles that explain why not everyone moves.
- Mixing national "urban" figures with Degree of Urbanization figures; they use different definitions and give different totals.
- Writing only about the place of origin or only about the destination when the question asks about both.
- Confusing counter-urbanisation (people leaving cities) with rural depopulation (a fall in the rural population, also possible through natural decrease or reclassification).

## Summary

- Urbanisation is a rising share of people in towns and cities; urban growth is a rising number of people.
- Cities held 45% of the world’s people in 2025, up from 20% in 1950; megacities rose from 8 in 1975 to 33 in 2025.
- Rural–urban migration depends on push factors, pull factors and intervening obstacles, so it is selective.
- Counter-urbanisation is a population movement; rural depopulation is a decrease in rural numbers. They can coexist in different places within one country.
- International migration brings remittances and labour, but also brain drain, labour loss and pressure on housing and services.
