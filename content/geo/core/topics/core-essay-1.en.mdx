## Big idea

A 10-mark Section C essay is one argument, not a list of facts. You answer the question in a clear thesis, support it with three developed points that each use a named place with figures and years, weigh the other side, and finish with a direct answer. Marks follow the judgement and the evidence, not the amount of description.

## Explanation

A Section C essay is worth 10 marks. It tests four things: accurate knowledge, application of that knowledge to named places, analysis of causes and effects, and a judgement that answers the question. Clear communication (a logical structure and precise terms) supports all four.

**The structure.** Use the same shape every time:

- Introduction (3 to 4 sentences): define the key term briefly, then state your thesis. The thesis is a direct answer to the question, with a view on how far it is true.
- Three developed points: each makes a claim, explains the mechanism (cause and effect), supports it with a named place, a figure with its year and a source, and links back to the question.
- Evaluation: weigh the other side, state the conditions under which your view holds, and say which factor matters most.
- Conclusion (1 to 2 sentences): answer the question directly. Do not add new evidence.

<Callout kind="define">A thesis is a one-sentence answer that takes a position, for example: "Push factors explain why people leave, but pull factors decide where they go." It is not a topic statement such as "This essay is about migration."</Callout>

**What the command term changes.** The command term tells you what kind of answer is needed:

- Discuss: give a balanced review of the arguments for and against, supported by evidence, and reach a reasoned conclusion.
- Evaluate: weigh the strengths and limitations of a view or a factor, and judge which is more important or more reliable.
- To what extent: decide how far something is true or how much it matters. A partial answer is valid if you argue why it is partial.
- Examine: explain how and why something happens, showing the links and interactions between causes rather than just listing them.

**Evidence.** A named place with a figure, a year and a source is worth more than any general claim such as "in many countries". Use approximate figures with "about" or "around", and check the year before you write it. Two places used in depth are better than six names mentioned in passing. If you cannot recall a verified figure, use a qualitative example and say so, rather than inventing a number.

**Balance.** Essays that argue one side only lose the top bands. Where the question asks for a judgement, argue both sides, compare the evidence and state which is stronger. A conclusion that says "both are important" without a reason earns little.

**Time and planning.** In these practice essays you have about 20 minutes. Spend about three minutes on the plan: the thesis, the three points with their places and figures, and the judgement. The plan decides the structure and most of the marks, so do it before the first sentence.

**Markbands.** A 10-mark essay is placed in one of five bands: 1–2, 3–4, 5–6, 7–8 or 9–10 marks. Each band describes knowledge, application, analysis and evaluation. The markschemes in this topic paraphrase those descriptors in general words; the exact wording is in the Geography guide.

## Worked example

Two students plan the same question with different theses. Both plans are valid, because the examiner rewards a clear judgement supported by named evidence. The difference is which factor each student judges to matter more.

**Question:** Evaluate the relative importance of push and pull factors in rural–urban migration, using named examples of the places people move to. (10 marks, 20 minutes)

**Plan A: push factors matter more for the decision to leave**

<Steps>
<Step>Thesis: in low-income economies, push factors such as land shortage and low farm incomes explain most of the decision to leave, while pull factors mainly decide which city people choose.</Step>
<Step>Point 1 (push): name a rural origin and one push factor you can evidence. Use the UN projection that Nigeria will add 189 million urban dwellers between 2018 and 2050 (UN DESA, 2018 Revision) as the scale of the shift, not as proof of the cause.</Step>
<Step>Point 2 (push): add a second push mechanism, such as climate hazards or mechanised farming that reduces farm jobs, with a named place. If you have no verified figure, say that the evidence is qualitative.</Step>
<Step>Point 3 (counter-evidence): pull factors still matter. Shenzhen, a special economic zone, had 17.56 million residents at the 2020 census, many of them migrants drawn by jobs.</Step>
<Step>Judgement: push factors explain the decision to leave, but they say little about the destination; pull factors weigh more where urban jobs grow fast. Natural increase also drives city growth, so migration is only part of the answer.</Step>
<Step>Conclusion: push factors matter more for leaving and pull factors for choosing the city; the balance depends on the economy.</Step>
</Steps>

**Plan B: pull factors matter more overall**

<Steps>
<Step>Thesis: pull factors such as job growth, wages and services explain most urban migration, because people move when a city offers work, even when rural conditions are hard.</Step>
<Step>Point 1 (pull): Shenzhen, a special economic zone, grew to 17.56 million residents by the 2020 census, with migrants drawn by factory and service jobs.</Step>
<Step>Point 2 (pull): the scale of urban growth. The UN reports that 55% of the world's population lived in urban areas in 2018 and projects 68% by 2050, so cities will keep attracting people.</Step>
<Step>Point 3 (push, counter-evidence): push factors still matter, because a plan that ignores land shortage or climate stress is one-sided. Name one push factor with a place.</Step>
<Step>Judgement: pull factors decide the destination and much of the flow when cities grow fast, but push factors dominate where rural incomes collapse; the weight depends on the economy.</Step>
<Step>Conclusion: pull factors matter more for the overall flow in fast-growing economies, while push factors dominate in the poorest rural areas.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Which plan scores higher?">Neither plan is wrong. Both can reach the top bands if the judgement weighs both sides and every point has a named place and a figure with its year. Plan B is stronger on the destination example; Plan A is stronger on the origin. The better essay makes that comparison explicit in the judgement, not only in the conclusion.</Reveal>

## How it is examined

Each Section C essay is one 10-mark question. The answer is placed in one of five bands (1–2, 3–4, 5–6, 7–8 or 9–10 marks). The bands look at four assessment objectives, described here in general words: knowledge and understanding; application and analysis; synthesis and evaluation; communication. The exact descriptors are in the Geography guide, and the guide's wording is the official one.

What each band needs, in short:

- Bands 1–2 (1–2 marks): a few relevant ideas with little accuracy, no named place, description only, no judgement.
- Bands 3–4 (3–4 marks): accurate basic knowledge, one named place without a figure, one explained cause or effect, and a basic view with little support.
- Bands 5–6 (5–6 marks): accurate knowledge and terms, two named places (one with a figure and year), developed cause-and-effect chains, and a judgement with one argument on each side.
- Bands 7–8 (7–8 marks): precise knowledge, several named places with figures, years and sources, explicit links between factors, and a clear, sustained judgement with its conditions.
- Bands 9–10 (9–10 marks): wide and precise knowledge, contrasting evidence tested against the question, sophisticated analysis of interactions and limits, and a balanced, well-supported answer that says which factor matters most and why.

The four command terms in this topic (Discuss, Evaluate, To what extent, Examine) change the task, as explained above. In each case the marks go to:

- accurate knowledge, with precise terms;
- named places with figures, years and sources, applied to the question;
- a developed chain of cause and effect (analysis);
- a clearly argued judgement that weighs the other side (evaluation).

<Callout kind="exam">Students lose most marks in Section C by describing well and judging weakly. Put the judgement in the thesis, test it in the evaluation, and repeat the answer in the conclusion.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- Describing the model or the factors without answering the question. A list of push and pull factors, or the stages of a model, earns few marks unless it is linked to a judgement.
- Using a generic case ("in many countries") with no name, figure or year. A named place with a verified figure and year is worth far more.
- Writing a one-sided essay that ignores the other side. For Discuss, Evaluate and To what extent, the counter-argument is part of the answer.
- Mixing up terms: the total fertility rate (children per woman) is not the crude birth rate (births per 1,000 people). Use the right one and say which you mean.
- Ending with a conclusion that repeats the introduction or adds a new case study. The conclusion gives the answer and nothing more.

## Summary

- A 10-mark essay is one argument: a thesis, three developed points, a judgement and a direct conclusion.
- Each developed point needs a named place, a figure with its year and a link back to the question.
- Discuss asks for both sides and a view; Evaluate asks for a weighed judgement; To what extent asks how far, with a clear position; Examine asks how and why, with the links explained.
- The top bands need sustained evaluation: the other side, the conditions for your view and a clear answer.
- Spend about three minutes planning; the plan decides the structure and most of the marks.
- Avoid the common errors: description without judgement, generic cases and one-sided essays.
