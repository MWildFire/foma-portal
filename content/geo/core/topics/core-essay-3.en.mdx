## Big idea

A 10-mark Section C essay on resource consumption is one argument, not a list of resources. You answer the question in a clear thesis, support it with three developed points that each use a named place with a figure, a year and a source, weigh the other side, and finish with a direct answer. Marks follow the judgement and the evidence, not the amount of description.

## Explanation

A Section C essay is worth 10 marks. It tests four things: accurate knowledge, application of that knowledge to named places, analysis of causes and effects, and a judgement that answers the question. Clear communication, with a logical structure and precise terms, supports all four.

Unit 3 asks four kinds of question, and the command term shows which one:

- Security: is rising consumption a threat to food, water and energy supplies? (To what extent)
- Impacts: who bears the costs of resource use, the country that buys the goods or the country that produces them? (Discuss)
- Management: do stewardship methods such as quotas, certification, recycling and governance work in a named case? (Evaluate)
- Links: how do water, food and energy trade off against each other, and against conservation? (Examine)

The structure is the same every time:

- Introduction (3 to 4 sentences): define the key term briefly, then state a thesis that answers the question and says how far it is true.
- Three developed points: each makes a claim, explains the mechanism, supports it with a named place, a figure with its year and a source, and links back to the question.
- Evaluation: weigh the other side, state the conditions under which your view holds, and say which factor matters most.
- Conclusion (1 to 2 sentences): answer the question directly. Do not add new evidence.

<Callout kind="define">An ecological footprint is the land and sea area, in global hectares (gha), that a country's production or consumption needs. Biocapacity is the productive area available. When the footprint is larger than the biocapacity, the country or the world is in ecological overshoot.</Callout>

Two accounts matter in Unit 3. A production footprint counts the land and sea used to make goods inside a country. A consumption footprint counts the land and sea used by its people and firms, including imported goods. The two can differ, so always say which one you mean.

Some terms need care in this unit:

- Resource security is reliable access to enough of a resource, at a price people can pay, without serious disruption.
- A reserve is the part of a known deposit that can be extracted at current prices and with current technology. It is not the total amount in the ground.
- Stewardship is managing a resource so that it is used efficiently and kept for future use.
- A quota limits how much may be taken, sold or exported. Certification is an independent check that a product meets set standards. Governance is the set of rules and institutions that decide who uses a shared resource and how.

Evidence separates a weak essay from a strong one. A named place with a figure, a year and a source is worth far more than a general claim such as "in many countries". Use each figure as its source gives it, with "about" for rounded numbers. If you calculate something, show the inputs: for example, the world footprint of production (2.56 global hectares per person) divided by biocapacity (1.50) gives 1.7 (National Footprint and Biocapacity Accounts, 2026 edition). Do not set a stock (reserves) against an annual flow (output) as if they were the same kind of number.

Balance is essential. Discuss, Evaluate and To what extent all reward the other side: the case for, the case against and the conditions under which your view holds. A conclusion that says "both matter" earns little without a reason.

Time and planning: the practice essay has 20 minutes. Spend about three minutes on the plan before the first sentence.

## Worked example

Two students plan the same question with different theses. Both can reach the top bands, because the examiner rewards a clear judgement supported by named evidence. The difference is which mechanism each student judges to matter more.

**Question:** To what extent does rising consumption threaten global resource security in food, water and energy? (10 marks, 20 minutes)

**Plan A: supply concentration and policy matter more than total demand**

<Steps>
<Step>Thesis: rising consumption threatens resource security to a large extent for minerals, because supply is concentrated and policy can cut it off at short notice; for food and water the threat is moderate.</Step>
<Step>Point 1 (minerals): the Democratic Republic of the Congo produced about 73% of world cobalt mine output in 2025 (USGS, 2026). Its February 2025 export ban was replaced by quotas in October 2025 (USGS, 2026).</Step>
<Step>Point 2 (energy transition): Indonesia's nickel mine output rose by about 13% to an estimated 2,600,000 tonnes in 2025, about two-thirds of the world total of 3,900,000 tonnes (USGS, 2026; the share is an own calculation).</Step>
<Step>Point 3 (counter-evidence, food): the share of people facing hunger fell from 8.5% in 2023 to 8.2% in 2024 (FAO, SOFI 2025), so the aggregate food threat is smaller than the risk from concentrated supply; access and price remain the problem.</Step>
<Step>Judgement: the threat is large where supply is concentrated and smaller in aggregate for food; the answer turns on governance more than on volume.</Step>
<Step>Conclusion: rising consumption threatens resource security to a large extent for minerals and to a moderate extent for food and water.</Step>
</Steps>

**Plan B: total demand matters more than concentration**

<Steps>
<Step>Thesis: rising consumption threatens resource security to a large extent because demand already exceeds what the planet can renew, so pressure falls on every resource; concentrated supply is a symptom.</Step>
<Step>Point 1 (overshoot): the world footprint of production was 2.56 global hectares per person in 2023, against a biocapacity of 1.50, and carbon made up about 60% of the footprint (National Footprint and Biocapacity Accounts, 2026 edition).</Step>
<Step>Point 2 (water): Colorado River storage was about 36% of capacity in April 2026, and agriculture uses 70% of the river's water (US Bureau of Reclamation).</Step>
<Step>Point 3 (counter-evidence, minerals): cobalt output is concentrated, but the USGS reports that cobalt-free substitutes held a significant market share in China, so demand can change as well as supply (USGS, 2026).</Step>
<Step>Judgement: total demand creates the pressure and concentration decides where it breaks; both matter, but demand sets the long-run limit.</Step>
<Step>Conclusion: rising consumption threatens resource security to a large extent, because demand already exceeds renewal; the risk is especially high where demand meets concentrated supply.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Which plan scores higher?">Neither plan is wrong. Both can reach the top bands if the judgement weighs both sides and every point has a named place and a figure with its year and source. Plan A is stronger on the mechanism of risk (concentration and policy); Plan B is stronger on the scale of demand. The better essay makes that comparison explicit in the judgement, not only in the conclusion.</Reveal>

## How it is examined

Each Section C essay is one 10-mark question. The answer is placed in one of five bands (1–2, 3–4, 5–6, 7–8 or 9–10 marks). The bands look at four assessment objectives, described here in general words: knowledge and understanding; application and analysis; synthesis and evaluation; communication. The exact descriptors are in the Geography guide, and the guide's wording is the official one.

What each band needs, in short:

- Bands 1–2 (1–2 marks): a few relevant ideas with little accuracy, no named place, description only, no judgement.
- Bands 3–4 (3–4 marks): accurate basic knowledge, one named place without a figure, one explained link between consumption and a resource, and a basic view with little support.
- Bands 5–6 (5–6 marks): accurate terms, two named places (one with a figure and year), developed cause-and-effect chains, and a judgement with one argument on each side.
- Bands 7–8 (7–8 marks): precise knowledge, several named places with figures, years and sources, explicit links between demand, supply and policy, and a clear judgement with its conditions.
- Bands 9–10 (9–10 marks): wide and precise knowledge, contrasting evidence tested against the question, sophisticated analysis of interactions and limits, and a balanced answer that says which factor matters most and why.

For the four command terms in this topic, the marks go to: accurate knowledge with precise terms; named places with figures, years and sources, applied to the question; a developed chain of cause and effect; and a judgement that weighs the other side. For Evaluate, a method is judged by its result in a named case, not only described. For Examine, the links between resources must be shown, not listed.

<Callout kind="exam">Students lose many Section C marks by describing well and judging weakly. Put the judgement in the thesis, test it in the evaluation, and repeat the answer in the conclusion.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- Describing resources and figures without answering the question. A list of minerals, percentages or products earns few marks unless it is linked to a judgement.
- Mixing up the two footprint accounts. Say whether a figure is a production footprint or a consumption footprint, and do not compare them as if they were the same.
- Treating reserves as the total supply, or as the year the resource runs out. A reserve is a known deposit that can be extracted economically; the ratio of reserves to annual output is only a rough guide.
- Giving a figure without its year or source, or with the wrong year. Footprint figures are for 2023, cobalt output for 2025 and Amazon clearing for the year from August 2024 to July 2025.
- Writing about stewardship in general terms, without testing a method against a named case and its result.

## Summary

- A Unit 3 essay is one argument: a thesis, three developed points, a judgement and a direct conclusion.
- Name the resource (food, water, energy or a mineral) and the account (production or consumption) for every figure.
- Use the Unit 3 cases and figures with their years and sources, for example cobalt and nickel, the Colorado River, the Aral Sea and the footprint accounts.
- Discuss asks for both sides and a view; Evaluate asks for a weighed judgement on a method; To what extent asks how far; Examine asks how and why, with the links explained.
- Stewardship methods such as quotas, certification, recycling and governance are judged by their results in a named case, not by their description.
- Keep the judgement in the thesis, test it in the evaluation and give the answer in the conclusion, without new evidence.
