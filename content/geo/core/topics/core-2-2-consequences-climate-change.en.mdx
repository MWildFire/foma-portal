## Big idea

Climate change does not only raise temperatures. It changes the sea, ice, weather and ecosystems, and these physical changes reach people through food, water, health, money and where they can live. Impacts are uneven: people and places that contributed little to the problem are often more exposed to it and have less capacity to adapt. Global surface temperature in 2011–2020 was 1.09 °C above 1850–1900 (IPCC, 2021, section A.1.2).

## Explanation

**Physical consequences**

- <Term id="core-sea-level-rise"/> comes from warming seawater expanding and land ice melting. Global mean sea level rose by 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018, and the rate rose from 1.3 mm per year (1901–1971) to 3.7 mm per year (2006–2018) (IPCC, 2021, section A.1.7).
- <Term id="core-ice-loss"/> comes mainly from glaciers and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets. Human influence is very likely the main driver of glacier retreat since the 1990s, and ice-sheet loss rose fourfold between 1992–1999 and 2010–2019 (IPCC, 2021, sections A.1.5, A.4.3).
- Hot extremes, including <Term id="core-heatwave"/>s, have become more frequent and more intense across most land regions since the 1950s (IPCC, 2021, section A.3.1).
- Heavy precipitation has increased over most land areas with enough data, and intense rain can cause a <Term id="core-flash-flood"/> within hours (IPCC, 2021, section A.3.2). Human-induced climate change has contributed to more <Term id="core-drought"/>s in some regions (medium confidence) (IPCC, 2021, section A.3.2). In Australia, the 2019–2020 bushfires burned over 24 million hectares (<Case id="core-2-2-case-australia"/>).
- The ocean absorbs much of the extra heat and carbon dioxide; <Term id="core-ocean-acidification"/> is the fall in pH that follows carbon dioxide uptake (IPCC, 2021, section A.2.4).
- Warming of 1.5 °C is projected to shift many marine species towards higher latitudes, a form of <Term id="core-biome-shift"/>. Coral reefs are projected to decline by a further 70–90% at 1.5 °C and by more than 99% at 2 °C (IPCC, 2018, SR1.5, section B.4.2).
- Abrupt responses and <Term id="core-tipping-point"/>s, such as strongly increased Antarctic ice-sheet melt and forest dieback, cannot be ruled out (high confidence) (IPCC, 2021, section C.3.2).

<Callout kind="define">Tipping point (IPCC glossary): a critical threshold beyond which a system reorganises, often abruptly and/or irreversibly.</Callout>

**Human consequences**

- <Term id="core-food-security"/> and <Term id="core-water-security"/> are threatened by drought, heat and salt water. WHO reports that in 2020, 98 million more people experienced food insecurity than in the 1981–2010 average (WHO, 2023). Rising seas drive <Term id="core-salinity-intrusion"/> into rivers and aquifers, cutting fresh water in low-lying deltas.
- Health: WHO estimates about 250 000 extra deaths a year in 2030–2050 from undernutrition, malaria, diarrhoea and heat stress alone (WHO, 2023). Heat-related deaths among people over 65 have risen by 70% in two decades (WHO, 2023).
- Infrastructure and economies: WMO recorded 11,778 reported disasters from 1970 to 2021, with just over 2 million deaths and USD 4.3 trillion of economic losses (WMO, 2023). Over 90% of reported deaths occurred in developing countries.
- <Term id="core-displacement"/> and migration: the World Bank says climate change could force 216 million people in six world regions to move within their countries by 2050 (World Bank, 2021).

**Vulnerability, resilience and adaptive capacity**

<Term id="core-vulnerability"/> depends on exposure, sensitivity and the ability to cope. <Term id="core-resilience"/> is the ability to absorb a shock and recover; <Term id="core-adaptive-capacity"/> is the potential to adjust. Tuvalu shows all three. Its average elevation is 1.83 m (GCF, 2015), and its coastal adaptation project relies on a USD 36.0 million Green Climate Fund grant (GCF, 2015). Migration is another route: Australia's Falepili Mobility Pathway offers up to 280 visa places in the 2026–27 round (Australian High Commission in Tuvalu, 2026). <Case id="core-2-2-case-tuvalu"/>

**Why impacts are uneven**

**Between countries.** In vulnerable regions the death rate from extreme weather in the last decade was 15 times higher than in less vulnerable regions (WHO, 2023, citing IPCC AR6). Low-income countries and small island developing states face more severe health impacts despite contributing minimally to global emissions (WHO, 2023).

**Within one country.** WHO names women, children, ethnic minorities, poor communities, migrants, older people and people with health conditions as disproportionately affected. In Bangladesh, a severe flood could cut GDP by as much as 9%, and 13 million people could become internal climate migrants by 2050 (World Bank, 2022). <Case id="core-2-2-case-bangladesh"/>

## Worked example

**Example 1: Calculate (2 marks).** The IPCC gives a global mean sea-level rise of 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018. Calculate the average rate in millimetres per year.

<Steps>
<Step>Convert the rise to millimetres: 0.20 m = 200 mm.</Step>
<Step>Count the years: 2018 − 1901 = 117 years.</Step>
<Step>Average rate: 200 ÷ 117 = 1.7 mm per year. This is lower than the 3.7 mm per year of 2006–2018, which shows that the rate has increased.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why does one average hide the trend?">A single average describes the whole period. The rate was slower in 1901–1971 (1.3 mm per year) and faster in 2006–2018 (3.7 mm per year), so the average alone understates the recent rise.</Reveal>

**Example 2: Explain (4 marks).** Explain how a drought can reduce water security for a farming community.

<Steps>
<Step>Physical link: less rain lowers river flow and recharges groundwater less, so less water is available for irrigation and drinking.</Step>
<Step>Human link: crops and livestock fail, so farm income falls and families spend their savings on water and food.</Step>
<Step>Vulnerability: households with little money, no storage and no other supply are less able to cope.</Step>
<Step>Conclusion: the drought reduces water and food security together, and poorer households are hit harder.</Step>
</Steps>

## Common mistakes

- Saying that melting sea ice causes most sea-level rise. Sea ice already floats, so the rise comes from land ice and the expansion of warm water.
- Describing ocean acidification as the sea turning acid. Seawater stays alkaline, but its pH falls.
- Describing a physical change without its human consequence, or a human consequence without its physical cause.
- Treating vulnerability and exposure as the same. A place can be exposed to a flood but have low vulnerability if it is well protected.
- Quoting a figure without its year and source, or presenting a projection as if it were an observation.

## Summary

- Global mean sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018; the rate rose from 1.3 mm per year (1901–1971) to 3.7 mm per year (2006–2018) (IPCC, 2021).
- Heatwaves have become more frequent and more intense across most land regions, and heavy precipitation has increased over most land areas; human-induced climate change has contributed to droughts in some regions (IPCC, 2021).
- Ocean acidification threatens corals and shellfish; coral reefs are projected to decline by a further 70–90% at 1.5 °C (IPCC, 2018).
- Tipping points are thresholds beyond which change is abrupt or irreversible, and the IPCC says they cannot be ruled out (IPCC, 2021).
- Food, water and health are affected: WHO projects about 250 000 extra deaths a year in 2030–2050 from some causes (WHO, 2023).
- Vulnerability, resilience and adaptive capacity explain uneven impacts: Tuvalu (average elevation 1.83 m) and Bangladesh (13 million potential climate migrants by 2050) show how (GCF, 2015; World Bank, 2022).
