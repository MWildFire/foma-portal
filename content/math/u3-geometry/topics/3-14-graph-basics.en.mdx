## Big idea

A graph records which objects are joined: vertices are the objects, edges are the joins. Degrees, simple and complete graphs, directed graphs, subgraphs and trees form the basic vocabulary that lets you describe any network exactly.

## Explanation

A graph $G$ is a set of vertices joined by edges. Two vertices are adjacent when an edge joins them, and that edge is incident to both of them. A simple graph has no loops (an edge from a vertex to itself) and at most one edge between any two vertices.

<Term id="graph-vertex-degree"/> of a vertex is the number of edges that meet it, written $\deg(v)$.

Each edge has two ends, so it adds 1 to the degree of each of two vertices. Hence the sum of all the degrees is twice the number of edges. This is the handshaking result:

$$
\sum_{v}\deg(v) = 2\,|E|
$$

<Formula id="graph-handshaking"/>

<Term id="graph-complete"/> is a simple graph in which every pair of vertices is joined by an edge. On $n$ vertices it has $n(n - 1)/2$ edges, see <Formula id="graph-complete-edges"/>.

<Term id="graph-in-out-degree"/> are defined for directed graphs: the in-degree of a vertex counts the arcs ending at it, and the out-degree counts the arcs starting at it. The total of the in-degrees and the total of the out-degrees both equal the number of arcs, see <Formula id="graph-directed-degrees"/>.

A weighted graph attaches a number, such as a distance or a cost, to each edge. A subgraph keeps some of the vertices and edges of a larger graph, and every kept edge keeps both of its ends. <Term id="graph-tree"/> is a connected graph with no cycles. A tree on $n$ vertices has $n - 1$ edges, see <Formula id="graph-tree-edges"/>.

<Callout kind="define">Simple graph: a graph with no loops and no repeated edges. Degree: the number of edges meeting a vertex, written $\deg(v)$.</Callout>

## Worked example

The simple graph $G$ has vertices A, B, C, D and E, and six edges: AB, AC, BC, BD, CD and DE.

<Steps>
<Step>Degrees: $\deg(A) = 2$, $\deg(B) = 3$, $\deg(C) = 3$, $\deg(D) = 3$ and $\deg(E) = 1$. The sum is $2 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 12 = 2 \times 6$, so $G$ has 6 edges.</Step>
<Step>Tree test: a tree on 5 vertices has $5 - 1 = 4$ edges. $G$ has 6 edges and contains the cycle A–B–C–A, so $G$ is not a tree.</Step>
<Step>Subgraph: delete vertex E and the edge DE. The subgraph on A, B, C and D keeps the edges AB, AC, BC, BD and CD, so it has 5 edges.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why must the subgraph drop the edge DE?">A subgraph can keep an edge only when both of its end vertices are kept. Deleting E removes DE, because E is one of its ends.</Reveal>

## Visual

<Callout kind="tip">Draw each vertex as a dot and each edge as a line. Before you use the handshaking result, count the lines at each dot. For a directed graph, put an arrow on each arc and count the arrows pointing into and out of each dot separately.</Callout>

## How it is examined

Find and Write down questions give marks for the method and for the answer: for a degree or edge count, the sum of the degrees comes first, then the number of edges. Show that questions need a reason stated in words, such as a comparison with the number of edges of a tree or a named cycle. In a directed graph, check that the in-degree total and the out-degree total agree.

<Callout kind="exam">If the degrees you are given do not add to an even number, no simple graph can have them. Say so before you try to draw the graph.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Counting each edge once when adding the degrees. Each edge adds 1 at each of its two ends, so the total is twice the number of edges.</Callout>

- Calling a graph a tree because it is connected. A tree must also have no cycles, and then it has exactly $n - 1$ edges.
- Keeping an edge in a subgraph after one of its end vertices has been removed.
- Using $n$ as the number of edges of a complete graph. It is $n(n - 1)/2$, one edge for each pair of vertices.

## Summary

- A graph has vertices and edges; two vertices are adjacent when an edge joins them.
- The degree of a vertex is the number of edges meeting it, and the degrees add to twice the number of edges.
- A simple graph has no loops and no repeated edges; a complete graph on $n$ vertices has $n(n - 1)/2$ edges.
- A weighted graph has a number on each edge; a directed graph has arcs, with in-degrees and out-degrees.
- A subgraph keeps some vertices and edges, and each kept edge keeps both of its ends.
- A tree is a connected graph with no cycles; on $n$ vertices it has $n - 1$ edges.
- An odd sum of degrees means a mistake, because the handshaking sum is always even.
