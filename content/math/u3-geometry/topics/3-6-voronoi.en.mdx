## Big idea

A Voronoi diagram assigns points to their nearest sites using a stated distance rule. Here distances are straight-line Euclidean distances and sites are distinct. Boundary points have ties, so closed cells share their boundaries.

A <Term id="voronoi-vertex"/> is equally far from three or more nearest sites; no other site is closer. For three noncollinear sites it is their circumcentre. A circumcentre of an arbitrary triple in a larger set need not be a Voronoi vertex: another site may lie closer. Three collinear sites have no finite circumcentre.

When a distinct site is added, its cell takes precisely the points where it becomes nearest or tied for nearest. Old cells can shrink, edges can be shortened or removed, and cells it does not cut remain unchanged. “Nearby” alone is not a numerical test: compare distances.

<Term id="nearest-neighbour-interpolation"/> copies the reading at a nearest site. At a tie, report the tied sites and specify a selection rule if one estimate is required. This does not automatically mean averaging the readings. Predictions can jump between cells with different readings; adjacent cells with equal readings need not have a jump.

## Worked example

Stations at $(0,0)$, $(5,0)$ and $(2,6)$ record $10$, $14$ and $8$ °C. Estimate the temperature at $Q(3,2)$.

<Steps>
<Step>The squared distances are $13$, $8$ and $17$ respectively; their square roots are approximately $3.61$, $2.83$ and $4.12$.</Step>
<Step>The nearest station is $(5,0)$, so the nearest-neighbour estimate is $14$ °C.</Step>
<Step>The bisector of $(0,0)$ and $(5,0)$ is $x=2.5$, and Q is on the second station's side. That pairwise check alone would not rule out the third station; the three-distance comparison does.</Step>
</Steps>

### Choosing a site far from the existing sites

Maximise the distance to the nearest site, not to a convenient single site. State the allowed location region first. If the question permits only listed vertices, compare those candidates as in q-3-6-03. That conclusion is restricted to the permitted candidates.

For a closed bounded convex polygon, also consider the polygon's corners and the intersections of its boundary with Voronoi edges, as well as Voronoi vertices inside it. In each clipped cell the nearest-site distance is distance to that cell's site; a maximum occurs at a vertex of the clipped polygon. The boundary therefore cannot be discarded. If the entire unbounded plane is allowed and the set of sites is finite, moving sufficiently far away makes the nearest-site distance arbitrarily large: there is no finite global best point.

<Reveal title="Counterexample: must the best location be an internal Voronoi vertex?">No. Put sites at A(2,2), B(8,2), C(5,8), and allow the square $0\le x,y\le10$. Their vertex is $(5,4.25)$, at distance $3.75$ from each site. At the allowed corner $(0,10)$, the nearest site is C, at distance $\sqrt{29}\approx5.39$, which is greater. This already disproves the internal-vertex claim; finding the global optimum would require comparing all allowed candidates.</Reveal>

## How it is examined

Show distances or squared distances, compare them and identify the nearest site before copying its reading. For a candidate vertex check equality and ensure no other site is closer. For a location problem state the allowed candidates or boundary and maximise the nearest-site distance over them. Exact marks depend on the question.

<Callout kind="exam">Equal distances to two sites only establish their bisector. Other sites can prevent that point from belonging to their Voronoi edge.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- Using the full perpendicular bisector as an edge without checking the other sites.
- Assuming any midpoint is a vertex, or assuming a midpoint can never be a vertex. With A(-1,0), B(1,0), C(0,1), their midpoint $(0,0)$ is equidistant from all three and is a vertex.
- Choosing the largest distance to one site instead of the largest nearest-site distance.
- Ignoring an allowed boundary when locating a facility.
- Treating a boundary tie as a unique nearest station without a rule.

## Summary

- A closed cell contains points at least as close to its site as to every other site; boundaries have ties.
- Edges are appropriate portions of bisectors; vertices have at least three nearest sites and an empty interior circumcircle.
- Nearest-neighbour interpolation copies a reading; tie handling must be specified.
- A farthest-location problem needs a stated allowed region or candidate set. Boundary candidates can matter.
