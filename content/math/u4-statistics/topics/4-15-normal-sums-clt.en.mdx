## Big idea

A combination of independent normal variables is again normal, so a total or a difference of normal measurements can be modelled directly. The mean of a sample from a normal population is normal with variance $\sigma^{2}/n$. For a population of any shape, the central limit theorem says the sample mean is approximately normal once the sample is large.

## Explanation

Throughout, a random sample means independent identically distributed observations with finite mean and finite positive variance. The CLT is a large-sample approximation; n ≥ 30 is a guide, not a guarantee of accuracy for every population.

## Worked example

An apple $A$ and an orange $O$ are put in one bag. Their masses are independent, with $A \sim N(150, 144)$ and $O \sim N(180, 225)$ in grams, where the second number in each bracket is the variance. The total mass is $T = A + O$.

<Steps>
<Step>$T \sim N(330, 369)$: the mean is $150 + 180 = 330$ g and the variance is $144 + 225 = 369$ g², so the standard deviation is $\sqrt{369} = 19.21$ g.</Step>
<Step>$P(T > 340) = 0.301$ (to 3 decimal places), using the GDC normal cdf with mean 330 and standard deviation 19.21.</Step>
</Steps>

The waiting time at a help desk has mean 4.2 minutes and standard deviation 3.0 minutes, but its distribution is not normal. For a random sample of 40 customers, assume independent observations and use a CLT approximation. The sample size $n=40$ alone cannot establish its accuracy; severe skewness could require more observations.

<Steps>
<Step>The standard error is $\sigma/\sqrt{n} = 3.0/\sqrt{40} = 0.474$ minutes (to 3 decimal places). The variance of $\bar{X}$ is $3.0^{2}/40 = 0.225$, so $\bar{X} \approx N(4.2, 0.225)$.</Step>
<Step>$P(\bar{X} > 5) \approx 0.046$ (to 3 decimal places), using the GDC normal cdf with mean 4.2 and standard deviation 0.474.</Step>
</Steps>

## Visual

<Callout kind="tip">Sketch two curves on the same axes. The first is the population curve, which may be skewed. The second is the curve of $\bar{X}$: the same centre $\mu$, but narrower, with spread $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. As $n$ grows, the curve for $\bar{X}$ becomes narrower and more bell-shaped.</Callout>

## How it is examined

State the distribution with its mean and variance, distinguish the sum of separate observations from a multiple of one observation, and use the standard error for a sample mean. Say whether normality is exact or approximate and justify the sampling assumptions. A sample-size rule alone does not guarantee the CLT approximation.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Using $\sigma^{2}$ instead of $\sigma^{2}/n$ for the variance of $\bar{X}$, or $\sigma$ instead of $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ for its standard deviation.</Callout>

- Adding standard deviations: $\sigma_{X+Y} \neq \sigma_X + \sigma_Y$. Add the variances, then take the square root.
- Stating that $\bar{X}$ is normal without a normal population or a large sample.
- Combining normal variables without checking that they are independent.

## Summary

- A linear combination of independent normal variables is normal, with mean $a\mu_1 + b\mu_2$ and variance $a^{2}\sigma_1^{2} + b^{2}\sigma_2^{2}$.
- If $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^{2})$, then $\bar{X} \sim N(\mu, \sigma^{2}/n)$ exactly.
- The standard error of the sample mean is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.
- By the central limit theorem, for large $n$, $\bar{X}$ is approximately $N(\mu, \sigma^{2}/n)$ whatever the population shape.
- Add variances, not standard deviations, and square the coefficients.
- Name the distribution with its parameters; write "approximately" when you use the central limit theorem.
