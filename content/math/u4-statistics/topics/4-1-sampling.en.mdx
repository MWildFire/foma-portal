## Big idea

Statistics uses a sample to learn about a whole population. A well-designed probability sample supports estimates of population quantities. Random sampling still has sampling variation and does not automatically remove nonresponse or measurement errors. Increasing sample size does not by itself repair a biased selection method.

## Explanation

The <Term id="population-and-sample"/> is the whole group you want to know about, for example all 900 students of a school. A sample is the part of it that you actually measure. Data are the values you record: qualitative data record categories; quantitative data record numbers. A discrete variable takes separate possible values (such as a sibling count); a continuous variable can vary throughout an interval (such as height). Rounding a height to centimetres does not turn the underlying height variable into a count.

For a simple random sample of fixed size n, every subset of n distinct members is equally likely. Every member then has inclusion probability n/N. Random choice does not ensure that a particular sample is typical. Other probability designs can use unequal inclusion probabilities and require appropriate weighting; an unweighted mean or proportion is not automatically unbiased for every such design. <Term id="random-sample-technique"/>

A sample chosen by convenience can carry <Term id="selection-bias"/>, a systematic difference from the population. Interviewing the first students through the school gate, or taking responses to an online poll from one group only, can exclude groups or over-represent people with particular characteristics. This creates a risk of selection bias, whose direction needs contextual evidence. One observed discrepancy does not establish systematic bias; simply taking more responses does not repair the exclusion mechanism.

Sample statistics estimate population values. The sample mean estimates the population mean:

$$
\bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i
$$

<Formula id="sample-mean-estimate"/>

The sample proportion estimates the population proportion, so for a simple random sample with accurate responses it can be scaled up to estimate a population count. Unequal-probability designs need appropriate weights. <Formula id="sample-proportion-estimate"/>

An outlier is a value that lies far from the rest of the data. Check it before you use it: it may be a recording error, or a real but unusual member of the population. Do not delete it silently.

## Worked example

A random sample of 50 students from a school of 900 students is asked whether they have a part-time job. 15 of the 50 say yes.

<Steps>
<Step>Sample proportion: $\hat{p} = \dfrac{15}{50} = 0.300$.</Step>
<Step>Estimate for the school: $0.300 \times 900 = 270$ students.</Step>
<Step>The first 50 students to arrive at the gate give $\hat{p} = \dfrac{22}{50} = 0.440$. This is $0.440 - 0.300 = 0.140$ higher than the estimate from the random sample, a difference of 14 percentage points between these two estimates. This alone does not prove bias: the random-sample estimate is not the known population truth, and sampling variation can also produce different proportions.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why does a bigger gate sample not fix this?">A larger sample can reduce sampling variation under an appropriate design, but does not automatically remove systematic exclusion. If early arrival is related to the property studied, selecting only early arrivals can bias the estimate. Its direction cannot be inferred from these two samples alone; check the sampling frame, inclusion and response patterns.</Reveal>

<Reveal title="Do equal individual probabilities prove simple random sampling?">No. For four people labelled 1–4, choose either the pair $\{1,2\}$ or the pair $\{3,4\}$, each with probability $1/2$. Every person has inclusion probability $1/2$, but four of the six possible pairs can never be chosen. A simple random sample of size 2 would give each of the six pairs probability $1/6$.</Reveal>

## Visual

<Callout kind="tip">Sketch the population as a rectangle of dots and mark your sample on it. A simple random sample may look uneven or clustered by chance. A visual cluster does not establish bias. To assess a method, identify who can be selected and whether inclusion or response is related to the quantity being estimated.</Callout>

## How it is examined

For Estimate, show the sample proportion or mean and scale it to the population only when the sampling design justifies that inference. For Explain, identify the selection mechanism and a plausible risk of bias; do not infer systematic bias from one difference between samples. Distinguish an estimate from an exact population total.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Calling any large or quickly collected sample random.</Callout>

- Believing that a bigger biased sample gives a more reliable estimate.
- Scaling a sample proportion by the sample size instead of the population size.
- Deleting an outlier without checking whether it is a recording error or a real value.

## Summary

- The population is the whole group of interest; the sample is the part you measure.
- In a simple random sample of fixed size, every subset of that size is equally likely.
- Bias is a systematic error from the sampling method, and a larger sample does not remove it.
- Discrete variables take separate values; continuous variables vary over intervals; inspect outliers before using or excluding them.
- The sample mean and the sample proportion estimate the population mean and proportion.
- Scale an estimated proportion by the population size to estimate a count.
