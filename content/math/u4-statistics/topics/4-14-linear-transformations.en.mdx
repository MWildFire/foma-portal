## Big idea

A linear transformation $Y = aX + b$ shifts the mean by the same rule that applies to the data, but it changes the variance only by $a^{2}$, so adding a constant never changes the spread. Means always add up; variances add for independent variables; more generally, zero covariance suffices. These rules explain why the sample mean is an unbiased estimate of $\mu$ and why the sample variance divides by $n - 1$.

## Explanation

Assume the stated means and variances are finite. Independence is a sufficient condition for adding variances; uncorrelated variables also suffice. Otherwise covariance matters, so independence is not a necessary condition. The unbiased sample-variance result below assumes independent identically distributed observations and n > 1.

Let $X$ be a random variable with mean $E(X)$ and variance $\operatorname{Var}(X)$. The <Term id="linear-transformation-rv"/> $Y = aX + b$ multiplies every value by $a$ and then adds $b$. Its mean and variance follow directly:

<Formula id="linear-transform-mean-variance"/>

Adding $b$ moves the whole distribution by $b$ and leaves its spread unchanged. Multiplying by $a$ scales every deviation from the mean by $a$, so the standard deviation is multiplied by $|a|$.

The <Term id="expected-value-linear-combination"/> is the same linear combination of the means. It needs no independence:

<Formula id="linear-combination-mean"/>

The <Term id="variance-linear-combination-independent"/> uses independence here, and every coefficient is squared, so the sign disappears:

<Formula id="linear-combination-variance"/>

An estimator turns sample data into an estimate of a population parameter. Take $X_1, \dots, X_n$ independent, each with mean $\mu$. By the rules above,

$$
E(\bar{X}) = \frac{1}{n}\bigl(E(X_1) + \dots + E(X_n)\bigr) = \frac{1}{n} \times n\mu = \mu,
$$

so the sample mean $\bar{X}$ is an <Term id="unbiased-estimator"/> of $\mu$.

Dividing the sum of squared deviations by $n$ gives a variance estimate that is too small on average. Dividing by $n - 1$ gives the sample variance $s_{n-1}^{2}$, whose expected value is $\sigma^{2}$:

<Formula id="sample-variance-n-minus-1"/>

<Callout kind="define">Linear transformation: a new random variable $Y = aX + b$, where $a$ and $b$ are constants. Its mean is $aE(X) + b$ and its variance is $a^{2}\operatorname{Var}(X)$.</Callout>

<Callout kind="gdc">On a Calculator page, One-Variable Statistics (`Menu → Statistics → Stat Calculations → One-Variable Statistics`) shows two standard deviations: `sx` with divisor $n-1$ (the sample value) and `σx` with divisor $n$. When you estimate $\sigma$ from data, use `sx`, and check the label before you square it.</Callout>

<Reveal title="Why does the divisor become n − 1?">The deviations are measured from $\bar{x}$, which is calculated from the same data, so they are smaller on average than deviations from $\mu$. Using up one degree of freedom for $\bar{x}$ corrects this.</Reveal>

## Worked example

A coffee kiosk sells $X$ cups a day, with $E(X) = 120$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 225$. Each cup gives a profit of AED 4.50, and the kiosk has a fixed daily cost of AED 150. The daily profit is $P = 4.5X - 150$.

<Steps>
<Step>$E(P) = 4.5\,E(X) - 150 = 4.5 \times 120 - 150 = 390$ AED, the mean daily profit.</Step>
<Step>$\operatorname{Var}(P) = 4.5^{2}\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4.5^{2} \times 225 = 4556.25$. The fixed cost of 150 does not change the variance.</Step>
<Step>$\sigma_P = \sqrt{4556.25} = 67.5$ AED, the standard deviation of the daily profit.</Step>
</Steps>

A parcel holds a box $B$ and its contents $C$. The two masses are independent, with $E(B) = 0.40$ kg, $\operatorname{Var}(B) = 0.0025$ kg², $E(C) = 2.35$ kg and $\operatorname{Var}(C) = 0.0400$ kg². The total mass is $W = B + C$.

<Steps>
<Step>$E(W) = 0.40 + 2.35 = 2.75$ kg.</Step>
<Step>$\operatorname{Var}(W) = 0.0025 + 0.0400 = 0.0425$ kg², so $\sigma_W = \sqrt{0.0425} = 0.206$ kg (to 3 decimal places).</Step>
</Steps>

A bottle is filled four times, with volumes 499, 503, 500 and 498 ml. Treat them as a random sample of size $n = 4$ from a population with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^{2}$.

<Steps>
<Step>$\bar{x} = \dfrac{499 + 503 + 500 + 498}{4} = 500$ ml, the estimate of $\mu$.</Step>
<Step>$\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^{2} = 1 + 9 + 0 + 4 = 14$, so $s_{n-1}^{2} = \dfrac{14}{3} = 4.67$ ml² (to 2 decimal places), the estimate of $\sigma^{2}$.</Step>
</Steps>

## Visual

<Callout kind="tip">Picture $X$ as a distribution along a line. The map $Y = aX + b$ slides the whole picture by $b$, stretches it by $|a|$ and reflects it when $a < 0$. For $a\ne0$, sketch the two curves side by side: the same shape, a different centre, and a width that changes by $|a|$. If $a=0$, the variable is the constant $b$, so the distribution collapses to a point.</Callout>

## How it is examined

Write the mean and variance as separate calculations, with units and squared coefficients for variance. Before omitting covariance terms, justify independence or zero covariance. In a proof of unbiasedness, compute the expectation of the estimator rather than claiming that one realised estimate equals the parameter.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Writing $\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a\operatorname{Var}(X) + b$: the constant $b$ does not change the variance, and $a$ must be squared.</Callout>

- Subtracting variances: $\operatorname{Var}(X - Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y)$ for independent $X$ and $Y$.
- Squaring the coefficient in the mean: $E(2X) = 2E(X)$, not $4E(X)$.
- Dividing the sum of squares by $n$ when estimating $\sigma^{2}$: the estimate is biased. Use $n - 1$.

## Summary

- For $Y = aX + b$: $E(Y) = aE(X) + b$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = a^{2}\operatorname{Var}(X)$.
- The mean of any linear combination is the same combination of the means; no independence is needed.
- The variance of a linear combination uses independence here, and every coefficient is squared.
- The sample mean $\bar{X}$ is an unbiased estimate of $\mu$.
- The sample variance $s_{n-1}^{2}$, with divisor $n - 1$, is an unbiased estimate of $\sigma^{2}$.
- State independence before you add variances.
