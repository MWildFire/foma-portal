## Big idea

Correlation measures how closely two numerical variables follow a straight line. Regression turns that line into a rule for predicting one variable from the other.

## Explanation

A scatter diagram plots each pair $(x, y)$ as a point. First look at the pattern: is it roughly a straight line, and does $y$ tend to rise or fall as $x$ rises? The <Term id="pearson-r-coefficient"/> summarises the strength and direction of a linear pattern. When both variables have nonzero variation, it lies between $-1$ and $1$ and is calculated with the <Formula id="pearson-product-moment-r"/>.

<Callout kind="gdc">Enter the $x$-values and the $y$-values in two named columns of a Lists & Spreadsheet page. On a Calculator page choose `Menu → Statistics → Stat Calculations → Linear Regression (mx+b)`, set X List and Y List, and read `m` (the gradient $a$), `b` and `r`. `Two-Variable Statistics` in the same menu also gives `r`.</Callout>

The <Term id="regression-line-y-on-x"/> predicts $y$ from $x$. It is written $y = ax + b$, where $a$ is the gradient and $b$ is the intercept. Its gradient can also be found from $r$ and the standard deviations, using <Formula id="regression-line-slope-intercept"/>.

For ordinary least squares with an intercept and nonconstant x, the line passes through the <Term id="mean-point-regression"/>. Substituting $\bar{x}$ into the equation gives $\bar{y}$, as <Formula id="regression-line-mean-point"/> states. Use this to check your answer by hand.

<Callout kind="define">Regression line of $y$ on $x$: the least-squares straight line $y = ax + b$ that minimises the sum of squared vertical distances from the points, used to predict $y$ from $x$.</Callout>

## Worked example

A gardener gives five plants different masses of fertiliser, $x$ grams, and records their heights $y$ cm after four weeks: $x$: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10; $y$: 5, 9, 10, 14, 18.

<Steps>
<Step>The means are $\bar{x} = 6$ and $\bar{y} = 11.2$, so the regression line passes through $(6, 11.2)$.</Step>
<Step>The GDC gives $a = 1.55$ and $b = 1.90$, so the regression line is $y = 1.55x + 1.90$.</Step>
<Step>The correlation is $r = 0.986$, a strong positive linear relationship. For $x = 9$, the prediction is $\hat{y} = 1.55 \times 9 + 1.90 = 15.85$ cm.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why check the mean point?">Put $x = 6$ into the line: $1.55 \times 6 + 1.90 = 11.2$, which equals $\bar{y}$. A mismatch may indicate entry, rounding or equation-selection errors; this mean-point identity applies to least squares with an intercept, not every regression convention.</Reveal>

## How it is examined

For Find, give the regression direction, coefficients and requested accuracy. Keep full coefficients for predictions. For Interpret, give the slope with response units per explanatory-variable unit. For Comment, refer to the scatterplot, outliers and interpolation or extrapolation; correlation alone does not establish causation.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Swapping $x$ and $y$ in the GDC lists: the two regressions generally differ; with perfect nonzero linear correlation they describe the same geometric line.</Callout>

- Reading $b$ as the gradient. The gradient is $a$, and $b$ is the intercept where the line crosses the $y$-axis.
- Predicting $y$ for an $x$ outside the data range without a warning. Such an extrapolation is less reliable.
- Writing that a strong correlation proves that $x$ causes $y$. Correlation shows association only.

## Summary

- Pearson's r measures linear association and lies in[-1,1] when both variables vary; it is undefined if either variable is constant.
- The regression line of $y$ on $x$ is $y = ax + b$, with $a = r\,s_y/s_x$ and $b = \bar{y} - a\bar{x}$.
- The ordinary least-squares line with an intercept and nonconstant x passes through the mean point $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$.
- Use the line to predict $y$ from $x$ inside the data range, and say when a prediction is an extrapolation.
- Correlation does not show cause: say so when a question asks about cause.
- Check the decimal places and the order of the variables in every answer.
