## Big idea

A hypothesis test asks whether the sample gives enough evidence to reject a claim about the population. A test can go wrong in two ways: it can reject a claim that is true, or keep a claim that is false. The significance level controls the first risk, and a larger sample helps with the second.

## Explanation

<Callout kind="define">Hypothesis test: a procedure that decides whether the data give enough evidence to reject the null hypothesis $H_0$ in favour of the alternative hypothesis $H_1$.</Callout>

Write the claim being tested as $H_0$, and the claim you want evidence for as $H_1$. Choose the significance level $\alpha$, and whether the test is one-tailed or two-tailed, from $H_1$ before you calculate. Reject $H_0$ when the p-value is less than $\alpha$. The p-value is the probability of a result at least as extreme as the one observed, assuming $H_0$ is true. It is not the probability that $H_0$ is true.

When the population variance is unknown, use the <Term id="population-mean-t-test"/>. Its statistic is given below, where $s$ is the sample standard deviation with $n - 1$ in the denominator, so the degrees of freedom are $n - 1$. If the population standard deviation $\sigma$ is known, the same logic gives a z-test with the normal distribution.

<Formula id="population-mean-t-statistic"/>

<Callout kind="gdc">On a Calculator page type `tTest μ0,x̄,sx,n,Hypoth` with your numbers; for raw data, first read `x̄` and `sx` from One-Variable Statistics. The last number (`Hypoth`) sets $H_1$: 0 for not equal to (the default), a positive number for greater than, a negative number for less than. `stat.t` gives the statistic and `stat.PVal` gives the p-value for the tail you chose, so compare `stat.PVal` with $\alpha$ directly and do not halve it.</Callout>

For a Poisson mean, suppose $X \sim \text{Po}(m)$ and you test $H_0: m = m_0$ from one count; the critical region then comes straight from the Poisson cumulative probabilities. For $n$ independent counts over equal intervals, test the total $T$, which has the distribution given below under $H_0$ (the sum rule from topic 4.17). Because the Poisson distribution is discrete, the actual significance level is at most $\alpha$, and often well below it.

<Callout kind="gdc">On a Calculator page choose `Menu → Probability → Distributions → Poisson Cdf` (typed `poissCdf(m,c)`) and enter the mean for the whole period, $n m_0$, not the mean for one interval. Try successive values of $c$ and take the largest one whose cumulative probability $P(T \le c)$ is at most $\alpha$.</Callout>

<Reveal title="Why is the actual significance level rarely exactly 5%?">Counts take whole-number values, so the cumulative probability $P(T \le c)$ jumps from one value to the next. A critical region rarely has probability exactly $0.05$, so the test uses the largest region with probability at most $\alpha$.</Reveal>

For bivariate normal data with sample correlation $r$ from $n$ pairs, test $H_0: \rho = 0$ with the statistic below, which has $n - 2$ degrees of freedom. Use $H_1: \rho \neq 0$ for a two-tailed test, or $H_1: \rho > 0$ or $H_1: \rho < 0$ for a one-tailed test. The t-test for the slope of the regression line gives the same p-value.

<Callout kind="gdc">Enter the pairs of data in two named columns of a Lists & Spreadsheet page; `Menu → Statistics → Stat Calculations → Linear Regression (mx+b)` on a Calculator page gives `r`. For the test, use the Linear Reg t Test from the list of statistics tests: it tests the slope and gives the same $t$ and p-value as $H_0: \rho = 0$. Check the tail of $H_1$ before you compare the p-value with $\alpha$.</Callout>

Two kinds of error are possible. A <Term id="type-i-error-probability"/> happens when $H_0$ is true but the test rejects it; its probability is $\alpha$ for a continuous test, and at most $\alpha$ for a discrete one. A <Term id="type-ii-error-probability"/> happens when $H_1$ is true but the test does not reject $H_0$; its probability is $\beta$, and the power of the test is $1 - \beta$.

To find $\beta$, you need one specific value of the parameter under $H_1$. First find the critical region using $H_0$, then find the probability of falling outside it when the parameter has that value. For a fixed $\alpha$, a larger sample reduces both errors. Raising $\alpha$ lowers $\beta$, but increases the risk of a Type I error.

<Callout kind="tip">In a context question, say what each error means for the people involved, for example "we concluded that the fault rate has fallen when it has not".</Callout>

## Worked example

A bakery claims that its loaves weigh 500 g on average. A random sample of 9 loaves has mean 512 g and standard deviation 18 g. Assume the weights are normal. Test $H_0: \mu = 500$ against $H_1: \mu > 500$ at the 5% significance level.

<Steps>
<Step>$t = \dfrac{512 - 500}{18/\sqrt{9}} = 2.00$ (to 2 decimal places), with $9 - 1 = 8$ degrees of freedom.</Step>
<Step>The one-tailed p-value is $P(T > 2.00) = 0.040$ (to 3 decimal places) for $T \sim t_{8}$.</Step>
<Step>Since $0.040 < 0.05$, reject $H_0$. There is evidence that the mean weight of the loaves is more than 500 g.</Step>
</Steps>

A workshop's machine produces faults at random. Let $X$ be the number of faults in one day, with $X \sim \text{Po}(m)$. Test $H_0: m = 10$ against $H_1: m < 10$ at the 5% significance level, using one day's count.

<Steps>
<Step>Under $H_0$, $P(X \le 4) = 0.029$ and $P(X \le 5) = 0.067$ (to 3 decimal places). The largest critical region with probability at most 0.05 is $X \le 4$, so the actual significance level is 0.029.</Step>
<Step>If $X \sim \text{Po}(6)$, then $P(X \le 4) = 0.285$: this is the power of the test. The Type II error probability is $\beta = 1 - 0.285 = 0.715$.</Step>
</Steps>

## How it is examined

Calculate and Find questions give a mark for the test statistic, a mark for the p-value and a mark for the conclusion in words. Write down questions ask for the hypotheses, the significance level and the tail; marks are lost when $H_1$ is not written in the form the question needs. Comment and Explain questions ask about errors in context: use the words "reject" and "do not reject", and say what the decision means for the claim. For a discrete test, write the critical region and the actual significance level.

<Callout kind="exam">Write the hypotheses in terms of the parameter ($\mu$, $m$ or $\rho$) before any calculation. A conclusion with no comparison with the significance level loses the final mark.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Writing "accept $H_0$". The data support "do not reject $H_0$"; a non-significant result does not prove the claim.</Callout>

- Using $n$ instead of $n - 1$ for the t-test degrees of freedom, or $n - 1$ instead of $n - 2$ for the correlation test.
- Choosing a one-tailed test after seeing the data, or quoting a one-tailed p-value when $H_1$ is two-tailed.
- Taking the p-value as the probability that $H_0$ is true.
- Calculating $\beta$ with the wrong critical region, or without a specific value of the parameter under $H_1$.

## Summary

- Reject $H_0$ only when the p-value is less than the significance level $\alpha$; otherwise write "do not reject $H_0$".
- For the mean of a normal population with unknown variance, use the t-statistic with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom.
- For a Poisson mean, test the total of $n$ independent counts: under $H_0$ it is Poisson with mean $n m_0$.
- To test $\rho = 0$ for bivariate normal data, use $t = \dfrac{r\sqrt{n-2}}{\sqrt{1-r^2}}$ with $n - 2$ degrees of freedom.
- A Type I error rejects a true $H_0$ (probability $\alpha$); a Type II error keeps a false $H_0$ (probability $\beta$).
- Finding $\beta$ needs one specific value under $H_1$; the power of the test is $1 - \beta$.
