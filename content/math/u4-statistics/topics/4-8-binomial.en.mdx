## Big idea

The binomial distribution counts the successes in a fixed number $n$ of independent trials, each with the same probability $p$ of success. Two numbers, $n$ and $p$, describe the whole distribution: every probability, the mean and the variance follow from them.

## Explanation

A count $X$ follows a <Term id="binomial-distribution"/> when the <Term id="binomial-conditions"/> hold: a fixed number $n$ of trials, two outcomes in each trial (success or failure), the same probability $p$ of success in every trial, and independent trials. We write $X \sim \text{B}(n, p)$, and $X$ takes the values $0, 1, \dots, n$.

<Callout kind="define">Binomial distribution: the distribution of the number of successes in $n$ independent trials, each with probability $p$ of success. Written $X \sim \text{B}(n, p)$.</Callout>

For $r = 0, 1, \dots, n$:

$$
P(X = r) = \binom{n}{r} p^{r} (1-p)^{n-r}
$$

<Formula id="binomial-pmf"/>

The factor $\binom{n}{r}$ counts the ways to place the $r$ successes among the $n$ trials, and $p^{r}(1-p)^{n-r}$ is the probability of one particular sequence with $r$ successes.

The mean is $np$ and the variance is $np(1-p)$. <Formula id="binomial-mean-variance"/>

<Callout kind="gdc">On a Calculator page choose `Menu → Probability → Distributions → Binomial Pdf` or `Binomial Cdf`, or type `binomPdf(n,p,r)` and `binomCdf(n,p,a,b)`. The pdf gives $P(X = r)$, the cdf gives $P(a \le X \le b)$, and `binomCdf(n,p,x)` with one bound gives $P(X \le x)$. For $P(X \ge k)$, use `binomCdf(n,p,k,n)`.</Callout>

A <Term id="binomial-cumulative-probability"/> such as $P(X \ge k)$ comes from the cdf: $P(X \ge k) = 1 - P(X \le k - 1)$. <Formula id="binomial-tail-complement"/>

## Worked example

A basketball player makes 70% of her free throws, and each throw is independent of the others. In 10 throws, let $X$ be the number of baskets, so $X \sim \text{B}(10, 0.7)$.

<Steps>
<Step>$P(X = 8) = \dbinom{10}{8}(0.7)^{8}(0.3)^{2} = 0.233$ (to 3 decimal places).</Step>
<Step>$P(X \ge 9) = P(X = 9) + P(X = 10) = 0.149$ (to 3 decimal places).</Step>
<Step>$E(X) = np = 10 \times 0.7 = 7$ and $\text{Var}(X) = np(1-p) = 10 \times 0.7 \times 0.3 = 2.1$.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why does at least 9 start at 9?">"At least 9" includes 9 itself, so the tail starts at 9. "More than 9" starts at 10, and it gives a smaller answer.</Reveal>

## How it is examined

Define the count and success event, name n and p and check all four binomial conditions. Show the probability expression or GDC entry and distinguish at least, more than and exactly. Give mean, variance or standard deviation according to the question.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Treating "at least 9" as "more than 9", so the answer uses $P(X \ge 10)$ instead of $P(X \ge 9)$.</Callout>

- Dropping the binomial coefficient $\binom{n}{r}$: the product $p^{r}(1-p)^{n-r}$ is the probability of one sequence only.
- Quoting the standard deviation $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$ when the question asks for the variance $np(1-p)$.
- Using the binomial model when the probability changes between trials, for example when items are chosen without replacement.

## Summary

- $X \sim \text{B}(n, p)$ counts the successes in $n$ independent trials, each with probability $p$ of success.
- Check the four conditions in the context, and name $n$ and $p$ before you calculate.
- $P(X = r) = \binom{n}{r} p^{r}(1-p)^{n-r}$; on the GDC use the pdf for one value and the cdf for a range.
- The mean is $np$ and the variance is $np(1-p)$.
- Use the complement for "at least" and "more than" when the cdf accumulates from zero.
- Round only at the end, and give the number of decimal places the question asks for.
