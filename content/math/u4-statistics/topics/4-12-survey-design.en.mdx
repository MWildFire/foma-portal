## Big idea

A survey is useful only when its data are valid (they measure the intended thing from a representative sample) and reliable (repeating it gives consistent results). A chi-squared test can then check whether counts fit a model, but only after numerical data are grouped into sensible classes and the degrees of freedom are reduced for every parameter estimated from the data.

## Explanation

Good data collection starts with the sample and the questions. Probability sampling from a frame covering the population reduces selection bias compared with a convenience sample of friends; non-response and measurement errors still matter. Choose variables that answer the research question, and word the questions so that they do not lead the respondent. A pilot test on a few people shows confusing questions before the main survey.

<Term id="reliability-of-questionnaire"/> means that the questionnaire gives consistent results when it is used again under the same conditions. A common check is test–retest: give the same questionnaire twice and compare the answers. For numerical scores, use the correlation between the two sets, Pearson's $r$ or Spearman's $r_s$. For categorical answers, a chi-squared test can examine association, as in topic 4.11, but association is not agreement. If every yes response becomes no and every no becomes yes, association can be perfect while no answer agrees. Also compare paired agreement directly.

<Term id="validity-of-questionnaire"/> means that it measures what it is meant to measure. Compare the results with an independent criterion, such as marks from a school test, or ask experts to judge the content. A strong association with the criterion supports validity, but no single number proves it.

Before a chi-squared test, numerical data must be grouped. <Term id="categorising-numerical-data"/> means splitting the values into classes that do not overlap and cover every value. Neighbouring classes are combined until every expected frequency is at least 5.

For counts modelled by a Poisson distribution, the mean is estimated by the sample mean, and the expected number of observations with $x$ events is <Formula id="expected-fitted-poisson"/>. For a continuous variable modelled by a normal distribution with the sample mean and variance, the expected number in the class from $a$ to $b$ is <Formula id="expected-fitted-normal"/>. Class probabilities come from standardising each boundary, <Formula id="z-score-class-boundary"/>, and reading the standard normal distribution on the GDC.

Each parameter estimated from the data removes one more degree of freedom, so the degrees of freedom are <Formula id="df-estimated-parameters"/>.

<Callout kind="define">Degrees of freedom for a goodness-of-fit test: $\nu = k - 1 - p$, where $k$ is the number of classes after grouping and $p$ is the number of parameters estimated from the sample.</Callout>

## Worked example

A school measures the heights of 100 students and groups them into four classes. The sample mean is 165 cm and the standard deviation is 7 cm, and a normal model with these estimates is fitted. Observed counts: below 158 cm, 18; from 158 to 165 cm, 30; from 165 to 172 cm, 36; above 172 cm, 16. Test at the 5% level whether the normal model fits the data.

<Steps>
<Step>Four classes and two parameters estimated from the sample give $\nu = 4 - 1 - 2 = 1$.</Step>
<Step>Under the model, $P(X < 158) = P\left(Z < \dfrac{158 - 165}{7}\right) = P(Z < -1) = 0.1587$ (to 4 decimal places). The expected count below 158 cm is $100 \times 0.1587 = 15.9$, and by symmetry the expected count above 172 cm is also 15.9. Each of the two middle classes has $100 \times (0.5 - 0.1587) = 34.1$.</Step>
<Step>$\chi^2 = \sum \dfrac{(O-E)^2}{E} = 0.891$ (to 3 decimal places). With $\nu = 1$, the p-value is $P(\chi^2 > 0.891) = 0.345$ (to 3 significant figures), and the 5% critical value is $3.841$.</Step>
<Step>Decision: $0.345 > 0.05$, or equivalently $0.891 < 3.841$. Do not reject $H_0$: the normal model is consistent with the data at the 5% level.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why is the degree of freedom 1 and not 3?">The four counts add up to 100, which removes one degree of freedom and leaves $4 - 1 = 3$. Estimating the mean and the standard deviation from the same data uses up two more, leaving $3 - 2 = 1$.</Reveal>

## Visual

<Callout kind="tip">Sketch the observed counts as bars and mark the expected counts on the same axes. A bar far above or below its expected height identifies a discrepancy; a discrepancy alone does not establish model failure. In the chi-squared sum, each class contributes $(O-E)^2/E$, so the largest contribution points to the class that disagrees most with the model.</Callout>

## How it is examined

Explain validity, reliability or selection bias using a concrete feature of the survey. Reliability concerns consistency or agreement; association alone can be misleading. For goodness of fit, show exhaustive disjoint classes, adequate expected counts and degrees of freedom after pooling and parameter estimation. Interpret the statistical decision without declaring a model proved.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Using $\nu = k - 1$ after the mean or the standard deviation has been estimated from the data. Subtract one more for each estimated parameter.</Callout>

- Keeping a class whose expected frequency is below 5. Combine it with a neighbour before the statistic is calculated.
- Calling a questionnaire reliable because its questions look sensible. Reliability needs repeated data and a test.
- Writing "the survey is valid" without saying how: name the sampling method, the pilot test or the criterion.

## Summary

- A survey is valid when it measures the intended variable on a sample that represents the population; probability sampling reduces selection bias but does not guarantee a representative realised sample.
- Reliability means consistent results on repetition; test it with a second administration and agreement measures as well as any correlation or association test.
- Validity is judged against an independent criterion or expert opinion, not by one number.
- Group numerical data into non-overlapping classes before a chi-squared test, and combine classes until every expected frequency is at least 5.
- Each parameter estimated from the sample reduces the degrees of freedom: $\nu = k - 1 - p$.
- For a Poisson fit use $E_i = nP(X = x_i)$ with the sample mean as $m$; for a normal fit use class probabilities with the sample mean and standard deviation.
