## Big idea

Combined events use the words "or" and "and", and a condition changes the sample space. Two rules do most of the work: add the probabilities and subtract the overlap, or multiply along a branch when the second event depends on the first.

## Explanation

Two events, $A$ and $B$, combine in two ways. $A \cup B$ means A or B or both, and $A \cap B$ means both A and B. A <Term id="venn-diagram-events"/> shows these regions. For dice or counters, a sample-space grid or a list of outcomes does the same job.

Events that cannot occur together are <Term id="mutually-exclusive-events"/>. For them $A \cap B$ is empty. For any two events that can overlap, the addition rule subtracts the overlap, so that it is counted once:

$$
P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)
$$

<Formula id="addition-rule-two-events"/>

The probability of $A$ when $B$ has already occurred is a <Term id="conditional-probability"/>. The given event $B$ becomes the new sample space, so it goes in the denominator:

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}
$$

<Formula id="conditional-probability-formula"/>

<Callout kind="define">Conditional probability: the probability of $A$ when the sample space has been reduced to the event $B$, written $P(A \mid B)$.</Callout>

In a tree diagram, the probabilities on the second stage are conditional on the first stage. Along one branch, multiply the probabilities:

$$
P(A \cap B) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B)
$$

<Formula id="multiplication-rule-tree"/>

Two events are <Term id="independent-events"/> when knowing that one has occurred does not change the probability of the other. Test this with a calculation: the events are independent only if $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$.

$$
P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)
$$

<Formula id="independence-product-rule"/>

## Worked example

Of 50 students, 30 study French, 24 study German, and 12 study both. One student is chosen at random, and each student is equally likely. Let $F$ be the event that the student studies French and $G$ the event that the student studies German.

<Steps>
<Step>Addition rule: $P(F \cup G) = \dfrac{30 + 24 - 12}{50} = 0.840$, so most students study at least one of the two languages.</Step>
<Step>Conditional probability: $P(F \mid G) = \dfrac{P(F \cap G)}{P(G)} = \dfrac{12/50}{24/50} = 0.500$. Among the German students, half also study French.</Step>
<Step>Independence test: $P(F) = 0.600$ and $P(G) = 0.480$, so $P(F)\,P(G) = 0.288$. But $P(F \cap G) = 0.240$, and $0.288 \ne 0.240$, so $F$ and $G$ are not independent.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why is P(F | G) different from P(F)?">Knowing that a student studies German lowers the probability of French from $0.600$ to $0.500$. A change in the probability means the two events are not independent.</Reveal>

## Visual

<Callout kind="tip">Start a Venn diagram with the overlap: write the number of students who study both in the middle, then the numbers outside the overlap in each circle, and finally the students outside both circles inside the rectangle $U$. For a tree diagram, write each probability on its branch, multiply along one branch, and add the results of the branches that give the same outcome.</Callout>

## How it is examined

Questions give a Venn diagram, a two-way table or a tree diagram, and ask for a combined or conditional probability. Method marks (M1) are given for the addition rule and for the correct denominator in a conditional probability. The accuracy mark (A1) is for the answer to the required number of decimal places. An independence question needs a reason (R1): compare $P(A \cap B)$ with $P(A)\,P(B)$ and state the conclusion.

<Callout kind="exam">For a conditional probability, write the given event in the denominator before you substitute. Examiners check that the denominator is the probability of the event that is given in the question.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Dividing by $P(A)$ when the condition is $B$. The denominator of $P(A \mid B)$ is $P(B)$.</Callout>

- Adding $P(A)$ and $P(B)$ for overlapping events, and forgetting to subtract $P(A \cap B)$.
- Keeping the first-stage probabilities on the second stage of a tree diagram when the counter is not replaced.
- Calling mutually exclusive events independent. Events that cannot occur together are dependent when both have non-zero probability.
- Deciding independence by intuition instead of by calculating $P(A \cap B)$ and $P(A)\,P(B)$.

## Summary

- $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$; mutually exclusive events have $P(A \cap B) = 0$.
- $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$: the given event $B$ is the denominator.
- On a tree diagram, multiply along a branch: $P(A \cap B) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B)$.
- Events are independent when $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$; test this with numbers.
- Draw a Venn diagram or a tree diagram before you calculate, and fill the overlap first.
- Without replacement, the probabilities on later branches change.
