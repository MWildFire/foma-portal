## Big idea

The Poisson distribution counts events in a fixed interval when they occur at random, one at a time, independently and at a constant average rate. One parameter, $m$, sets both the mean and the variance.

## Explanation

Counts like these are common: calls to a helpline in an hour, faults along a cable, customers at a café in half an hour. For a homogeneous Poisson process, counts in disjoint intervals are independent, the rate is constant, and the probability of two or more arrivals in an interval of length $h$ is negligible compared with $h$ as $h$ tends to zero. Constant rate alone is insufficient.

The <Term id="poisson-distribution"/> is written $X \sim \text{Po}(m)$, where <Term id="poisson-parameter"/> $m$ is the average number of events in the interval:

$$
P(X = x) = \frac{e^{-m}\, m^{x}}{x!}, \qquad x = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

<Formula id="poisson-pmf"/>

For a Poisson variable both the mean and the variance equal $m$. Because $m$ belongs to one particular interval, it must be rescaled when the interval changes: an average of 4 per 10 minutes becomes $m = 12$ over 30 minutes, not $m = 4$.

<Term id="independent-poisson-sum"/> is the same idea in reverse. If $X \sim \text{Po}(m_1)$ and $Y \sim \text{Po}(m_2)$ are independent, then $X + Y \sim \text{Po}(m_1 + m_2)$.

<Callout kind="define">Poisson distribution: the distribution of the number of events in a fixed interval, when the events happen one at a time, independently, at a constant mean rate. Written $X \sim \text{Po}(m)$.</Callout>

<Callout kind="gdc">On a Calculator page choose `Menu → Probability → Distributions → Poisson Pdf` or `Poisson Cdf`, or type `poissPdf(m,x)` and `poissCdf(m,x)`, entering the mean $m$ for the interval you need, not the rate per minute. The cdf gives $P(X \le x)$, so $P(X \ge k) = 1 - P(X \le k - 1)$.</Callout>

## Worked example

Assume calls to a helpline follow a Poisson process: counts in disjoint intervals are independent, the rate is constant, and multiple arrivals in a sufficiently short interval are negligible. The average is 3 per hour. Let $X$ be the number of calls in one hour, so $X \sim \text{Po}(3)$.

<Steps>
<Step>$P(X = 5) = \dfrac{e^{-3}\, 3^{5}}{5!} = 0.101$ (to 3 decimal places).</Step>
<Step>$P(X \le 2) = e^{-3}\left(1 + 3 + \dfrac{3^{2}}{2!}\right) = 8.5\,e^{-3} = 0.423$ (to 3 decimal places).</Step>
<Step>Over two hours the mean doubles to $m = 6$. Two independent hours give $Y \sim \text{Po}(6)$, so $P(Y = 4) = \dfrac{e^{-6}\, 6^{4}}{4!} = 0.134$ (to 3 decimal places).</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why not keep m = 3 for two hours?">The mean grows with the length of the interval: two hours have twice the expected number of calls. Keeping $m = 3$ would give the answer for one hour, not for two.</Reveal>

## How it is examined

Define the count and interval, state the process assumptions and rescale the mean to that interval. Use the PMF for exactly and the CDF or complement for a tail. A rare event or a constant average rate alone does not justify the Poisson model. Link a statistical comment to the tail probability and a stated significance threshold.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Using the rate per hour for a half-hour interval without rescaling $m$.</Callout>

- Writing $P(X \ge k)$ as $P(X \le k)$. The complement is $1 - P(X \le k - 1)$.
- Forgetting that $0! = 1$ when $x = 0$.
- Calling a count Poisson without stating the assumptions: single, independent events at a constant rate.

## Summary

- The Poisson distribution counts events in a fixed interval; $m$ is the expected count for that interval, equal to rate × interval length. A constant rate alone is insufficient to justify the model.
- $P(X = x) = e^{-m} m^{x} / x!$ for $x = 0, 1, 2, \dots$; the mean and the variance both equal $m$.
- Rescale $m$ whenever the interval length changes.
- A sum of independent Poisson counts is Poisson, with the means added.
- The model needs single, independent events at a constant rate; say so in your answer.
