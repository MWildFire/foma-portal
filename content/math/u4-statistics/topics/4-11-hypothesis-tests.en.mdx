## Big idea

A hypothesis test asks whether the data are consistent with a claim. We assume the claim is true for now and measure how surprising the data would be if it were. If the data would be very surprising, we reject the claim; if not, we do not reject it.

## Explanation

Every test starts with two hypotheses. The <Term id="null-hypothesis-h0"/> ($H_0$) states no effect, no association, or the claimed value. The alternative hypothesis $H_1$ states what we suspect instead. A one-tailed test is used when $H_1$ gives a direction, such as $\mu > 8$; a two-tailed test is used when $H_1$ says only $\mu \ne 8$.

We decide at a fixed <Term id="significance-level-alpha"/> $\alpha$, usually 0.05. The <Term id="p-value-of-a-test"/> is the probability, assuming $H_0$ is true, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed. The rule is: if the p-value is less than $\alpha$, reject $H_0$; otherwise do not reject $H_0$. A test never proves $H_0$ true.

For a table of counts, the <Term id="chi-squared-independence-test"/> compares observed frequencies with the frequencies expected under independence. Each expected frequency comes from the totals, <Formula id="expected-frequency-independence"/>. The statistic is <Formula id="chi-squared-statistic"/>, summed over all cells, and the degrees of freedom are <Formula id="df-contingency-table"/>.

<Callout kind="define">Critical value: the number $c$ with $P(\chi^2 > c) = \alpha$. Reject $H_0$ when the statistic is greater than $c$; this gives the same decision as the p-value.</Callout>

Every expected frequency should be at least 5. If a cell is smaller, combine categories in a contextually meaningful way before computing the test, otherwise the chi-squared distribution is a poor approximation.

For a goodness-of-fit test, the expected count in category $i$ is $E_i = np_i$ for the claimed probabilities $p_i$. With $k$ categories and no parameter estimated from the data, the degrees of freedom are $k - 1$. Topic 4.12 covers what changes when a parameter is estimated.

For independent observations from a normal population with positive variance and $n>1$, a claim about the population mean $\mu$ with unknown standard deviation can be tested using a t-test with the statistic <Formula id="t-statistic-one-sample"/>, compared with the t-distribution with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom. In a two-tailed test the p-value counts both tails. Comparing two means follows the same rule: a small p-value means the difference would be unlikely if the means were equal.

## Worked example

A school asks 120 students for their year group and how they usually travel to school. IB1: bus 28, walk 9, car 23 (60 students). IB2: bus 22, walk 6, car 32 (60 students). Test at the 5% level whether year group and travel are independent.

<Steps>
<Step>$H_0$: year group and travel are independent. $H_1$: they are not independent. The column totals are 50, 15 and 55, and both row totals are 60. The expected frequency for IB1 and bus is $E = \dfrac{60 \times 50}{120} = 25$. The other expected values in the IB1 row are $7.5$ (walk) and $27.5$ (car). The IB2 row has the same values, because its row total is also 60.</Step>
<Step>$\chi^2 = \sum \dfrac{(O-E)^2}{E} = 2.793$ (to 3 decimal places). The six cell contributions add up to this value.</Step>
<Step>$\nu = (2-1)(3-1) = 2$. The p-value is $P(\chi^2 > 2.793) = 0.247$ (to 3 significant figures). The 5% critical value for 2 degrees of freedom is $5.991$ (to 3 decimal places).</Step>
<Step>Decision: $0.247 > 0.05$, which is the same as $2.793 < 5.991$. Do not reject $H_0$. There is insufficient evidence at the 5% level that year group and travel are associated; this does not establish independence.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why is the expected value 25 and not 28?">The expected count is the average predicted by independence, not a count that every independent sample must show. Both rows total 60, so each travel mode should take half of its column total: $50 / 2 = 25$. The observed 28 is slightly higher, but a difference of this size is well within what chance produces.</Reveal>

## How it is examined

State hypotheses about the population and the significance level before calculating. Show an expected count or the test statistic when requested; report degrees of freedom and p-value with the specified accuracy. Compare with the threshold and conclude in context. A failure to reject does not prove the null hypothesis.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Writing that $H_0$ is accepted or proved true. A test either rejects $H_0$ or does not reject it.</Callout>

- Finding $E$ as row total divided by $N$. The expected value needs both totals: $E = R_i C_j / N$.
- Taking $\nu = n - 1$ for a table. For an $r \times c$ table, $\nu = (r-1)(c-1)$.
- Forgetting to double the one-tailed probability in a two-tailed t-test.

## Summary

- $H_0$ supplies the reference model for calculating the p-value; $H_1$ gives a direction (one-tailed) or none (two-tailed).
- Reject $H_0$ when the p-value is less than $\alpha$, equivalently when the statistic lies in the critical region for the chosen alternative. The chi-squared tests here reject in the right tail. For a t-test, let $c>0$ have right-tail probability $\alpha$: reject for $t>c$ in a right-tailed test or $t<-c$ in a left-tailed test. For a two-tailed test, instead choose $c$ with right-tail probability $\alpha/2$ and reject for $|t|>c$. Never write that $H_0$ is accepted.
- For independence, $E = R_i C_j / N$; every expected frequency should be at least 5.
- The chi-squared statistic is $\sum (O-E)^2/E$. The degrees of freedom are $(r-1)(c-1)$ for a table and $k-1$ for a goodness-of-fit test with no estimated parameter.
- The t-statistic is $t = (\bar{x} - \mu_0)/(s/\sqrt{n})$ with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom; a two-tailed p-value counts both tails.
- The GDC gives p-values, expected values and statistics, but you write the hypotheses and the conclusion in context.
