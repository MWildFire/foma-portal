## Big idea

Many quantities change at a rate that depends on their current value. A hot drink cools faster when it is much hotter than the room, and a tank loses salt faster when it holds more salt. A differential equation writes such a rule in symbols. Separation of variables solves the simplest of these equations: put every term in $y$ on one side and every term in $x$ (or $t$) on the other, then integrate both sides.

## Explanation

To set up a model, name the variable, describe the rate of change in words, and write it as a derivative. "The rate of cooling is proportional to the difference between the temperature and the room temperature" becomes $\dfrac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - 20)$. An equation that contains only a first derivative is a <Term id="de-first-order-equation"/>.

When the equation has the form $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = f(x)\,g(y)$, the variables can be separated. The <Term id="de-separation-of-variables"/> method then gives $\dfrac{1}{g(y)}\,dy = f(x)\,dx$: integrate both sides, add one constant, and solve for $y$. Work on an interval where $g(y) \neq 0$ and check any constant solutions $g(y)=0$ separately.

<Formula id="de-separated-form"/>

Integrating $\dfrac{1}{y}$ produces a logarithm, so after integrating, take exponentials of both sides to solve for $y$.

<Formula id="de-log-integral"/>

The <Term id="de-initial-condition"/> (for example $T(0) = 90$) fixes the constant. Cooling, limited growth and mixing with fresh water all have solutions of the form $y = L + (y_0 - L)e^{-kt}$, which approach $L$ as $t$ tends to infinity when $k > 0$. Here $t \geq 0$ and $k$ has reciprocal-time units.

<Formula id="de-relaxation-solution"/>

<Callout kind="define">A first-order differential equation relates a function to its first derivative. It can be solved by separation of variables when it can be written as $\dfrac{dy}{g(y)} = f(x)\,dx$.</Callout>

## Worked example

A cup of tea at 90 °C is placed in a room at 20 °C. The rate of cooling is proportional to $T - 20$, where $T$ is the temperature in degrees Celsius and $t$ is the time in minutes. After 10 minutes the tea is at 55 °C.

<Steps>
<Step>Model: $\dfrac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - 20)$ with $T(0) = 90$. Separating and integrating gives $\dfrac{dT}{T - 20} = -k\,dt$, so $\ln|T - 20| = -kt + C$ and $T - 20 = Ae^{-kt}$. The initial condition gives $A = 70$, so $T = 20 + 70e^{-kt}$.</Step>
<Step>Use $T(10) = 55$: $20 + 70e^{-10k} = 55$, so $e^{-10k} = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and $k = \dfrac{\ln 2}{10} = 0.0693$ per minute (to 4 decimal places).</Step>
<Step>After 20 minutes $e^{-20k} = \dfrac{1}{4}$, so $T = 20 + 70 \times \dfrac{1}{4} = 37.5$ °C. The tea reaches 30 °C when $e^{-kt} = \dfrac{1}{7}$, so $t = \dfrac{10\ln 7}{\ln 2} = 28.1$ minutes (to 1 decimal place).</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Check by substitution">Differentiate $T = 20 + 70e^{-kt}$ to get $\dfrac{dT}{dt} = -70k\,e^{-kt} = -k(T - 20)$, which is the model. The initial condition also holds, because $20 + 70 = 90$.</Reveal>

## Visual

<Callout kind="tip">Sketch $T$ against $t$. The curve starts at 90, falls quickly while $T$ is well above 20, and flattens towards the horizontal line $T = 20$ without crossing it. A larger $k$ makes the curve flatten sooner.</Callout>

## How it is examined

Set-up marks come from turning the words of the model into an equation with the right sign and the right quantity (for example $T - 20$, not $T$). Method marks need the separation and integration written out, including the constant of integration. The accuracy mark depends on using the initial condition to find the constant. Show that questions give the target expression, so every step must be visible. Comment questions need a reason linked to the context, such as the limit as $t$ becomes large or the range of time in which the model applies.

<Callout kind="exam">Write the general solution first, then substitute the initial condition. Give the particular solution in the form the question asks for, and state the units of $t$.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Writing the model with $T$ instead of $T - 20$. The tea would then cool towards 0 °C rather than towards the room temperature of 20 °C.</Callout>

- Forgetting the arbitrary constant of integration. One constant on one side is sufficient: two constants combine into a single $C = C_2 - C_1$.
- Dividing by $y$ and losing the constant solution $y = 0$. Check constant solutions separately.
- Writing $\ln|T - 20| = -kt + C$ and then treating it as $T - 20 = -kt + C$, without taking exponentials.

## Summary

- A differential equation links a function to its rate of change; a model turns the phrase "the rate is proportional to ..." into an equation for the derivative.
- Separation of variables: bring the equation to $\dfrac{dy}{g(y)} = f(x)\,dx$, integrate both sides and add one constant.
- The initial condition fixes the constant and gives the particular solution.
- Cooling, limited growth and mixing models have the form $y = L + (y_0 - L)e^{-kt}$ with $k > 0$ and approach $L$ as $t \to \infty$.
- Check a solution by substitution: differentiate it, compare with the equation and test the initial condition.
- Interpret the answer: the limit as $t$ becomes large, the time to reach a level, and the range in which the model is valid.
