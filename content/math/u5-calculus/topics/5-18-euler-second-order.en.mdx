## Big idea

A second-order equation for $x(t)$, such as a damped oscillation, can be solved numerically. Write $v = \frac{dx}{dt}$, turn the equation into two first-order equations, and step both forward together with Euler's method.

## Explanation

Let $v = \frac{dx}{dt}$. Then $\frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^{2}x}{dt^{2}}$, so the equation $\frac{d^{2}x}{dt^{2}} = f(x, \frac{dx}{dt}, t)$ becomes the system

$$
\frac{dx}{dt} = v, \qquad \frac{dv}{dt} = f(x, v, t).
$$

<Term id="second-order-reduction-system"/> is the name for this step, and <Formula id="second-order-as-system"/> gives it in full.

Euler's method with step length $h$ starts from $t_0$, $x_0$ and $v_0$, and moves forward one step at a time:

$$
x_{n+1} = x_n + h\,v_n, \qquad v_{n+1} = v_n + h\,f(x_n, v_n, t_n), \qquad t_{n+1} = t_n + h.
$$

<Formula id="euler-second-order-update"/>

Both updates use the values at the start of the step, as <Term id="euler-second-order-step"/> requires. After $n$ steps the time is $t_n = nh$, as <Term id="step-length-h-second-order"/> explains. If $f$ contains $t$, use the current $t_n$ at every step.

<Callout kind="define">Euler's method for a second-order equation: the approximate values of $x$ and $\frac{dx}{dt}$ at time $t_n$, found by applying the Euler step to the system with a fixed step length $h$.</Callout>

## Worked example

Take $\frac{d^{2}x}{dt^{2}} = -4x - 0.5\frac{dx}{dt}$, with $x = 1$ and $\frac{dx}{dt} = 0$ at $t = 0$, and step length $h = 0.1$.

<Steps>
<Step>Step 1 from $t_0 = 0$: $t_1 = 0.1$, $x_1 = 1 + 0.1 \times 0 = 1.000$ and $v_1 = 0 + 0.1(-4 \times 1 - 0.5 \times 0) = -0.400$.</Step>
<Step>Step 2 uses the values from step 1 on the right-hand side: $t_2 = 0.2$, $x_2 = 1.000 + 0.1 \times (-0.400) = 0.960$ and $v_2 = -0.400 + 0.1(-4 \times 1.000 - 0.5 \times (-0.400)) = -0.780$.</Step>
<Step>Step 3 again: $t_3 = 0.3$, $x_3 = 0.960 + 0.1 \times (-0.780) = 0.882$ and $v_3 = -0.780 + 0.1(-4 \times 0.960 - 0.5 \times (-0.780)) = -1.125$.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why not use the new x in the update for v?">Using the new position in the update for $v$ gives a different scheme, not Euler's method. Both updates use the values at the start of the step, which is why step 2 uses only $x_1$ and $v_1$.</Reveal>

## Visual

<Callout kind="tip">Plot $x$ against $t$ from your table and join the points with a smooth curve. Smaller steps put the points closer to the true curve. For an oscillation, each Euler step adds a little energy, so the peaks grow slowly; <Formula id="euler-energy-growth"/> gives the growth per step.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Using the new $x_{n+1}$ on the right-hand side when updating $v$. That is a different method, and it gives different values.</Callout>

- Applying Euler's method to $\frac{d^{2}x}{dt^{2}}$ directly, without first writing the system.
- Counting the steps wrongly. The number of steps is the total time divided by $h$.
- Rounding at every step, so that the final answer drifts away from the value the method gives.

## Summary

- Set $v = \frac{dx}{dt}$ to turn a second-order equation into two first-order equations.
- Each Euler step updates $x$ with $h\,v_n$ and $v$ with $h\,f(x_n, v_n, t_n)$, using the values at the start of the step.
- After $n$ steps the time is $t_n = nh$, so the number of steps is the total time divided by $h$.
- A smaller step length $h$ gives a more accurate result, but needs more steps.
- For an oscillation, Euler's method adds energy at each step, so the amplitude grows.
- Round only the final answers, and check them against an exact solution if one is given.
