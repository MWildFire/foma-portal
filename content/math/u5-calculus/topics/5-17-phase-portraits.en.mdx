## Big idea

Two quantities that influence each other can be described by two linear differential equations. When the determinant is nonzero, the type of the equilibrium at the origin is decided by the trace and determinant of the coefficient matrix. Together they show whether trajectories spiral, circle, settle into the origin, or leave along two lines.

## Explanation

A coupled linear system has the form $\frac{dx}{dt} = ax + by$, $\frac{dy}{dt} = cx + dy$. In matrix form it is $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = A\mathbf{x}$, where $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$.

<Formula id="linear-system-2d"/>

Each point of the <Term id="phase-plane-2d"/> is a state of the system. The origin is an <Term id="equilibrium-point-2d"/>, and it is the only one when $D \neq 0$. Its type follows from the trace $T = a + d$, the determinant $D = ad - bc$ and the eigenvalues, which solve $\lambda^{2} - T\lambda + D = 0$.

<Formula id="trace-determinant-2d"/>

<Formula id="eigenvalues-trace-determinant"/>

When $D=0$, solve $A\mathbf{x}=0$: a nonzero rank-one matrix gives a line of equilibria, while the zero matrix makes every point of the plane an equilibrium. For $D\neq0$, read the type in this order:

- $D < 0$: the eigenvalues are real with opposite signs. The origin is a <Term id="saddle-point-2d"/> and is always unstable.
- $D > 0$ and $T^{2} \geq 4D$: the eigenvalues are real with the same sign, so the origin is a node. It is stable if $T < 0$, when nearby trajectories go into the origin, and unstable if $T > 0$. A repeated eigenvalue ($T^{2} = 4D$) also gives a node.
- $D > 0$, $T^{2} < 4D$ and $T \neq 0$: the eigenvalues are complex, so the origin is a spiral (focus). It is stable if $T < 0$ and unstable if $T > 0$.
- $T = 0$ and $D > 0$: the eigenvalues are purely imaginary, $\lambda = \pm i\sqrt{D}$. The origin is a <Term id="centre-equilibrium-2d"/>, surrounded by closed orbits.

## Worked example

Take $\frac{dx}{dt} = x + 2y$ and $\frac{dy}{dt} = 3x + 2y$, so that $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

<Steps>
<Step>$T = 1 + 2 = 3$ and $D = (1)(2) - (2)(3) = -4$. Since $D < 0$, the origin is a saddle point.</Step>
<Step>The characteristic equation is $\lambda^{2} - 3\lambda - 4 = 0$, which factorises as $(\lambda - 4)(\lambda + 1) = 0$. The eigenvalues are $\lambda = 4$ and $\lambda = -1$.</Step>
<Step>For $\lambda = 4$, the first row gives $(1 - 4)x + 2y = 0$, so $y = 1.5x$: the eigen-direction $(2, 3)$ has slope $1.5$. For $\lambda = -1$, the first row gives $2x + 2y = 0$, so $y = -x$: the eigen-direction $(1, -1)$ has slope $-1$.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why is the origin unstable?">Trajectories that start on the line through $(2, 3)$ move away from the origin, because the eigenvalue $4$ is positive. Trajectories on the line through $(1, -1)$ move in towards the origin, because $-1$ is negative. A trajectory that starts near the origin in a general direction is pushed away along the first line, so the origin is unstable.</Reveal>

## How it is examined

Find questions give marks for the characteristic equation, for each eigenvalue and for the reason behind the classification. Determine questions ask for the type and the stability, justified by the signs of $D$ and $T$. Sketch questions give marks for the eigen-directions, for arrows that show whether trajectories move towards or away from the origin, and for a label that names the type.

<Callout kind="exam">Write the values of $T$ and $D$ before you name the type. A marker can then follow your working even if a later eigenvalue has a slip.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Calling any system with $T = 0$ a centre without checking that $D > 0$. If $D < 0$, the origin is a saddle.</Callout>

- Taking the trace as $b + c$ instead of $a + d$.
- Choosing between node and spiral from the sign of $T$ alone. The test is the sign of $T^{2} - 4D$.
- Drawing the arrows the wrong way. Arrows show increasing $t$, so a stable node has its arrows pointing towards the origin.

## Summary

- Write the system as $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = A\mathbf{x}$, then find $T = a + d$ and $D = ad - bc$.
- $D < 0$: a saddle point, always unstable.
- $D > 0$ and $T^{2} \geq 4D$: a node, stable if $T < 0$ and unstable if $T > 0$.
- $D > 0$, $T^{2} < 4D$ and $T \neq 0$: a spiral, stable if $T < 0$ and unstable if $T > 0$.
- $T = 0$ and $D > 0$: a centre, with closed orbits and purely imaginary eigenvalues.
- When $D \neq 0$ the origin is the only equilibrium point; sketch the eigen-directions first, then the arrows.
