## Big idea

A complex number $z = a + bi$ adds the imaginary unit $i$, defined by $i^{2} = -1$, to the real numbers. Its real part $a$ and imaginary part $b$ are both real, so each complex number is one point in the plane.

## Explanation

Unless the question specifies degrees, give the principal argument in radians, with $-\pi<\arg z\leq\pi$ (Oxford AI HL, ch. 8.4, p. 357). The argument of zero is undefined.

<Term id="complex-cartesian-form"/> is the form $z = a + bi$, where $\text{Re}\,z = a$ and $\text{Im}\,z = b$. Because $i^{2} = -1$, negative numbers have square roots: $\sqrt{-9} = 3i$. Two complex numbers are equal only when their real parts and their imaginary parts are equal.

Add and subtract by combining real parts and imaginary parts separately. Multiply by expanding the brackets and replacing $i^{2}$ by $-1$. To divide, multiply the top and the bottom by the <Term id="complex-conjugate"/> of the denominator, which makes the denominator real:

<Formula id="complex-division"/>

$$
\frac{a+bi}{c+di} = \frac{(a+bi)(c-di)}{c^{2}+d^{2}}
$$

<Term id="complex-modulus"/> $|z|$ is the distance from the origin to $z$. The argument $\arg z$ is the angle from the positive real axis to the line from the origin to $z$, measured anticlockwise. The <Term id="argand-diagram"/> shows the quadrant of $z$, so check it before you quote the angle.

<Formula id="complex-modulus-formula"/>

<Callout kind="define">Argand diagram: the complex plane, in which $a + bi$ is plotted as the point $(a, b)$, with the real part on the horizontal axis and the imaginary part on the vertical axis.</Callout>

If a quadratic with real coefficients has a negative discriminant, its two roots are complex conjugates of each other:

## Worked example

Let $z = 2 + 3i$ and $w = 1 - 4i$.

<Steps>
<Step>Quotient: $\dfrac{z}{w} = \dfrac{(2+3i)(1+4i)}{(1-4i)(1+4i)} = \dfrac{-10+11i}{17} = -0.588 + 0.647i$ (to 3 decimal places).</Step>
<Step>Modulus and argument of $z$: $|z| = \sqrt{2^{2}+3^{2}} = 3.61$ (to 3 significant figures) and $\arg z = 0.983$ radians (3 decimal places), equivalent to $56.3^\circ$ (1 decimal place).</Step>
<Step>Roots of $u^{2} - 4u + 13 = 0$: the discriminant is $-36$, so $u = \dfrac{4 \pm \sqrt{-36}}{2} = 2 \pm 3i$.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why multiply by the conjugate?">Multiplying by $1 + 4i$ turns the denominator into $(1-4i)(1+4i)$, which is a real number. The division then changes only the real and imaginary parts, and no complex denominator is left.</Reveal>

## How it is examined

Find and Show that questions on complex numbers award method marks for the right step, such as multiplying by the conjugate, using $i^{2} = -1$ or choosing the quadrant of the argument. Accuracy marks follow for the final values to the required accuracy. Write down and Find questions on the argument need the angle in the unit the question asks for. Powers of large complex numbers are usually found on the GDC, and the steps must still be written.

<Callout kind="exam">Write the answer in the form $a + bi$ with both parts shown. A correct real part with a missing imaginary part loses the final accuracy mark.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Dropping the imaginary part of a product, for example writing $(1+i)^{2} = 1 + i^{2} = 0$ instead of $2i$.</Callout>

- Dividing by $c + di$ without multiplying by its conjugate, or forgetting to divide the real and imaginary parts by $c^{2} + d^{2}$.
- Taking $\arg z$ as $\arctan(b/a)$ without checking the quadrant, which gives the wrong angle when $a < 0$.
- Writing the roots of $u^{2} - 4u + 13 = 0$ as $2 \pm 6i$. The imaginary part must also be divided by $2a = 2$, which gives $2 \pm 3i$.

## Summary

- $i^{2} = -1$, and every complex number is written $z = a + bi$ with real part $a$ and imaginary part $b$.
- To divide, multiply the top and the bottom by the conjugate of the denominator. The conjugate of $a + bi$ is $a - bi$.
- $|z| = \sqrt{a^{2} + b^{2}}$, and $|z|^{2} = z\bar{z}$.
- $\arg z$ is the anticlockwise angle from the positive real axis. Check its quadrant on the Argand diagram.
- Complex roots of a real quadratic with negative discriminant come in conjugate pairs.
- Use the GDC for large powers, and state the steps you used.
