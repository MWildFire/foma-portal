## Big idea

A logarithm undoes a power, so it turns multiplication into addition, division into subtraction and a power into a multiplication. Three laws, valid for a positive base $a \neq 1$ and positive arguments, let you combine, split and simplify logarithms.

## Explanation

If $\log_a x = p$, then $x = a^{p}$. This is the idea behind every law. <Term id="logarithm-definition"/>

Take $x = a^{p}$ and $y = a^{q}$. Then $xy = a^{p+q}$, so $\log_a(xy) = p + q = \log_a x + \log_a y$. The same index rules give the other two laws: $\dfrac{x}{y} = a^{p-q}$ and $x^{m} = a^{mp}$.

<Term id="log-product-law"/> turns a product inside a logarithm into a sum. <Formula id="log-product-rule"/>

<Term id="log-quotient-law"/> turns a quotient into a difference. <Formula id="log-quotient-rule"/>

<Term id="log-power-law"/> moves the power in front as a multiplier. <Formula id="log-power-rule"/>

<Callout kind="define">Laws of logarithms: for $a>0$, $a \neq 1$ and $x,y>0$, $\log_a(xy)=\log_a x+\log_a y$, $\log_a\left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right)=\log_a x-\log_a y$ and $\log_a(x^{m})=m\log_a x$.</Callout>

<Callout kind="gdc">To check a law numerically on a Calculator page, evaluate both sides with `log(x,b)`, where `b` is the base: `log(6*4,2)` and `log(6,2)+log(4,2)` give the same value. Without `b`, `log(x)` has base 10, and `ln(x)` has base $e$.</Callout>

## Worked example

Solve $\log_2(x+1)+\log_2(x-1)=3$ for $x>1$.

<Steps>
<Step>The domain is $x>1$, so both logarithms exist. Combine them with the product law: $\log_2\big((x+1)(x-1)\big)=3$.</Step>
<Step>Write in index form: $(x+1)(x-1)=2^{3}=8$. So $x^{2}-1=8$ and $x^{2}=9$.</Step>
<Step>Solve: $x=-3$ or $x=3$. Only $x=3$ satisfies $x>1$, so the solution is $x=3$.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why reject x = -3?">For $x=-3$ the factor $x-1$ is negative, so $\log_2(x-1)$ does not exist. Every root must be checked against the domain before it is accepted.</Reveal>

## Visual

On the graph of $y=\log_2 x$, moving from $x$ to $2x$ always raises $y$ by exactly $1$, whatever $x$ is. That is the product law $\log_2(2x)=\log_2 2+\log_2 x$ seen as a picture.

<Callout kind="tip">Read each law from both sides. A logarithm of a product becomes a sum, and a power inside a logarithm becomes a multiplier in front.</Callout>

## How it is examined

Show that questions ask for each law written as a separate line, with the law named. Find and Determine questions give a mark for each law used correctly, a mark for the equation in index form and a mark for the answer. The domain check, such as rejecting a root that makes an argument negative, is a separate reasoning mark, so skipping it loses that mark even when the algebra is right.

<Callout kind="exam">Check the domain before you give the final answer. Every argument of a logarithm must be positive.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Splitting a sum: writing $\log_a(x+y)=\log_a x+\log_a y$. The product law needs a product inside the logarithm.</Callout>

- Confusing $(\log_2 8)^{2}$ with $\log_2(8^{2})$. The first is $9$, the second is $6$.
- Accepting a root that makes an argument zero or negative, such as $x=-3$ in the worked example.
- Turning a quotient of logarithms into a logarithm of a quotient: $\dfrac{\log_a x}{\log_a y}$ is not $\log_a\left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right)$.

## Summary

- $\log_a x=y$ means $a^{y}=x$, for $a>0$, $a \neq 1$ and $x>0$.
- Product law: $\log_a(xy)=\log_a x+\log_a y$.
- Quotient law: $\log_a\left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right)=\log_a x-\log_a y$.
- Power law: $\log_a(x^{m})=m\log_a x$, for any real $m$.
- The laws apply to products, quotients and powers inside a logarithm, never to sums.
- Check the domain: every argument must be positive before you accept a solution.
