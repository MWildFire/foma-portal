## Big idea

A function assigns exactly one output to each input in its domain. An inverse reverses a one-to-one function, with the original range as its domain. Its graph reflects the original graph in $y=x$.

## Explanation

A <Term id="function-single-output"/> gives one output for each allowed input. In $f(x)$, the letter $f$ names the function and $x$ is its argument; $f(x)$ is the corresponding output. Different inputs may have the same output, so not every function has an inverse on its whole domain.

The <Term id="domain-and-range-of-function"/> describe allowed inputs and outputs actually attained. For $f(x)=\sqrt{x-2}$ on its maximal real domain, the domain is $[2,\infty)$ and the range is $[0,\infty)$. Context may restrict these sets further. Elapsed time from a stated start is nonnegative; a signed time coordinate relative to another reference can be negative. Counts of individual objects are integers unless a continuous approximation is explicitly used.

The <Term id="inverse-function-reflection"/> $f^{-1}$ exists as a function from the range of $f$ back to its domain when $f$ is one-to-one: each attained output comes from exactly one input. For example, $x^2$ on all real numbers is not one-to-one, since $2$ and $-2$ both give4. Restricting its domain to $x\ge0$ gives the inverse $\sqrt{x}$.

<Formula id="inverse-composition-identity"/>

For $f(x)=ax+b$ with $a\neq0$, write $y=ax+b$, interchange the variables to obtain $x=ay+b$, then solve for $y=(x-b)/a$.

<Formula id="inverse-linear-function"/>

The function $e^x$ maps all real numbers one-to-one onto the positive numbers. Its inverse is $\ln x$, whose domain is $x>0$ and range is all real numbers.

<Formula id="inverse-exponential-log"/>

<Callout kind="define">If $f(b)=a$, an inverse gives $f^{-1}(a)=b$. The domain of $f^{-1}$ is the range of $f$, and its range is the domain of $f$. These sets are part of the answer.</Callout>

A point $(a,b)$ on the original graph becomes $(b,a)$ on the inverse graph. A point $(c,c)$ stays fixed under this reflection; this does not mean every point on $y=x$ lies on either graph.

<Callout kind="gdc">On a Graphs page, plot $f(x)$, its derived inverse with the appropriate domain, and the line $y=x$ to compare the reflected graphs. To find an inverse value $f^{-1}(a)$, solve $f(x)=a$ over the original domain using Numerical Solve, or intersect $y=f(x)$ with $y=a$. Verify that the solution is unique in that domain; one numerical result alone does not prove uniqueness.</Callout>

## Worked example

A hypothetical cost model for making $n$ bags is $C(n)=4.5n+12$ AED for nonnegative integer $n$. First extend it to a continuous model $\widetilde C(x)=4.5x+12$ for real $x\ge0$, then use it to analyse a budget of 100 AED.

<Steps>
<Step>$C(20)=4.5\times20+12=102$: twenty bags cost 102 AED.</Step>
<Step>For the continuous extension, $y=4.5x+12$ becomes $x=4.5y+12$ after interchanging variables. Thus $\widetilde C^{-1}(x)=(x-12)/4.5$ for $x\ge12$.</Step>
<Step>$\widetilde C^{-1}(100)=88/4.5\approx19.56$. This is a continuous budget threshold, not an attainable count or an inverse value of the original discrete function:100 is not in the discrete range of $C$.</Step>
<Step>Solve $4.5n+12\le100$ with integer $n\ge0$. The largest possible count is19. Check $C(19)=97.5\le100$ and $C(20)=102>100$.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why not round the continuous threshold up?">The budget condition is an inequality, not ordinary nearest rounding. Twenty bags exceed the budget. Checking the neighbouring integer counts establishes that19 is the greatest feasible count.</Reveal>

## Visual

Reflect each graph point in $y=x$ by interchanging its coordinates. The discrete cost graph consists of separate points; its inverse also consists of separate points. The continuous extension is a line restricted to $x\ge0$, and its inverse is a line restricted to inputs at least 12. Keep that distinction when using the graph to read a budget threshold.

<Callout kind="tip">Check both compositions with the correct sets: $f^{-1}(f(x))=x$ for $x$ in the original domain, and $f(f^{-1}(y))=y$ for $y$ in the original range. Checking only a few values is useful for detecting errors but is not a proof for an entire domain.</Callout>

## How it is examined

State the relevant domains and ranges, show the algebra or numerical equation, and interpret the result in context. A restriction must have a specific reason. Exact method and reasoning marks depend on the individual question.

<Callout kind="exam">Check what the variable represents. A discrete count must be an integer; a signed time coordinate can be negative, while elapsed time from the stated start cannot. An inverse input must belong to the original range.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">The inverse $f^{-1}(x)$ is not the reciprocal $1/f(x)$. For $f(x)=2x$, the inverse is $x/2$, whereas the reciprocal is $1/(2x)$ where defined.</Callout>

- Claiming an inverse on a domain where two inputs have the same output.
- Reflecting in an axis instead of $y=x$.
- Omitting the inverse domain or confusing a continuous approximation with a discrete attainable value.

## Summary

- A function assigns one output to each allowed input; one-to-one means different inputs give different outputs.
- An inverse reverses a one-to-one function from its range to its domain.
- Interchanging coordinates reflects a graph in $y=x$.
- Check both compositions and their domains.
- Distinguish an exact discrete inverse from a continuous budget threshold.
