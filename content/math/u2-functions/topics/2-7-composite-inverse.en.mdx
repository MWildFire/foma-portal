## Big idea

A composite function feeds the output of one function into another: $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$. An inverse function undoes a function, and it exists only when each output comes from exactly one input. A restricted domain can make a function one-to-one, so that its inverse exists.

## Explanation

<Term id="composite-function-fg"/> is read from right to left. In $(f \circ g)(x)$ the function $g$ acts first, and $f$ acts on the result. The domain of $f \circ g$ is the set of $x$ in the domain of $g$ for which $g(x)$ lies in the domain of $f$. In general $(f \circ g)(x) \neq (g \circ f)(x)$, so the order matters.

<Formula id="composite-function-definition"/>

An <Term id="inverse-function-one-to-one"/> $f^{-1}$ reverses $f$. It exists only when $f$ is one-to-one, meaning that no two inputs give the same output. The function $x^2$ on all real numbers is not one-to-one, because $2$ and $-2$ both give $4$.

<Callout kind="define">Inverse function: the function $f^{-1}$ with $f^{-1}(f(x)) = x$ for every $x$ in the domain of $f$. It exists only when $f$ is one-to-one.</Callout>

<Formula id="inverse-undo-identities"/>

A <Term id="restricted-domain-inverse"/> makes an inverse possible. On $x \ge 0$ the function $x^2$ is one-to-one, and its inverse is $\sqrt{x}$, the positive root. The domain of the inverse is the range of the original function on the restricted domain.

To find an inverse, write $y = f(x)$, swap $x$ and $y$, and solve for $y$. Then state the domain of $f^{-1}$.

<Formula id="inverse-by-swapping"/>

<Formula id="inverse-domain-range-swap"/>

<Callout kind="gdc">Compare a table of values for $f(x)$ with a graph of $y = f(x)$ and $y = x$ on the same axes. A table for $f^{-1}$ has the $x$- and $y$-values of the table for $f$ swapped, so a value of $f$ can be checked against the inverse directly.</Callout>

## Worked example

<Steps>
<Step>Let $f(x) = 3x - 1$ and $g(x) = x^2 + 2$ for all real $x$. For $x = 2$: $g(2) = 6$ and $f(6) = 17$, so $(f \circ g)(2) = 17$. Also $f(2) = 5$ and $g(5) = 27$, so $(g \circ f)(2) = 27$. The two composites are different.</Step>
<Step>Let $h(x) = x^2 - 4$ for $x \ge 0$. Its outputs satisfy $y \ge -4$, so the domain of $h^{-1}$ is $x \ge -4$. Swapping $x$ and $y$ gives $x = y^2 - 4$, so $y = \sqrt{x + 4}$; the positive root is taken because $y \ge 0$. Hence $h^{-1}(x) = \sqrt{x + 4}$ and $h^{-1}(12) = \sqrt{16} = 4$.</Step>
<Step>$(f \circ h^{-1})(12) = f(4) = 3 \times 4 - 1 = 11$.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why state the domain of the inverse?">The inverse takes the outputs of $h$ as its inputs. A value that $h$ never produces on $x \ge 0$ has no preimage, so $h^{-1}$ is not defined there. The domain is therefore part of the answer, not a detail.</Reveal>

## Visual

<Callout kind="tip">Sketch $y = f(x)$ and the line $y = x$ on the same axes. The graph of $y = f^{-1}(x)$ is the reflection of $y = f(x)$ in that line, because swapping $x$ and $y$ swaps the coordinates of every point. A point $(a, b)$ on $y = f(x)$ gives the point $(b, a)$ on $y = f^{-1}(x)$.</Callout>

## How it is examined

Find and Show that questions give a method mark for the composite or the swap, an accuracy mark for each value or expression, and a reasoning mark for rejecting a root outside the domain. Hence means use the previous part. In a context question the answer must make sense: a time or a number of items cannot be negative.

<Callout kind="exam">Whenever you give an inverse function, state its domain. Marks for the domain are among the most often lost.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Writing $f^{-1}(x)$ as $\dfrac{1}{f(x)}$. The superscript $-1$ means the inverse function, not a reciprocal.</Callout>

- Applying $f$ first in $(f \circ g)(x)$. With $f(x) = 3x - 1$ and $g(x) = x^2 + 2$, $(f \circ g)(2) = 17$, not $27$.
- Giving the inverse of $x^2$ on $x \ge 0$ as $\pm\sqrt{x}$. Only the positive root is valid on that domain.
- Keeping a root outside the restricted domain, such as $x = -2$ when $x \ge 3$.

## Summary

- $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$: the function on the right acts first.
- $(f \circ g)(x)$ and $(g \circ f)(x)$ are usually different.
- $f^{-1}$ exists only for a one-to-one function; restrict the domain when the function is not one-to-one.
- The domain of $f^{-1}$ is the range of $f$ on its restricted domain, and its graph is the reflection of $y = f(x)$ in $y = x$.
- To find an inverse, write $y = f(x)$, swap $x$ and $y$, solve for $y$, and state the domain.
- Reject a root that lies outside the domain, and check that a context answer makes sense.
