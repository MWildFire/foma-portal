## Big idea

Key features are the points and lines that describe the shape of a graph: where it meets the axes, which lines it approaches, and where it reaches its highest or lowest point. They turn a model into answers to practical questions: when the output is zero, how large it can become, and when two models agree.

## Explanation

Find key features algebraically when you can, and with technology when the equation is too hard to solve by hand. Whichever method you use, check that the answer lies in the domain of the model.

A <Term id="key-feature-x-intercept"/> of $y = f(x)$ is a point where the graph meets the $x$-axis, by crossing or tangency, so $y = 0$ and $f(x) = 0$. The $y$-intercept is $f(0)$, the value of the model at $x = 0$, if $0$ is in the domain.

A <Term id="key-feature-asymptote"/> describes limiting behaviour. A vertical asymptote $x=a$ occurs when $f(x)$ tends to $+\infty$ or $-\infty$ as $x$ approaches $a$ from at least one side. For $f(x)=(ax+b)/(cx+d)$, assume $c\neq0$ and $ad-bc\neq0$ so that the numerator does not cancel the denominator’s zero. Then the vertical asymptote is:

$$
x = -\frac{d}{c}
$$

<Formula id="key-feature-vertical-asymptote"/>

A horizontal asymptote $y=L$ means $f(x)\to L$ as $x\to+\infty$ or $x\to-\infty$. A graph can cross it at finite inputs. For the same nonconstant rational function, it is $y=a/c$:

$$
y = \frac{a}{c}
$$

<Formula id="key-feature-horizontal-asymptote"/>

A <Term id="key-feature-turning-point"/> changes from increasing to decreasing (a local maximum) or decreasing to increasing (a local minimum). To find the greatest or least value on a restricted interval, also check included endpoints. For example, $f(x)=x$ on $[0,1]$ has a greatest value 1 at the endpoint, without turning there. A plateau can give many points attaining the same maximum. A numerical local-extremum tool needs a suitable bracket and does not by itself compare all candidates.

<Callout kind="define">A denominator zero is a candidate discontinuity, not automatically a vertical asymptote. For $(x+1)/(x+1)$ with $x\neq-1$, cancellation gives1: there is a hole at−1, not an infinite limit.</Callout>

A <Term id="key-feature-intersection-point"/> lies where two graphs meet. Its $x$-coordinate solves $f(x) = g(x)$, and its $y$-coordinate is the common value of $f$ and $g$ there.

<Formula id="key-feature-intersection-condition"/>

## Worked example

A food truck's monthly profit, in thousands of AED, when it sells $x$ hundred meals is $P(x) = -0.5x^2 + 30x - 200$ for $0 \le x \le 60$.

<Steps>
<Step>Break-even points (x-intercepts): solve $P(x) = 0$. The zero function, or the quadratic formula, gives $x = 7.64$ or $x = 52.36$. Both values lie in the domain, so the truck breaks even when $x$ is 7.64 or 52.36 hundred meals.</Step>
<Step>Maximum: the turning point is $(30, 250)$. The largest monthly profit is AED 250 thousand, reached at $x = 30$, that is 30 hundred meals.</Step>
<Step>Profit of AED 100 thousand: solve $P(x) = 100$. The exact boundary values are $x=30\pm10\sqrt3$, approximately 12.68 and 47.32. For the continuous model, profit is at least 100 thousand AED when $30-10\sqrt3\le x\le30+10\sqrt3$. If individual meals must be counted, solve for integer $100x$:1268 through4732 meals. Do not substitute rounded endpoints for exact continuous inequality bounds.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Can a maximum also be a zero?">Yes: $y=-x^2$ has maximum 0 at $(0,0)$ and touches the x-axis there. In this particular profit example, the maximum is 250, so its vertex is not a zero. A turning point and an intercept are different features that can occur at the same point.</Reveal>

## Visual

Sketch a parabola that opens downwards. It crosses the horizontal axis at $x = 7.64$ and $x = 52.36$, peaks at $(30, 250)$, and crosses the horizontal line $P = 100$ at $x = 12.68$ and $x = 47.32$. Label all five points, because the labelled points are the key features the question asks for.

<Callout kind="tip">Label each key feature with its coordinates and its name (intercept, maximum, intersection). The labels make it easy to check each answer against the sketch.</Callout>

## How it is examined

Questions are worded with Find, Write down or Determine. Show the equation or method, give requested coordinates and interpret their units and domain. The precise mark allocation is specific to the question. Marks are lost when a value outside the domain is accepted, when the $x$- and $y$-coordinates are swapped, or when the answer is given to the wrong accuracy.

<Callout kind="exam">Write each point as an ordered pair $(x, y)$, check the domain given in the question, and round only at the end.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Assuming a turning point must, or cannot, be an x-intercept. For the y-intercept, evaluate $f(0)$ if 0 is in the domain; setting $y=0$ instead searches for x-intercepts.</Callout>

- Writing the vertical asymptote as $y = -d/c$ instead of $x = -d/c$.
- Taking the horizontal asymptote as $y = b/d$; the value is $a/c$, the ratio of the $x$-coefficients.
- Accepting a zero outside the domain, such as a break-even point beyond the number of tickets the business can sell.

## Summary

- An $x$-intercept is a zero, where $y = 0$; the $y$-intercept is $f(0)$.
- For $c\neq0$ and $ad-bc\neq0$, $\dfrac{ax+b}{cx+d}$ has vertical asymptote $x=-d/c$ and horizontal asymptote $y=a/c$.
- Turning points can be local extrema; compare all relevant candidates and included endpoints for an interval maximum or minimum.
- Points of intersection solve $f(x) = g(x)$; give both coordinates.
- Keep only the key features that lie in the domain of the model.
- Round as the question asks, and interpret each feature in the context.
