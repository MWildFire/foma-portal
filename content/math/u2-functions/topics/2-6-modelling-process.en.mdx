## Big idea

Modelling is a cycle rather than a single calculation. A model is a deliberate simplification: you choose a shape that suits the context, fit its parameters to data, use it to predict or decide, and then judge whether it is appropriate and reasonable. A model that fits the data well can still be the wrong tool for the question you are asking.

## Explanation

A <Term id="mathematical-model"/> is a simplified description of a real situation with variables, parameters and a rule. Choose its shape from the context and a scatter plot: a straight line for a steady change, a parabola for one peak or dip, an exponential curve for a constant percentage change, and a wave for a repeating cycle. Check that the values the model gives make sense for the variables; a count of cars, for example, cannot be negative.

A <Term id="least-squares-fit"/> chooses the parameters that make the sum of the squared residuals as small as possible: <Formula id="model-least-squares-sum"/> Technology does this for linear, quadratic and exponential regression. For an exponential model it applies least squares to the logarithms of the values.

A <Term id="model-residual"/> is the observed value minus the predicted value. <Formula id="model-residual-formula"/> Residuals should look random around zero. A curved or growing pattern in the residuals means the shape of the model is wrong, even when the coefficients look sensible.

<Callout kind="define">Residual: the observed value minus the value the model predicts. It is positive when the model underestimates the observation.</Callout>

A prediction inside the range of the data is called interpolation, and it is usually more reliable than <Term id="model-extrapolation"/>, which predicts outside that range.

Judge the model by asking whether its predictions are reasonable in the context, whether the residuals show a systematic pattern, and what the model leaves out, such as other seasons, policy changes or sudden events.

To check the fit, open Data & Statistics, put both columns on the axes with `Add Variable`, then use `Menu → Analyze → Regression` for the curve and `Menu → Analyze → Residuals` for the residuals. ## Worked example

The monthly average highs in Dubai from March to October are illustrative, rounded values, not official statistics. Let $t$ be the month number, with January as $t = 1$, so March is $t = 3$ and October is $t = 10$. The temperatures in °C, from March to October, are 28.7, 33.4, 38.1, 40.8, 41.9, 42.1, 39.8 and 35.9.

<Steps>
<Step>Choose and fit: the eight points rise to one peak in the summer and fall again, so a parabola is the natural shape. A quadratic regression gives $a = -0.766$, $b = 11.1$ and $c = 1.85$, so $T(t) = -0.766t^2 + 11.1t + 1.85$.</Step>
<Step>Use the model: the peak is at $t = 7.24$, around late July to early August, with a predicted high of $42.03$ °C. For June ($t = 6$) the model gives $40.85$ °C, close to the observed $40.8$ °C.</Step>
<Step>Judge: every residual lies between $-0.6$ and $0.6$ °C, so the fit is good from March to October. But for January ($t = 1$) the model gives $12.18$ °C, far below any January high in Dubai. The model should not be used outside March to October.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Would a different model describe the whole year?">A parabola is symmetrical, so it cannot follow the cooler months. A year-long cycle is better described by the sinusoidal model from the standard models topic, and data from several years would test any model further.</Reveal>

## Visual

Plot the eight points. They form a single hump: they rise from March to a peak around July and August, then fall again in September and October. A straight line would miss the hump, while a parabola follows it closely. Extend the parabola to January and it drops far too low, which is the visual version of the judgement step.

<Callout kind="tip">Plot the data before you fit anything. If the points do not look like the curve you fitted, a good-looking coefficient is not evidence that the model is right.</Callout>

## How it is examined

Modelling questions give a mark for choosing a model with a reason (R1), for the parameters to the accuracy stated (A1), for the prediction (A1), and for a judgement that refers to the context and the limitations (R1). A judgement needs a specific reason: "the model predicts a negative count" earns the mark, while "the model is not very good" does not.

<Callout kind="exam">Say which model you chose and why, give the parameters to the accuracy asked, then judge the result in the context of the problem.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Choosing a model because its curve passes near the points, rather than because its shape matches the context.</Callout>

- Writing the residual as predicted minus observed. It is observed minus predicted.
- Trusting a prediction far outside the data range, which is extrapolation.
- Rounding the parameters too early, so that later predictions drift away from the correct value.

## Summary

- A model is chosen by its shape and by the context, not only by how well it fits.
- Fit the parameters by least squares, with technology, and state them to the accuracy asked.
- A residual is observed minus predicted; residuals should scatter randomly around zero.
- Predictions inside the data range (interpolation) are usually more reliable than extrapolation.
- Judge a model by reasonableness in the context and by its limitations.
- Keep full accuracy in your working and round only the final answer.
