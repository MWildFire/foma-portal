## Big idea

Logarithms squeeze very large and very small numbers onto one scale, and they turn power and exponential relationships into straight lines. Once the data are straight, a best-fit line gives the parameters of the model, and the shape of the points shows which relationship the data follow.

## Explanation

A logarithm turns a huge range into a manageable one. $\log_{10}(4.8\times10^{7}) = 7.68$ shows at once that the number has eight digits, so it lies between $10^{7}$ and $10^{8}$. This is how the <Term id="order-of-magnitude"/> of a quantity is found.

Logarithms also make <Term id="linearisation"/> possible. Taking logarithms of both sides of a model turns products into sums and powers into multiples, using the laws in <Formula id="log-laws-linearisation"/>. Two standard models then become straight lines.

- Power relationship $y = ax^{b}$. Taking $\log$ gives $\log y = b\log x + \log a$. Plot $\log y$ against $\log x$ on a <Term id="log-log-graph"/>: the line has gradient $b$ and vertical intercept $\log a$. <Formula id="log-power-linear"/>
- Exponential relationship $y = ab^{x}$. Taking $\log$ gives $\log y = x\log b + \log a$. Plot $\log y$ against $x$ on a <Term id="semi-log-graph"/>: the line has gradient $\log b$ and vertical intercept $\log a$. <Formula id="log-exponential-linear"/>

<Callout kind="define">Linearisation: transforming data, usually by taking logarithms, so that a non-linear relationship appears as a straight line.</Callout>

To decide between the two models, compare how straight each transformed graph is. A cloud of points that lies closer to a line, a value of $|r|$ nearer to 1 and no clear curve in the residuals all point to the better model.

<Callout kind="gdc">In Lists & Spreadsheet, put the data in named columns and fill the logarithm columns you need in the formula cell (the second cell from the top), using one base throughout ($\log$ is base 10, $\ln$ is base $e$). Then use `Menu → Statistics → Stat Calculations → Linear Regression (mx+b)` with the two columns that make the straight line as `X List` and `Y List`; the output shows the gradient as $m$ and the intercept as $b$, and also gives $r$. `Power Regression` and `Exponential Regression` report $r$ for the log-transformed data, so you can compare it with your line.</Callout>

To return to the original model, use the intercept $c$ of the line: with base 10, $a = 10^{c}$; with natural logarithms, $a = e^{c}$.

## Worked example

<Steps>
<Step>Scaling: $\log_{10}(4.8\times10^{7}) = 7.68$, so the number has eight digits.</Step>
<Step>Exponential model $y = 20 \times 1.4^{t}$: taking natural logarithms gives $\ln y = \ln 20 + t\ln 1.4$. The gradient is $\ln 1.4 = 0.336$ and the intercept is $\ln 20 = 2.996$.</Step>
<Step>The gradient gives $\ln b = 0.336$, so the growth factor is $b = e^{0.336} = 1.40$.</Step>
</Steps>

<Steps>
<Step>Power model $y = 3x^{2}$: $\log y = 2\log x + \log 3$. The log-log line has gradient $2$ and intercept $\log 3 = 0.477$.</Step>
</Steps>

<Reveal title="Why not read the intercept as a?">The intercept of a log-log line is $\log a$, not $a$. To recover $a$, undo the logarithm, for example $a = 10^{\log a}$.</Reveal>

## Visual

<Sim id="math.logistic-loglog-lab"/>

The left panel shows the logistic curve $y=L/(1+Ce^{-kx})$ on ordinary $x$ and $y$ axes. The right panel is the **logit view**: its horizontal coordinate is $x$ and its vertical coordinate is $\ln(y/(L-y))$, not $\ln y$. Both panels use evenly spaced coordinates; the right panel plots transformed values rather than a logarithmic $y$ axis.

For $0<y<L$, rearranging the logistic model gives $y/(L-y)=e^{kx}/C$, hence $\ln(y/(L-y))=kx-\ln C$. A suitable $L$ makes the transformed data approximately linear: the fitted gradient estimates $k$ and the intercept estimates $-\ln C$. Move $L$ to see how the fit changes. The $C$ and $k$ sliders change the blue model on the left; the fitted line on the right comes from the fixed orange data and the chosen $L$.

Oxford AI HL, ch. 7.4, pp. 325–326 explains semi-log and log-log plots; ch. 7.5, p. 332 gives the logistic model. The logit transformation above is derived from that model. A semi-log plot for exponential data uses $x$ and $\ln y$; a log-log plot for power data uses $\ln x$ and $\ln y$.

## How it is examined

Command terms include Calculate, Find, Determine, Show that and Comment. Marks are given for taking logarithms correctly (M1), for reading the gradient and the intercept from the line (M1), for returning to $a$ and $b$ to the required accuracy (A1) and for a justified choice of model (R1).

<Callout kind="exam">State both plotted coordinates: log-log, semi-log and logit plots use different transformations. Explain why your chosen model gives a straight line. A value of $r$ without that reasoning loses the reasoning mark.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="mistake">Reading the intercept of a log-log line as $a$ instead of $\log a$, so the coefficient comes out wrong.</Callout>

- Taking the logarithm of only one side of the model.
- Mixing up the graphs: $\log y$ against $x$ is for exponential data, while $\log y$ against $\log x$ is for power data.
- Using the wrong base: $a = 10^{c}$ for base-10 logarithms, but $a = e^{c}$ for natural logarithms.

## Summary

- Logarithms squeeze very large and very small numbers onto one scale and give their order of magnitude.
- $y = ax^{b}$ is a straight line on a log-log graph, with gradient $b$ and intercept $\log a$.
- $y = ab^{x}$ is a straight line on a semi-log graph, with gradient $\log b$ and intercept $\log a$.
- Fit a best-fit line to the transformed data, then use the intercept to find $a$.
- Choose the model by how straight each graph is, using $r$ and the shape of the points.
- Always state the base of the logarithm you used.
